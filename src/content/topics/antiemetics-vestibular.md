#### Anticholinergics (vestibular suppressants)
        **Mechanism:** Block muscarinic acetylcholine receptors in the vestibular nucleus and chemoreceptor trigger zone. Suppress vestibular signals and reduce nausea from both central and peripheral sources.

        **Key drugs:** Hyoscine (scopolamine), atropine, dicyclomine.

        **Vestibular effect:** Strong. Particularly useful in vertigo with nausea.

          **Hyoscine (0.3-0.6 mg IM/IV, or transdermal patch 1 mg over 72h):** Gold standard for acute vestibular vertigo, motion sickness, and nausea in the emergency/acute setting. Onset 30-60 minutes. Transdermal useful for prolonged prophylaxis.
          **Side effects:** Dry mouth, urinary retention, tachycardia, drowsiness, mydriasis. Caution in glaucoma, urinary retention, tachyarrhythmias.

        #### H1 receptor antagonists (first-generation antihistamines)
        **Mechanism:** Cross blood-brain barrier (lipophilic); block H1 receptors in vomiting centre and vestibular nuclei. Also possess anticholinergic properties.

        **Key drugs:** Cyclizine, meclozine, promethazine, diphenhydramine.

        **Vestibular effect:** Moderate to strong. Useful for vertigo-related nausea; sedation is common.

          **Cyclizine (50 mg PO/IM TDS):** Most commonly used antihistamine antiemetic in NZ. Onset 30 minutes PO, 15 minutes IM. Sedating.
          **Meclozine (25-50 mg PO daily):** Longer half-life; less sedating than cyclizine; used as prophylaxis for motion-related vertigo.
          **Side effects:** Drowsiness (severe with cyclizine), anticholinergic effects, blurred vision. Avoid in angle-closure glaucoma.

        #### H2 receptor antagonists (second-generation antihistamines)
        **Mechanism:** Do not cross blood-brain barrier (hydrophilic); block H1 receptors in the gut and chemoreceptor trigger zone only.

        **Key drugs:** Cetirizine, loratadine, fexofenadine.

        **Vestibular effect:** None. Useful for nausea in gastroenteritis or food-related triggers, but ineffective for vertigo-related nausea.

          - Non-sedating, safer profile; good for prolonged use in post-surgical nausea or chronic conditions.

        #### 5-HT3 receptor antagonists (setrons)
        **Mechanism:** Block serotonin receptors in the chemoreceptor trigger zone and gut. No direct vestibular effect.

        **Key drugs:** Ondansetron, granisetron, tropisetron.

        **Vestibular effect:** None. Effective for post-operative nausea and chemotherapy-induced nausea; not useful for vertigo-related nausea.

          **Ondansetron (4-8 mg PO/IV TDS):** Well-tolerated, no sedation. Caution: constipation, headache, can prolong QT interval at high doses.

        #### Dopamine antagonists (metoclopramide, prochlorperazine)
        **Mechanism:** Block D2 dopamine receptors in the chemoreceptor trigger zone; also weak anticholinergic activity.

        **Key drugs:** Metoclopramide, prochlorperazine, domperidone.

        **Vestibular effect:** Metoclopramide and prochlorperazine have some vestibular activity via anticholinergic properties; domperidone does not cross BBB, so no central effect.

          **Metoclopramide (10 mg PO/IM TDS):** Useful for post-operative and chemotherapy nausea. Some effect on vestibular nausea via anticholinergic component. Risk of tardive dyskinesia with prolonged use.
          **Prochlorperazine (5-10 mg IM/PO TDS):** Stronger anticholinergic effect; more useful for vertigo-related nausea than metoclopramide. Extrapyramidal side effects; akathisia common.
          **Domperidone:** Does not cross BBB; useful for gastro-motility but no vestibular effect.

        #### NK1 receptor antagonists (aprepitant)
        **Mechanism:** Block substance P receptors in the chemoreceptor trigger zone and vomiting centre.

        **Vestibular effect:** None. Reserved for chemotherapy-induced nausea; expensive and not used in primary vestibular vertigo.

        #### BPPV (benign paroxysmal positional vertigo)
        Short episodes of intense nausea from rapid head movement. Treatment is **canalith repositioning (Epley manoeuvre), not drugs**. Antiemetics are supportive only. Hyoscine or cyclizine may be used acutely if nausea is severe, but do not accelerate resolution.

        #### Vestibular neuritis
        Acute peripheral vestibular inflammation. Severe vertigo with significant nausea. **Hyoscine (IM or transdermal patch)** is most effective for symptom control. Prochlorperazine is alternative if hyoscine contraindicated. Corticosteroids (if given early) may reduce recovery time, but antiemetics do not affect this.

        #### Meniere's disease
        Recurrent episodes with variable timing. **Hyoscine or cyclizine** for acute attacks. Preventive therapy targets underlying fluid regulation (thiazide diuretics, betahistine) rather than symptoms.

        #### Vestibular migraine
        Nausea and vertigo as part of migraine syndrome. Preventive treatment is migraine prophylaxis (propranolol, amitriptyline, topiramate). During acute episodes, **cyclizine or hyoscine** for symptom control; migraine-specific agents (triptans) if migrainous headache present.

        #### Chronic vertigo / central causes
        Prolonged use of anticholinergics (hyoscine especially) risks tolerance and tachyphylaxis. **Cyclizine or meclozine** are preferred for longer-term use. Ensure underlying cause is identified and managed.

      ⚠Practical prescribing notes▾
      
        #### Acute vertigo (emergency/acute setting)
        
          **First-line:** Hyoscine 0.3-0.6 mg IM/IV, or transdermal patch 1 mg (apply to hairless skin behind ear; change every 72 hours).
          **Alternative:** Cyclizine 50 mg IM (faster than PO); prochlorperazine 5-10 mg IM if hyoscine contraindicated.
          **Caution:** All cause drowsiness. Warn patients not to drive.

        #### Chronic vertigo (primary care)
        
          **First-line:** Cyclizine 50 mg PO BD or TDS; or meclozine 25-50 mg PO daily (less sedating).
          **Duration:** Vestibular suppressants are symptom control, not curative. Taper gradually if no longer needed; abrupt cessation can paradoxically worsen symptoms.
          **Monitoring:** Anticholinergic effects (dry mouth, urinary retention, tachycardia) increase with age and polypharmacy. Avoid in narrow-angle glaucoma, urinary retention, heart failure.

        #### Nausea without vertigo
        Gastroenteritis, post-operative, chemotherapy: ondansetron is often first-line in secondary care. Metoclopramide or prochlorperazine if vestibular component is suspected. H2 antihistamines ineffective.

        #### Motion sickness prophylaxis
        
          **Hyoscine patch:** Apply 6-12 hours before travel; highly effective. Change every 72 hours if prolonged travel.
          **Meclozine:** 25-50 mg 30 minutes before travel; less effective but less sedating.
          **Ginger or peppermint:** Modest evidence; safe and worth trying if patient prefers non-pharmacological approach.

        #### Interactions and cautions
        
          **Anticholinergics + benzodiazepines:** Increased CNS depression and anticholinergic effects.
          **Prochlorperazine:** Extrapyramidal side effects (dystonia, akathisia, parkinsonism) especially in young patients. Use lowest effective dose; short courses only.
          **Metoclopramide:** Tardive dyskinesia risk increases after 3 months continuous use. Reserve for short-term post-operative or acute situations.
          **Elderly patients:** Anticholinergics increase fall risk, delirium, and urinary retention. Use with caution; lower doses.
          **Pregnancy:** Cyclizine and hyoscine are considered safe in pregnancy (limited data). Ondansetron has reassuring safety profile. Prochlorperazine avoid in first trimester.
