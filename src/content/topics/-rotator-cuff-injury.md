## Overview


Patient Handout
      Rotator Cuff Injury
      Understanding your shoulder pain, recovery options and what you can do to help yourself heal.

    ## What is the rotator cuff?
    The rotator cuff is a group of four muscles and tendons that hold your shoulder joint together and allow you to move your arm in all directions. These muscles are called the supraspinatus, infraspinatus, teres minor and subscapularis. When these tendons become inflamed, torn or injured, you have rotator cuff-related shoulder pain.

    The rotator cuff is one of the most used structures in your body-it works every time you raise your arm, throw, carry, or lift something.

        **The Four Rotator Cuff Muscles:**

            **Supraspinatus** - Lifts arm up; most commonly torn

            **Infraspinatus** - Rotates arm outward

            **Teres Minor** - Assists rotation, stabilizes joint

            **Subscapularis** - Rotates arm inward; hidden underneath

    ## Why is this happening?
    Rotator cuff injury can develop gradually over time or suddenly after a specific injury. Common causes include:

      **Overuse:** Repetitive overhead activities (throwing, reaching, painting, swimming)
      **Age:** Tendon quality naturally decreases with age; tears become more common after age 40
      **Weak shoulder muscles:** Muscles that stabilise your shoulder may not be strong enough for your activities
      **Poor posture:** Slouching or hunching reduces the space available for the rotator cuff tendons
      **Direct injury:** A fall or trauma to the shoulder
      **Neck or spine problems:** Nerve irritation from the neck can cause shoulder pain
      **Inflammatory conditions:** Conditions like rheumatoid arthritis or diabetes increase risk
    
    Often, there is no single "cause"-it's a combination of factors. Most rotator cuff injuries are **not** full tears; they are inflammation and partial damage that can heal with appropriate care.

    ## What does rotator cuff injury feel like?
    
      - Aching pain in the shoulder, often worse at night or when lying on the affected side
      - Weakness when lifting your arm or carrying objects
      - Loss of range of motion-difficulty reaching behind your back or overhead
      - Pain with specific movements (e.g., reaching across your body or lifting your arm out to the side)
      - A clicking or popping sensation in the shoulder
      - Pain that may radiate down your arm
    
    Symptoms can be mild or severe, and they may come and go. Some people have pain but good strength; others have significant weakness.

      **Seek urgent care if:** You have severe pain after a fall or injury, sudden loss of strength, or numbness/tingling in your arm. These may indicate a full tear or nerve damage.

    ## How is rotator cuff injury diagnosed?
    Your GP will:

      - Ask about your symptoms, how they started, and what activities make them worse or better
      - Perform simple physical tests to assess pain, strength and range of motion
      - Check your neck, as neck problems can also cause shoulder pain
    
    Imaging (X-rays, ultrasound or MRI) is sometimes used but **not always needed**. Many people with rotator cuff tears on imaging have no symptoms, and many people with shoulder pain have normal imaging. Your symptoms matter more than a scan. Imaging is most useful if you have severe pain, significant weakness, or if you're not improving with conservative treatment.

    ## What can you do to help yourself recover?
    Most rotator cuff injuries improve with time and the right exercises. Recovery usually takes 6-12 weeks, though some cases take longer.

    ### 1. Rest and activity modification
    
      - Avoid activities that cause pain in the short term (e.g., overhead reaching, throwing, heavy lifting)
      - Rest does not mean complete immobility-gentle movement is important
      - Use ice for the first 48 hours if you have acute pain; heat can help after that
      - Sleep on your back or on the unaffected side; avoid sleeping on the injured shoulder

    ### 2. Physiotherapy and exercises
    This is the cornerstone of treatment. A physiotherapist will teach you exercises to:

      - Restore range of motion (pendulum swings, gentle stretching)
      - Strengthen the rotator cuff muscles and the larger shoulder stabilizers
      - Improve posture and shoulder blade position
    
    Exercises progress from gentle stretching to resistance work over 6-8 weeks. You should do these exercises regularly-ideally daily or at least 3-4 times per week. This is not optional; physiotherapy is the primary treatment for rotator cuff injury and has strong evidence for effectiveness.1

    ### Progressive Rehabilitation Guide
    Recovery typically follows three phases. Progress only when pain-free; if an exercise causes pain, skip it and try again in a few days.

      **✓ Pain-free test:** Does the exercise cause sharp stabbing pain? If yes, skip it. Mild ache or muscle tiredness is OK. When in doubt, do less-you can always progress next week.

    #### 🟢 Phase 1: Pain Relief & Gentle Mobility (Weeks 1-2)
    Goals: Reduce pain, restore basic range of motion, reduce inflammation.

    Your arm will feel stiff and weak. This is normal. Gentle movement helps; complete rest makes it worse.

        **Pendulum swings:** 
        
          - Bend forward at the hip, rest your good hand on a table
          - Let your injured arm hang. Gently swing it in small circles (10–20 seconds each direction)
          **Frequency:** 2–3 times daily
          Why: Gravity helps gently mobilize the joint

        **Passive shoulder lift:** 
        
          - Lie on your back on a bed
          - Use your good arm to gently lift the injured arm up and overhead
          - Do not force it; lift only as high as is comfortable
          **Frequency:** 10 reps × 2 times daily
          Why: Your good arm does the work, restoring overhead range

        **Scapular squeezes (shoulder blade retractions):** 
        
          - Sit upright, good posture
          - Pull your shoulder blades back and together, as if squeezing a pencil between them
          - Hold 3 seconds, then relax
          **Frequency:** 10 reps × 2 times daily
          Why: Activates the stabilizer muscles

        **Wrist and elbow circles:** 
        
          - Keep your shoulder completely still
          - Gently circle your wrist and elbow (forearm and hand move, shoulder doesn't)
          **Frequency:** 10 circles each direction, 1–2 times daily
          Why: Keeps distal joints mobile without loading the shoulder

    **🧊 Ice:** Apply for 15 minutes, 3–4 times daily if swollen or after exercises. **💤 Sleep position:** Back or unaffected side; avoid sleeping on the injured shoulder.

    #### 🟡 Phase 2: Restore Strength & Control (Weeks 3-6)
    Goals: Build muscle strength, improve stability, increase functional range of motion.

    By now, swelling should be less. Pain should be mild or only with certain movements. If pain is still severe, stay in Phase 1 longer.

        **Isometric holds (muscle tensioning without movement):** 
        
          *Elbow press (against body):* Keep arm at your side, elbow bent 90°. Press elbow gently against your ribs without moving. Hold 5 seconds, rest 2 seconds. Do 10 reps, 2× daily.
          *External rotation hold:* Hold a rolled towel at your elbow (keep elbow at side, bent 90°). Gently press the towel outward without moving. Hold 5 seconds, rest 2 seconds. Do 10 reps, 2× daily.
          Why: Builds strength without stressing healing tissues

        **Resistance band exercises (light resistance band):** 
        
          *External rotation:* Stand or sit. Keep elbow at side (bent 90°), forearm across your body. Hold band with both hands. Pull outward against resistance, then slowly return. Do 12–15 reps, 2× daily.
          *Internal rotation:* Keep elbow at side (bent 90°). Hold band with both hands in front of body. Pull inward against resistance. Do 12–15 reps, 2× daily.
          *Lateral raise:* Stand on band with feet. Grasp band in both hands (light tension). Slowly raise arms out to sides to shoulder height. Hold 1 second, lower. Do 10–12 reps, 2× daily.
          Why: Progressive strengthening as healing progresses. Use light resistance; you should feel muscle work, not pain.

        **Prone I-Y-T rows (lie face-down, lift arms):** 
        
          - Lie face-down on a bed with your injured arm hanging off the edge
          *I position:* Lift arm straight overhead. Hold 2 seconds.
          *Y position:* Lift arm at 45° angle (between overhead and out to side). Hold 2 seconds.
          *T position:* Lift arm straight out to the side (perpendicular to body). Hold 2 seconds.
          - Do 8–10 reps of each position, daily.
          Why: Strengthens the entire rotator cuff and shoulder stabilizers in functional patterns

        **Wall push-ups (scapular stability):** 
        
          - Stand facing a wall, hands flat at shoulder height, shoulder-width apart
          - Gently lean forward, elbows bending slightly
          - Push back to starting position
          - Do 10–15 reps, 2× daily
          Why: Activates shoulder blade muscles (serratus anterior) that help stabilize the shoulder

    **Frequency:** Do exercises 3–4 times per week. Take 1–2 rest days per week to allow muscle recovery. Rest does NOT mean sitting still-it means avoiding these specific exercises.

    #### 🔴 Phase 3: Power & Return to Activity (Weeks 7-12)
    Goals: Build endurance and full strength, regain confidence in movement, return to sport or work.

    Pain should be mostly gone by now. You should have good strength and range of motion. Focus on gradually increasing demands.

        **Progressive resistance training:** 
        
          - Replace resistance band with light dumbbells (0.5–2 kg) or heavier band
          - Perform same exercises as Phase 2 (external rotation, internal rotation, lateral raises, rows)
          - Gradually increase weight by 0.5–1 kg every 2 weeks
          - Do 12–15 reps per set, 3 sets, 3–4 times per week
          Why: Builds true strength and muscle endurance

        **Functional movement training:** 
        
          *Carrying:* Gradually carry heavier objects (shopping bags, books) in the injured arm. Start light and increase each week.
          *Overhead reaching:* Begin with low shelves, gradually progress to higher shelves. Practice reaching behind your back.
          *Pushing/pulling:* Open doors, push shopping carts, pull drawers. Start gently and increase force gradually.
          Why: Real-world movements that prepare you for daily life

        **Sport-specific training (if applicable):** 
        
          *Throwing:* Start with light ball, short distances (2–3 m), slow throws. Gradually increase distance and speed weekly.
          *Tennis/racquet sports:* Start with forehand drives. Progress to serve gradually. Use a lighter racquet initially.
          *Swimming:* Start with gentle breaststroke. Progress to freestyle or backstroke as strength increases. Avoid butterfly.
          Why: Safely returns you to activities you enjoy while building sport-specific strength

        **Maintenance (after Week 12):** 
        
          - Continue rotator cuff exercises 2–3 times per week indefinitely
          - These prevent re-injury and maintain strength
          - No more than 1–2 rest days per week; consistency is key

    **Key rule:** If an exercise causes sharp pain, reduce intensity or frequency or drop it. Mild muscle tiredness or mild ache is normal and expected-sharp, shooting, or catching pain is not.

      **Red flags during exercises:** Stop if you experience sharp stabbing pain, swelling that doesn't improve, significant loss of strength, or numbness/tingling. Contact your GP or physiotherapist.

      **What helps recovery most:** Consistent, progressive exercise supervised by a physiotherapist. Starting early gives the best results. The key is patience-don't expect overnight improvement, but gradual, steady progress.

    ### 3. Pain management
    
      **Over-the-counter pain relief:** Paracetamol or ibuprofen can help manage pain, especially in the first few weeks. Take as needed; avoid regular long-term use without medical advice.
      **Ice or heat:** Ice in the first 48 hours, then heat (warm compress, heat pack) if it feels better
      **Injections:** Your GP may offer a corticosteroid injection into the shoulder. This can reduce inflammation and pain, allowing you to do exercises more comfortably. Effects typically last 4-12 weeks.

    ### 4. Posture and ergonomics
    
      - Sit and stand upright; avoid slouching
      - Keep your shoulders relaxed and slightly back
      - At your desk: keyboard at elbow height, monitor at eye level, chair supporting your back
      - When carrying bags or loads, distribute weight evenly; avoid constant heavy lifting on one side

    ### 5. Gradual return to activity
    As pain improves and strength returns (usually after 4-6 weeks), gradually resume activities. Progress slowly: don't jump back into full activity or sports. If something hurts, scale back and progress more gradually.

    ## When should I see my GP again?
    
      - If pain is not improving after 4-6 weeks of physiotherapy
      - If strength is getting worse or you're losing function
      - If you have severe pain despite rest and pain relief
      - If you develop numbness or tingling in your arm
      - If you suspect you have re-injured the shoulder

    ## What if conservative treatment doesn't work?
    Most rotator cuff injuries (roughly 80-90%) improve with physiotherapy and time. If you're not improving after 12 weeks of proper physiotherapy, your GP may refer you to a specialist (orthopaedic surgeon or sports medicine doctor). At that point, imaging and other treatments may be considered, including:

      - Further physiotherapy or specialised rehabilitation programs
      - Advanced imaging to clarify the diagnosis
      - Surgical repair (in rare cases of large tears or severe functional loss)

    ## Key things to remember
    
      - Most rotator cuff injuries improve without surgery
      - Physiotherapy is the most important treatment-exercises are essential
      - Recovery takes time; expect 6-12 weeks for significant improvement
      - Consistency matters more than intensity; regular gentle exercise beats sporadic hard efforts
      - Patience and gradual progression are key to avoiding re-injury
      - Pain is not permanent; most people return to normal activities
