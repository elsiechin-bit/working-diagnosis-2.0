## Overview


Cellulitis is a clinical diagnosis: spreading, **unilateral**, warm, tender erythema with poorly demarcated borders, often with a portal of entry (tinea pedis, ulcer, cracked skin) and sometimes fever. There is no confirmatory test in primary care - which is why the mimics matter.

  MimicClue
   Venous stasis dermatitis**Bilateral**, chronic, itchy not tender, no fever - the commonest misdiagnosis
   DVTCalf swelling/pain, risk factors - consider and exclude
   Acute goutJoint-centred, exquisite, first MTP
   Contact dermatitisItch > pain, demarcated to contact area
   Necrotising fasciitisPain out of proportion, systemic toxicity, rapid progression - emergency
  
  Bilateral lower-leg "cellulitis" is rarely cellulitisTreat it as venous eczema (emollients, topical steroid, compression once arterial supply confirmed) rather than antibiotics.

      💊Antibiotics & route▾
      Cover strep and staph. **Flucloxacillin** is first-line; erythromycin/clarithromycin (or cefalexin) in penicillin allergy. Most cellulitis is treated orally as an outpatient.

   **Oral first-line:** flucloxacillin (e.g. 500 mg QID), 5-7 days, extend if slow to settle.
   **Mark the margin** with a skin pen and date it - the objective way to judge response at review.
   **Elevate** the limb and treat the portal of entry (e.g. topical antifungal for tinea pedis).
   **IV / admission** for systemic sepsis, rapid spread, immunocompromise, facial/periorbital involvement, or failure of oral therapy.
  
  #### Specific situations
  
   **Periorbital vs orbital:** painful eye movement, proptosis or diplopia signals orbital cellulitis - emergency referral.
   **Abscess:** needs incision and drainage; antibiotics alone are insufficient.
   **Animal/human bites:** co-amoxiclav for polymicrobial cover.
   **MRSA risk** (recurrent, known colonisation): adjust per local sensitivities.

      🔁Recurrence & prevention▾
      
   **Treat predisposing factors:** tinea pedis, lymphoedema, chronic oedema, leg ulcers, eczema.
   **Skin care and compression** reduce recurrence in chronic oedema.
   **Antibiotic prophylaxis** (e.g. low-dose penicillin) can be considered for frequent recurrences.

   ✦**Unilateral** - if it’s both legs, rethink the diagnosis.
   ✦**Mark and date the margin** so review is objective.
   ✦**Pain out of proportion** = think necrotising fasciitis.
   ✦**Find the front door:** treat tinea pedis to stop recurrence.
