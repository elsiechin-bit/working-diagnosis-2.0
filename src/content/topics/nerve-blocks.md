#### When to use nerve blocks in primary care
        
          - Minor surgical procedures: removal of skin lesions, cysts, minor lacerations
          - Acute pain management: fracture reduction, dislocation reduction, acute injuries
          - Diagnostic procedures: joint aspiration, wound exploration
          - When infiltration anaesthesia alone is insufficient or contraindicated

        #### Advantages over infiltration anaesthesia
        
          - Reduced volume of local anaesthetic required
          - Better anaesthesia distal to injection site (less distortion of tissue)
          - Faster onset of deep analgesia
          - Longer duration of action
          - Minimal tissue swelling (better visualization for procedures)

        #### Absolute contraindications
        
          - Allergy to local anaesthetic (true IgE-mediated reaction, not preservative allergy)
          - Infection at injection site or overlying skin
          - Patient refusal or inability to consent
          Anticoagulation (relative if INR 

        #### Toxicity risk
        
          **Local anaesthetic toxicity:** CNS symptoms (tinnitus, circumoral numbness, seizure) or cardiac (dysrhythmia, hypotension) if overdose or intravascular injection
          **Prevention:** Calculate max dose for patient weight; aspirate before injecting; inject slowly; have resuscitation equipment available
          **Treatment:** Stop injection, call for help, manage seizure (benzodiazepine), consider lipid emulsion (20% Intralipid) if cardiac instability

        #### Consent and setup
        
          Explain: needle insertion, numbness expected, temporary nature of anaesthesia, risks (nerve damage rare, infection 
          - Have emergency kit available: adrenaline, oxygen, suction, resuscitation equipment
          - Position patient comfortably; ensure adequate lighting and access to anatomical landmarks
          - Use aseptic technique: hand hygiene, sterile gloves, sterile field

      🖐️Digital nerve block (finger/thumb)▾
      
        Most commonly used nerve block in primary care. Simple anatomy, high success rate, rapid onset.

          Digital nerve anatomy
          **Four digital nerves per finger:** Two from ulnar/radial side; run along sides of digit in neurovascular bundles
          **Location of block:** Inject at base of digit (web space level) on both sides to ensure complete anaesthesia
          **Alternative:** Distal interphalangeal (DIP) approach — inject at level of DIP joint; technically easier for some operators

          Technique: Digital nerve block at base of digit
          **Position:** Patient palm up, digit extended on firm surface
          **Landmarks:** Palpate web space at base of digit; identify lateral (radial) and medial (ulnar) gutters
          
          **Procedure:**
          
            1
            Clean skin with chlorhexidine or iodine

            2
            Infiltrate skin and subcutaneous tissue at lateral (radial) gutter of digit base with 1% lignocaine on 25G needle

            3
            Advance needle until it reaches bone (metacarpal head or phalanx base); withdraw slightly

            4
            Inject 2-3 mL of 1% lignocaine slowly around lateral nerve

            5
            Withdraw needle; repeat on medial (ulnar) side of digit with same technique

            6
            Wait 5-10 minutes for full onset of anaesthesia

          **Local anaesthetic volume:** 2-3 mL per side (4-6 mL total); lignocaine 1% recommended
          **Success rate:** 95%+ with proper technique
          **Expected duration:** 1-2 hours (up to 4-6 hours with bupivacaine)

          Alternative: DIP-level digital block
          **Landmark:** DIP joint crease level (easier palpation; nerves lie more superficially)
          **Technique:** Similar to base approach but inject at DIP level on both sides; easier for operators unfamiliar with base anatomy
          **Note:** Does NOT anaesthetize tip of thumb (overlap anatomy); base approach preferred for thumb work

        #### Tips for success
        
          - Avoid epinephrine (adrenaline) in digital nerve block solutions — risk of vasospasm and tissue necrosis
          - Do not inject more than 6-10 mL total per finger (risk of compartment syndrome)
          - If anaesthesia incomplete after 10 minutes, may inject supplementary 1-2 mL without waiting longer
          - Test anaesthesia with pinprick or light touch before proceeding with procedure

        #### Complications
        
          **Incomplete block:** May need supplementary infiltration or retry
          **Nerve damage:** Rare; presents as persistent numbness; avoid repeated needle passes
          **Intravascular injection:** If blood aspirated, withdraw and redirect
          **Tissue necrosis:** If epinephrine used (avoid in digital blocks)

      🧠Interscalene nerve block (shoulder/arm)▾
      
        Useful for shoulder procedures, fracture reduction, and proximal arm analgesia. Moderate difficulty; requires good anatomical knowledge.

          Anatomy of brachial plexus at interscalene level
          **Location:** Brachial plexus roots (C5-C6) lie between anterior and middle scalene muscles at neck level
          **Key landmark:** Interscalene groove (depression between scalene muscles) at level of cricoid cartilage (C6)
          **Covers:** Shoulder, lateral arm, forearm (C5-C6 distribution)
          **Note:** Does NOT reliably cover hand/fingers (C8-T1); if ulnar/median sensation needed, use supraclavicular or axillary block

          Interscalene nerve block technique (palpation-guided)
          **Position:** Patient supine or semi-seated; head turned away from side being blocked
          **Landmarks:** Palpate sternocleidomastoid (SCM); trace posteriorly to find interscalene groove; locate at level of cricoid cartilage (C6 transverse process)
          
          **Procedure:**
          
            1
            Clean skin; infiltrate skin and subcutaneous tissue with 1% lignocaine on 25G needle

            2
            With 22G 50 mm needle on 10 mL syringe, insert into interscalene groove at C6 level, directed perpendicular to skin

            3
            Advance needle until paresthesia felt (patient reports pain/tingling radiating down arm) — STOP immediately

            4
            If no paresthesia after 3-4 cm advancement, withdraw slightly and aspirate to ensure not intravascular

            5
            Inject 15-20 mL of 1% lignocaine or 0.5% bupivacaine slowly with frequent aspiration checks

            6
            Withdraw needle; wait 10-15 minutes for full onset

          **Local anaesthetic volume:** 15-20 mL of 1% lignocaine or 0.5% bupivacaine
          **Success rate:** 75-85% with palpation; 95%+ with ultrasound
          **Duration:** 1-2 hours (lignocaine) or 4-6 hours (bupivacaine)

        #### Complications specific to interscalene block
        
          **Horner's syndrome:** Pupil miosis, ptosis from inadvertent sympathetic chain block; resolves when block wears off
          **Phrenic nerve block (C3-C4):** Diaphragmatic paralysis; patient may experience dyspnea; avoid in patients with respiratory compromise
          **Recurrent laryngeal nerve block:** Hoarseness, voice change; resolves within hours
          **Intravascular injection:** Seizure, cardiac dysrhythmia if bupivacaine; manage per toxicity protocol
          **Nerve injury:** Rare; persistent pain/numbness warrants referral

          Caution
          Ultrasound guidance significantly improves safety and success. If palpation technique difficult, consider referral to anaesthetist or do not proceed. Avoid in patients with severe COPD or unilateral diaphragm paralysis (phrenic nerve risk).

      🦵Common peroneal (fibular) nerve block (ankle/foot)▾
      
        Useful for foot/ankle procedures, fracture reduction of lateral foot structures. Straightforward anatomy; relatively safe block.

          Common peroneal nerve anatomy
          **Course:** Arises from sciatic nerve at mid-thigh; curves around fibular head and neck at knee level before dividing into deep and superficial branches
          **Location for block:** Just lateral to fibular head at knee level; posterior to fibula
          **Innervation:** Anterior and lateral leg, dorsum of foot, first web space (deep peroneal); lateral leg and dorsum of foot (superficial peroneal)

          Common peroneal nerve block technique
          **Position:** Patient supine or prone with knee slightly flexed
          **Landmarks:** Palpate fibular head (bony prominence lateral to knee); locate posterior to fibula at head level
          
          **Procedure:**
          
            1
            Clean skin; infiltrate with 1% lignocaine on 25G needle

            2
            Using 22G 50 mm needle, insert posterior to fibular head, directed slightly anterior and medial

            3
            Advance until paresthesia felt (patient reports tingling in anterior leg or foot) — STOP

            4
            Aspirate to exclude intravascular placement

            5
            Inject 15-20 mL of 1% lignocaine or 0.5% bupivacaine slowly

            6
            Withdraw needle; wait 10-15 minutes for full onset

          **Local anaesthetic volume:** 15-20 mL of 1% lignocaine or 0.5% bupivacaine
          **Success rate:** 80-90% with palpation
          **Duration:** 1-2 hours (lignocaine) or 4-6 hours (bupivacaine)

        #### Tips for success
        
          - Paresthesia elicitation is key; patient cooperation essential
          - If paresthesia not achieved after 3-4 cm needle advancement, slightly adjust needle direction and retry (1-2 mm increments)
          - Do not exceed max dose of local anaesthetic for patient weight
          - Test anaesthesia with pinprick before proceeding; foot drop may not develop immediately

        #### Complications
        
          **Incomplete block:** May need supplementary infiltration or re-block
          **Nerve damage:** Rare; persistent foot drop or numbness warrants neurology referral
          **Intravascular injection:** Toxicity risk; use aspirating needle to avoid

      ⚠️Complications and toxicity management▾
      
        #### Local anaesthetic toxicity
        **Mechanism:** Overdose or intravascular injection causes CNS and cardiac toxicity. Bupivacaine more cardiotoxic than lignocaine.

        #### Early signs (CNS toxicity)
        
          - Tinnitus, visual disturbances
          - Circumoral numbness, tongue numbness
          - Restlessness, confusion
          - Muscle twitching, tremor
          - Seizure (generalized tonic-clonic)

        #### Late signs (cardiac toxicity)
        
          - Bradycardia, hypotension
          - Dysrhythmias (VT, VF, PEA, asystole)
          - Cardiac arrest (may be refractory to standard ACLS)

        #### Management of toxicity
        
          **Stop injection immediately**
          **Call for help** and emergency services if not in hospital
          **Position patient supine**; elevate legs if hypotensive
          **Manage seizure:** Benzodiazepine (midazolam 0.1 mg/kg IV) or thiopental
          **Provide high-flow oxygen** (100% O2)
          **If cardiac instability or seizure refractory to benzodiazepine:** Consider 20% lipid emulsion (Intralipid) 1.5 mL/kg IV bolus over 1 minute, followed by continuous infusion; repeat every 5-10 minutes up to 12 mL/kg cumulative dose
          **ACLS as needed:** Standard advanced life support; prolonged resuscitation may be required (up to 60+ minutes reported with lipid therapy)

          Prevention is critical
          Calculate max dose for patient weight; aspirate frequently during injection; inject slowly; use lowest effective concentration; avoid intravascular injection. Have lipid emulsion available if using bupivacaine.

          **Infection:** Rare (
          **Hematoma:** Usually minor; manage with ice and analgesia
          **Nerve injury:** Rare; may be transient (neurapraxia) or permanent (axonotmesis); counsel patient before procedure
          **Allergic reaction:** To local anaesthetic (true allergy rare) or preservatives; have adrenaline available

        #### Equipment checklist
        
          - Sterile gloves, hand hygiene, skin disinfectant
          - 22G or 25G needles (1-2 inches depending on block)
          - 10 mL syringe for injection
          - Local anaesthetic solution (lignocaine 1%, bupivacaine 0.5%)
          - Resuscitation kit: adrenaline, oxygen, suction, benzodiazepines
          - 20% lipid emulsion (Intralipid) if using bupivacaine
          - Pulse oximetry, blood pressure cuff available
