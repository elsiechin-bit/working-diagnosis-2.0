## The conversation you're not having
      Dentists often field questions about medication side effects long after GPs prescribe them. Patients don't connect their new antibiotic to brown staining, or their seizure medication to gum swelling. These effects are real, sometimes irreversible, and often preventable with forethought. Your prescribing affects their dental health for decades.

      ## Fluorosis: too much of a good thing
      Fluoride prevents caries—but excessive ingestion during tooth development (age 0–8 years) causes dental fluorosis: white spots, brown flecking, pitting and surface irregularities.

        **Sources:** Fluoridated water (0.7ppm optimal in NZ), toothpaste swallowing (high in young children), supplements, tea (variable fluoride content), and some medications.
        **Medications with fluoride:** Quinolones (ciprofloxacin), sodium fluoride supplements, some formulations.
        - Mild fluorosis (white spots) cosmetic only. Severe fluorosis (brown staining, pitting) affects function and appearance.

      ## Gingival overgrowth
      Several drugs cause gum hyperplasia: enlargement and thickening of the gingival margin and interdental papillae. Cosmetically concerning, functionally impairing (poor oral hygiene, gingival bleeding), and sometimes requiring surgical trimming.

      ### Phenytoin
      
        - Classic culprit: 50% incidence of gingival overgrowth in patients on phenytoin long-term.
        - Onset weeks to months; severity increases with dose and duration.
        - Mechanism: fibroblast hyperactivity + collagen deposition in gingival tissue.
        - Prevention: excellent oral hygiene, professional cleaning, early intervention if swelling starts.
        - Management: may regress partially if medication stopped; otherwise surgical gingivectomy.

      ### Calcium channel blockers
      
        - Nifedipine, amlodipine, diltiazem: 10–20% incidence (less than phenytoin, but common).
        - Risk higher with older age, poor oral hygiene, male sex (inconsistent data).
        - Onset insidious: gradual gum swelling over months.
        - Management: excellent oral hygiene, professional cleaning; if severe and refractory, consider alternative antihypertensive; surgical trimming last resort.

      ### Immunosuppressants
      
        - Cyclosporine (post-transplant, severe autoimmune): 25–30% incidence of gingival overgrowth.
        - Tacrolimus: less common but reported.
        - Combined with poor oral hygiene, risk compounds.
        - Prevention & management: aggressive plaque control, frequent professional cleaning, oral assessment before starting cyclosporine.

      ### Other medications
      
        **Antiepileptics beyond phenytoin:** Valproate, phenobarbital (less common than phenytoin).
        **Anabolic steroids:** Rare but reported.

      ## Xerostomia (dry mouth)
      Anticholinergic medications, antihistamines, antidepressants, antipsychotics, and some antihypertensives reduce salivary flow. Saliva protects teeth; without it, caries risk skyrockets.

        **High-risk medications:** Tricyclic antidepressants (amitriptyline, nortriptyline), anticholinergics (benztropine), some antihistamines.
        **Effects:** Accelerated caries (especially cervical), fungal infection (candidiasis), difficulty swallowing, altered taste.
        **Management:** Frequent dental review, sugar-free lozenges, artificial saliva, topical fluoride, consider medication switch if severe.

      ## Bisphosphonates: jaw osteonecrosis
      High-dose IV bisphosphonates (alendronate IV, zoledronic acid for cancer & hypercalcemia) carry rare but serious risk of osteonecrosis of the jaw (ONJ): bone death, infection, exposed bone in the mandible or maxilla.

        **Risk factors:** Invasive dental work (extraction, implant, periodontal surgery) after bisphosphonate; duration of therapy >3 years; malignancy; poor oral hygiene; alcohol/tobacco.
        **Incidence:** ~1–10% in cancer patients on IV bisphosphonates who undergo dental procedures.
        **Prevention:** Oral assessment & comprehensive dental work BEFORE starting high-dose bisphosphonates. Avoid invasive procedures during therapy. Resume after drug-free interval (unclear duration—individualize).
        - Oral bisphosphonates (alendronate, risedronate for osteoporosis) carry minimal ONJ risk (~1 in 100,000).

        **Red flag:** Jaw pain, loose teeth, or non-healing extraction sockets during bisphosphonate therapy = osteonecrosis until proven otherwise. Urgent dental & oncology review.

      ## Statin-induced enamel defects
      Rare case reports of enamel hypoplasia (pitting, white spots) in children exposed to statins in-utero or in early infancy. Mechanism unclear; incidence unknown but likely very low. Clinical relevance in NZ limited (statins rarely used in pregnancy/infancy except familial hypercholesterolaemia). Document if prescribing to pregnant person.

      ## Antiretrovirals & enamel defects
      Some older antiretrovirals (particularly didanosine, if used during tooth development) have been associated with enamel hypoplasia. Modern regimens less well-studied; unlikely a major concern now, but relevant for children with vertical HIV transmission exposed during early tooth development.

      ## Clinical approach
      
      ### When prescribing in children
      
        - Ask about tooth development stage (primary vs permanent teeth).
        Counsel on tetracycline avoidance 
        - Discuss fluoride exposure (toothpaste, supplements, water) when prescribing supplements or high-fluoride medications.
        - Consider alternative if long-term therapy and medication known to cause dental effects.

      ### When starting gingival overgrowth-risk medications
      
        - Refer for dental assessment before starting phenytoin, cyclosporine, or long-term calcium channel blockers (especially if young/cosmetically concerned).
        - Counsel on rigorous oral hygiene importance.
        - Monitor for early swelling; escalate to dentist promptly if emerging.

      ### When starting bisphosphonates
      
        - Comprehensive dental assessment first (especially if any active disease or need for extraction).
        - Complete invasive dental work before therapy, if possible.
        - Document risk with patient; counsel on avoiding extractions during therapy unless unavoidable.

      ### Xerostomia management
      
        - Ask about dry mouth at each visit on anticholinergic or SSRI therapy.
        - If emerging, trial sugar-free lozenges or artificial saliva first.
        - Refer to dentist for topical fluoride & caries prevention.
        - Consider alternative medication if severe and functionally impairing (dry mouth + dysphagia poor quality of life).

      ## The reminder
      Most medication-induced dental effects are preventable or manageable with foresight. Your role: anticipate risk, counsel before prescribing, and coordinate with patients' dentists when long-term medications start. A 6-year-old on tetracycline for acne suffers lifelong staining because the risk wasn't considered. A 50-year-old on phenytoin for epilepsy needs gingival surgery because swelling wasn't managed early. These aren't unavoidable. They're foreseeable.
