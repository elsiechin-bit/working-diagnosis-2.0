## Overview


**Upper respiratory tract infections (URTIs) are the most common acute illness in primary care,** affecting all ages with highest incidence in young children (4–6 episodes per year). The vast majority are viral, self-limiting, and do not require specific treatment. Winter peaks correlate with temperature, humidity, and crowding; some viruses (RSV, influenza) are strictly seasonal, others (rhinovirus) year-round.

        #### Seasonal patterns in Aotearoa (Southern Hemisphere)
        
          **Winter (June–August):** Peak for influenza, RSV, parainfluenza, adenovirus, coronavirus
          **Spring (September–November):** Lingering RSV, parainfluenza; increasing rhinovirus
          **Summer (December–February):** Rhinovirus, enterovirus dominant; RSV/influenza rare
          **Autumn (March–May):** Transition; increasing rhinovirus, early influenza circulation
        
        #### Pathophysiology & transmission
        
          **Respiratory epithelial infection:** Viral replication in nasal, pharyngeal, and laryngeal epithelium → inflammatory response → mucus production, oedema, cough
          **Transmission:** Droplet (respiratory secretions); fomite contact (hands, surfaces); close proximity accelerates spread
          **Incubation:** 1–3 days for most viruses; contagious for 24 hours pre-symptom until 7 days post-onset (longer in immunocompromised)
          **Duration:** Most viral URTIs self-resolve in 7–10 days; cough often persists 2–3 weeks

      🦠Common viral aetiologies & clinical patterns▾
      
        #### Rhinovirus (~50% of URTIs)
        
          **Season:** Year-round; peaks spring & autumn
          **Presentation:** Nasal congestion, rhinorrhoea, mild throat soreness, low-grade fever or none; rarely systemic symptoms
          **Duration:** 7–10 days typically; cough may linger 3 weeks
          **Complications:** Otitis media, sinusitis (especially if congestion severe); asthma exacerbation in susceptible; rarely pneumonia
          **Age impact:** Mild across all ages; infants may have more lower respiratory involvement
          **Management:** Supportive only; analgesics, saline irrigation; most do not warrant testing
        
        #### Influenza virus (~5–10% of URTIs in season)
        
          **Season:** Strict winter peak (June–August in NZ); epidemics every 1–3 years with new strains
          **Presentation:** **Systemic illness**: abrupt onset fever (>38.5°C), malaise, myalgia (legs, back), headache, cough, sore throat; constitutional symptoms dominate
          **Duration:** Fever 3–5 days; weakness/malaise 1–2 weeks; cough 2–3 weeks
          **High-risk groups:** Age >65, chronic disease (asthma, COPD, diabetes, CVD, renal), pregnancy, obesity (BMI >30), Māori/Pasifika ancestry, immunocompromised
          **Complications:** Pneumonia (viral or secondary bacterial), myocarditis, encephalitis, Guillain-Barré syndrome (rare); mortality in high-risk groups
          **Antiviral window:** Neuraminidase inhibitors (oseltamivir, zanamivir) reduce duration by ~24 hours & complications if given within 48 hours of symptom onset; greatest benefit in high-risk groups
          **Management:** Supportive care; antivirals for high-risk groups within 48 hours; vaccine-preventable (annual, >60% efficacy)
        
        #### Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV; ~10–15% winter URTIs)
        
          **Season:** Strict winter peak (May–September in NZ); biennial cycle
          **Presentation:** Rhinorrhoea, cough (often productive/wheezy), low-grade fever; **lower respiratory involvement common** (bronchiolitis in infants, tracheobronchitis in older)
          **Age-risk:** Most severe in infants 
          **Duration:** Symptoms 7–14 days; cough may persist 4 weeks; excretion contagious for ~7 days (longer in young infants)
          **Hospitalisations:** Infants 
          **Complications:** Bronchiolitis, otitis media, sinusitis; secondary bacterial infection rare
          **Management:** Supportive (oxygen if SpO₂ 
        
        #### Parainfluenza virus (~5% winter URTIs)
        
          **Season:** Fall/winter peak; occasional spring surge
          **Presentation:** Rhinorrhoea, sore throat, mild fever; **croup (laryngotracheobronchitis) typical in children 6 months–3 years** → barking "seal" cough, stridor, hoarseness
          **Duration:** 7–10 days; croup symptoms subside within 3–5 days with dexamethasone
          **Croup management:** Dexamethasone 0.15 mg/kg PO/IM (single dose); epiglottitis less common but requires urgent airway assessment
        
        #### Coronavirus (including SARS-CoV-2; ~5–10% winter URTIs 2025)
        
          **Season:** Winter peaks; year-round circulation
          **Presentation:** Cough, fever, malaise, anosmia/dysgeusia; variable severity (mild URI to pneumonia)
          **Duration:** 7–14 days; post-viral cough/fatigue common
          **High-risk:** Age >65, chronic disease, immunocompromised; Long COVID (fatigue, dyspnoea, cognitive symptoms) in 10–20% at 12 weeks
          **Management:** Supportive; antivirals (nirmatrelvir/ritonavir) for high-risk within 5 days of symptom onset; vaccine available (uptake declining)
        
        #### Adenovirus (~3–5% URTIs)
        
          **Season:** Winter; year-round possible
          **Presentation:** Fever, pharyngitis (exudative, not strep), conjunctivitis ("pharyngoconjunctival fever"), cough, GI symptoms possible
          **Duration:** 5–10 days
          **Complications:** Rare; bronchitis, pneumonia possible in severe
        
        #### Enterovirus (Coxsackievirus, Echovirus; ~3% URTIs)
        
          **Season:** Late summer–autumn mainly; some year-round
          **Presentation:** Fever, sore throat, cough, rash (sometimes), hand-foot-mouth disease (enterovirus 71), herpangina (painful ulcers on palate)
          **Duration:** 3–7 days typically
          **Risk:** Neonates & immunocompromised at risk for severe disease (myocarditis, meningitis)

      🔍Clinical assessment & when to investigate▾
      
        #### History & exam features differentiating viral URTIs
        
          VirusKey featuresWhen to test
          
            **Rhinovirus**Nasal symptoms dominant; mild/no feverRarely; clinical diagnosis sufficient
            **Influenza**Abrupt systemic illness; fever >38.5°C; myalgiaDuring season if high-risk or considering antivirals (48-hr window)
            **RSV**Lower respiratory (wheeze, crackles); infant Hospitalised infants; elderly with pneumonia; immunocompromised
            **Parainfluenza**Croup (barky cough, stridor); 6 mo–3 yrsClinical diagnosis; no test needed for croup
            **Coronavirus**Variable (URI to pneumonia); anosmia/dysgeusia commonIf hospitalisable pneumonia, high-risk considering antivirals
            **Adenovirus**Pharyngitis + conjunctivitis (pharyngoconjunctival fever)Rarely; clinical recognition sufficient

        #### Symptoms NOT explained by uncomplicated viral URTI (investigate further)
        
          **Prolonged fever >7–10 days** (suggests secondary infection or other diagnosis)
          **Severe dyspnoea, stridor at rest** (croup, epiglottitis, peritonsillar abscess)
          **Focal chest pain, haemoptysis** (pneumonia, pleuritis)
          **Severe headache, neck stiffness, altered consciousness** (meningitis)
          **Unilateral ear pain with otalgia** (otitis media, mastoiditis)
          **Facial pain over sinuses + purulent nasal discharge** (bacterial sinusitis)
          **Swollen, deviated tonsils with trismus, dysphagia** (peritonsillar abscess)
          **Petechial/purpuric rash** (meningococcaemia, other serious infection)
        
        #### Testing: respiratory virus PCR
        
          **Rapid molecular PCR (nasopharyngeal swab):** Multiplex panels detect influenza, RSV, COVID-19, others; high sensitivity/specificity
          **When indicated:** Hospitalised patients, high-risk groups within 48 hours of symptom onset (to guide antivirals), outbreaks, epidemiological surveillance
          **When NOT indicated:** Uncomplicated URTI in low-risk outpatients; clinical diagnosis sufficient; testing does not change supportive management
          **NZ testing:** NZPHL surveillance; rapid tests available in ED/hospitals; community GP testing declining as most mild URTIs do not benefit from results

      💊Management & symptomatic care▾
      
        #### Supportive management (ALL viral URTIs)
        
          **Hydration:** Fluids oral; IV if unable to swallow or severe illness
          **Analgesia & antipyretics:**
            
              - Paracetamol: 15 mg/kg per dose (up to 1 g), 4–6 hourly, max 5 doses/24 h
              - Ibuprofen: 5–10 mg/kg per dose (up to 600 mg), 6–8 hourly; avoid in severe dengue or varicella (rare complications)
              - Avoid aspirin in children (Reye syndrome risk)

          **Saline rinse/spray:** Nasal congestion relief; safe, effective
          **Honey (age >1 year):** Cough suppression; 2.5–5 mL before bed; evidence supports use
          **Rest & isolation:** 24–48 hours off school/work if fever or acutely unwell; return when afebrile without antipyretics & well enough for usual activities
          **Avoid antibiotics:** Viral URTIs do not benefit from antibiotics; contributes to resistance
        
        #### Specific antiviral therapy
        
          **Influenza (oseltamivir/zanamivir):** 
            
              Indicated: High-risk group (age >65, chronic disease, pregnancy, obesity, Māori/Pasifika) OR hospitalised with severe illness, **within 48 hours of symptom onset**
              - Oseltamivir (Tamiflu): 75 mg PO BD × 5 days (age-adjusted dosing for children)
              - Benefit: ~24-hour reduction in fever duration; reduced complications in high-risk groups; no benefit if >48 hours from onset

          **COVID-19 (nirmatrelvir/ritonavir [Paxlovid]):**
            
              Indicated: High-risk group, **within 5 days of symptom onset**
              - Benefit: ~50% reduction in progression to severe disease; most benefit in first 3 days
              - NZ access: Limited funded access; mostly private ($400–600 NZD)

          **RSV:** No routine antiviral; ribavirin reserved for severe immunocompromised
        
        #### Croup (parainfluenza, rarely other viruses)
        
          **Dexamethasone:** 0.15 mg/kg PO/IM single dose (max 6 mg) within 24 hours of symptom onset
          **Effect:** Reduces croup severity & duration by ~50%; aerosolised epinephrine (1:1000) for severe stridor at rest (requires ED)
          **Cruppy cough alone without stridor:** Dexamethasone still beneficial; reduces progression
        
        #### Misconceptions to address
        
          **Antibiotics for viral URTI:** Do NOT help; only risk resistance. Reserve for confirmed bacterial superinfection (bacterial sinusitis, otitis media with discharge)
          Honey for infants  Avoid (botulism risk). Use saline rinse/steam instead.
          **Cough suppressants (codeine, dextromethorphan):** Not recommended; cough is protective. Use honey instead.
          **Fever = danger:** Fever is protective immune response; treat discomfort not the number. Antipyretics improve comfort; do not accelerate recovery.

      ⚠️Complications & red flags▾
      
        #### When uncomplicated viral URTI progresses (ED referral)
        
          **Pneumonia:** Fever + focal crackles, consolidation on CXR; tachypnoea, hypoxia
          **Acute otitis media:** Otalgia, fever; opaque, immobile tympanum; may have discharge
          **Bacterial sinusitis:** Fever >7 days + purulent nasal discharge + facial/sinus pain; CT shows sinus opacification
          **Peritonsillar abscess:** Severe sore throat, dysphagia, fever; deviated uvula, trismus; requires drainage
          **Epiglottitis:** Stridor at rest, dysphagia, drooling, respiratory distress; medical emergency; do not attempt IV/throat exam in ED (airway risk)
          **Myocarditis:** Chest pain, dyspnoea, palpitations, signs of heart failure; ECG changes, elevated troponin
          **Meningitis:** Headache, neck stiffness, fever, altered consciousness, petechial rash; CSF findings diagnostic
        
        #### Prolonged cough after viral URTI
        
          **Post-viral cough:** Cough persists 2–3 weeks after viral URI (rhinovirus); supportive care only; resolves spontaneously
          **Pertussis-like paroxysms:** Rare; if prolonged paroxysmal cough, test for Bordetella pertussis (serology, PCR)
          **Asthma exacerbation:** Viral URI triggers bronchospasm in susceptible; wheeze, dyspnoea; treat with bronchodilators/ICS

      💡Clinical pearls & pitfalls▾
      
        #### Pearls
        
          ✦**Most URTIs are viral & self-resolving.** Diagnosis clinical; testing rarely changes management. Save PCR for hospitalised or high-risk considering antivirals.
          ✦**Influenza antiviral window = 48 hours.** Oseltamivir must be given within 48 hours of symptom onset to reduce duration. Later = minimal benefit.
          ✦**Croup is clinical diagnosis; dexamethasone is game-changer.** Single-dose dexamethasone 0.15 mg/kg reduces severity/duration ~50%. Do not wait for imaging.
          ✦**Antibiotics do NOT help viral URTIs.** Only prescribe for confirmed bacterial superinfection (purulent discharge, persistent fever >7 days + focal findings).
          ✦RSV is highest-risk in infants  Bronchiolitis common; watch for tachypnoea, crackles, hypoxia. Hospitalisations peak June–August in NZ.
          ✦**Post-viral cough lasts 2–3 weeks; this is normal.** Most parents expect quick resolution. Set expectations upfront; cough does not indicate failure to recover.
          ✦**Fever is protective, not dangerous (in isolation).** Treat for comfort; do not target a specific temperature. Antipyretics do not prevent complications.
          ✦**Cough suppressants are not recommended.** Cough clears secretions. Honey (age >1 yr) is effective evidence-based alternative.
          ✦**Influenza in high-risk groups carries serious risk.** Age >65, chronic disease, Māori/Pasifika, obesity = higher hospitalisation, complications, mortality. Low threshold for antivirals.
        
        #### Pitfalls
        
          **Prescribing antibiotics for uncomplicated viral URTI.** Does not shorten illness, encourages resistance, side effects. Reserve for proven bacterial infection.
          **Testing every URTI.** Multiplex PCR results often misleading (prolonged shedding, asymptomatic colonisation); clinical diagnosis sufficient for most.
          **Delaying influenza antivirals past 48-hour window.** Benefits only realised if given early. Do not prescribe at 72+ hours from symptom onset.
          **Missing croup as diagnosis.** Do not assume all barky cough is laryngitis. Dexamethasone is highly beneficial; do not defer.
          **Overlooking red flags for serious complication.** Severe dyspnoea, altered consciousness, petechial rash, severe chest pain = ED evaluation, not office management.
          **Not counselling on post-viral cough duration.** Parents expect 7-day cure. Failure to set expectations → dissatisfaction, unnecessary follow-up, more testing.
          **Over-investigating uncomplicated URTI.** X-rays, swabs, blood work rarely change management in low-risk outpatients. Clinical judgment trumps investigations.

      🌍NZ-specific surveillance & winter preparedness▾
      
        #### New Zealand respiratory surveillance (ESR, NZPHL)
        
          **Respiratory Virus Surveillance Network:** Tracks influenza, RSV, parainfluenza, adenovirus, coronavirus weekly; reports published online
          **Influenza season:** Declared June–August typically; thresholds monitored; vaccine roll-out April–May
          **Free flu vaccine (DHB-funded):** Age >65, chronic disease groups (asthma, COPD, diabetes, CVD, renal), pregnant, Māori/Pasifika, healthcare workers
          **RSV surveillance:** Hospitalised cases tracked; peaks June–August; no vaccine available (in development)
        
        #### Primary care winter preparedness
        
          **Inventory antivirals early:** Oseltamivir (Tamiflu), zanamivir (Relenza); expected demand in June
          **Croup preparation:** Dexamethasone (0.15 mg/kg dosing); most PCPs can initiate in clinic
          **RSV awareness:** Counsel caregivers of infants 
          **Vaccination drive:** Influenza vaccine in April; COVID-19 vaccine for high-risk groups; promotion reduces winter severity
