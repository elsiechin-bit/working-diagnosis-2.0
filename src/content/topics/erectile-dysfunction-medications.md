## Overview


**Erectile dysfunction (ED) is inability to achieve or sustain erection sufficient for sexual intercourse.** Affects 200+ million men globally; prevalence rises sharply with age, cardiovascular disease, diabetes, depression. Often multifactorial; both organic and psychogenic components common.

        #### Physiology of penile erection
        
          **Normal mechanism:** Sexual stimulation → parasympathetic activation → nitric oxide (NO) release from endothelial cells & neuronal terminals → guanylate cyclase activation → ↑ cGMP → smooth muscle relaxation in corpus cavernosum → arterial dilation, venous occlusion → rigidity
          **Termination:** Phosphodiesterase-5 (PDE5) degrades cGMP → smooth muscle contraction → detumescence
          **Endothelial dysfunction:** In disease states (HTN, DM, smoking, dyslipidaemia), impaired NO synthesis & increased PDE5 activity reduce cGMP availability → ED
        
        #### Classification: psychogenic vs organic
        
          **Psychogenic (30% of cases):** Performance anxiety, depression, relationship conflict, sexual trauma, stress; sudden onset; preserved nocturnal/morning erections; responds well to PDE5i + counselling
          **Organic (70% of cases):** Vascular disease, neurogenic (DM neuropathy, post-prostatectomy, spinal cord injury), hormonal (testosterone deficiency), medication-induced, penile fibrosis (Peyronie's); gradual onset; loss of nocturnal erections; may require multidisciplinary approach
          **Mixed:** Most common; organic pathology triggers psychogenic overlay (performance anxiety, shame)
        
        #### Risk factors & associations
        
          **Cardiovascular:** Hypertension (most common), coronary artery disease, atherosclerosis, heart failure, prior MI/stroke
          **Metabolic:** Type 2 diabetes (affects ~50% of men with DM), dyslipidaemia, obesity, metabolic syndrome
          **Neurogenic:** Diabetes neuropathy, multiple sclerosis, Parkinson's, spinal cord injury, post-prostatectomy/pelvic surgery
          **Hormonal:** Testosterone deficiency (hypogonadism, aging), hyperprolactinaemia, hypothyroidism
          **Medications:** Antihypertensives (β-blockers, thiazides), antidepressants (SSRIs, tricyclics), antipsychotics, opioids, finasteride, cimetidine
          **Lifestyle:** Smoking (doubles risk), excessive alcohol, sedentary, stress, poor sleep

      🔍Clinical assessment & diagnosis▾
      
        #### History
        
          **ED severity & onset:** Gradual (organic) vs sudden (psychogenic); full loss vs partial; context (with partner, penetration attempts, masturbation)
          **Nocturnal/morning erections:** Preserved = psychogenic favoured; absent = organic likely
          **Associated symptoms:** Decreased libido (hypogonadism), ejaculation disorders, pelvic pain (Peyronie's), depression/anxiety
          **Relationship dynamics:** Partner satisfaction, communication, trauma, infidelity concerns
          **Medications & substances:** Antidepressants, antihypertensives, opioids, cannabis, alcohol, recreational drugs
          **Comorbidities:** Diabetes, CVD, depression, prostate disease, prior surgery
        
        #### International Index of Erectile Function (IIEF-5)
        **Quick 5-item screening tool; score 5–25 (lower = more severe).** Useful for baseline & monitoring response to therapy.

          ScoreSeverity
          
            21–25No ED
            17–20Mild
            12–16Mild-to-moderate
            8–11Moderate
            5–7Severe

        #### Physical examination
        
          **Cardiovascular:** BP, heart rate, peripheral pulses, signs of atherosclerosis
          **Genital:** Penile curvature or plaques (Peyronie's), testicular volume/consistency, secondary sexual characteristics
          **Neurological:** Monofilament testing, vibration sense, bulbocavernosus reflex (if neurogenic ED suspected)
          **Abdomen:** Obesity, gynaecomastia (hypogonadism)
        
        #### Investigations (selective, not routine for all)
        
          **Baseline all patients:** Fasting glucose/HbA1c, lipid panel, PSA (if considering finasteride)
          **If hypogonadism suspected:** Morning testosterone, LH, FSH, prolactin
          **If cardiovascular risk unclear:** ECG, lipids, consider stress testing in asymptomatic men wanting ED treatment
          **Penile duplex ultrasound:** Reserved for severe cases, suspected Peyronie's, or post-prostatectomy ED

      💊Phosphodiesterase-5 (PDE5) inhibitors: pharmacology & use▾
      
        **PDE5 inhibitors are first-line pharmacotherapy for ED.** Enhance NO-cGMP pathway by blocking cGMP breakdown. Efficacy 60–80% across aetiologies; safe if cardiovascular contraindications absent.

        #### Available PDE5 inhibitors (NZ)
        
          DrugDoseOnsetDurationKey features
          
            **Sildenafil (Viagra)**50–100 mg PO30–60 min4–5 hoursFirst PDE5i; shortest duration; requires sexual stimulation; blue vision tint; meals delay onset

            **Avanafil (Spedra)**50–200 mg PO12–15 min6–12 hoursFastest onset; minimal food effect; less visual side effects; new option; efficacy/safety similar to sildenafil

        #### Mechanism & efficacy
        
          **Selective PDE5 inhibition:** Allows cGMP accumulation in corpus cavernosum smooth muscle → vasodilation & tumescence
          **Requires sexual stimulation:** Not aphrodisiacs; no effect without neurogenic input; hence psychogenic cases respond better than severe organic
          **Efficacy:** ~60–70% achieve adequate erection in drug-responsive ED; ~80% in psychogenic; ~40–50% in post-prostatectomy/severe neuropathy
          **Onset of action:** Best on empty stomach; sildenafil/tadalafil delayed by fatty meals (avanafil minimally affected)
        
        #### Dosing strategy
        
          **Sildenafil:** Start 50 mg 30–60 min before intercourse; titrate to 100 mg if inadequate response; max frequency QD
          **Tadalafil:** 10 mg PRN 30–45 min before intercourse (max QD); OR 2.5–5 mg daily for men with frequent intercourse. Daily regimen improves spontaneity; efficacy similar to PRN
          **Avanafil:** 50–100 mg 15–30 min before intercourse; titrate to 200 mg max; fast onset allows more spontaneity
          **Initial trial:** Allow 4–6 attempts at each dose before concluding failure; educate patient on optimal timing, stimulation, partner communication
        
        #### Side effects
        
          **Common (mild, self-limiting):** Headache (15–20%), flushing (10%), dyspepsia (5–10%), nasal congestion, back pain (tadalafil), myalgia
          **Visual disturbances:** Sildenafil/tadalafil: blue-tinted vision (3%), photophobia; avanafil rarely
          **Cardiovascular:** Hypotension (especially with nitrates), syncope, palpitations, angina (rarely unmasked)
          **Rare but serious:** Non-arteritic anterior ischaemic optic neuropathy (NAION; vision loss; ~1/10,000), priapism (~1/12,000), sudden hearing loss

      ⚠️Contraindications & cardiovascular safety▾
      
        #### Absolute contraindications
        
          **Concurrent nitrate use (any route):** Sildenafil, tadalafil, avanafil cause severe, unpredictable hypotension. Absolute CI.
          **Riociguat (guanylate cyclase stimulator):** Same mechanism as PDE5i; additive vasodilation risk
        
        #### Relative contraindications & dose adjustment needed
        
          Recent MI (170/100): Defer until stabilised; requires cardiology assessment
          **Hepatic impairment (Child-Pugh B/C):** Reduce dose; sildenafil 25 mg, tadalafil 5 mg max
          Renal impairment (eGFR  Reduce dose; caution with tadalafil (longer accumulation); avanafil not studied
          **Concurrent medications:** CYP3A4 inhibitors (protease inhibitors, azole antifungals, macrolide antibiotics, ritonavir-boosted ARVs) → ↑ drug levels; reduce PDE5i dose
          Hypotension (baseline SBP  Use with caution; patient monitoring
        
        #### Cardiovascular safety: who can use PDE5i?
        
          Sexual activity itself is cardiovascular exertion.
          If a man cannot walk upstairs without symptoms, he may not be safe for intercourse (with or without PDE5i). Assessment should reflect this.

          **Low risk (safe for PDE5i):** Mild angina (stable, infrequent), HTN (controlled), prior MI >6 weeks, post-revascularisation, diabetes, dyslipidaemia without symptoms
          **Intermediate risk (needs assessment before PDE5i):** Frequent angina, uncontrolled HTN, heart failure (EF 
          **High risk (defer, rarely indicated):** Unstable angina, MI 
        
        #### Drug interactions
        
          **α-blockers (doxazosin, tamsulosin):** Additive hypotensive effect; relative CI if man on high-dose α-blocker; if both needed, separate dosing, monitor BP
          **Antihypertensives:** Additive BP lowering; educate on postural care, avoid rapid position changes
          **CYP3A4 inhibitors:** Reduce PDE5i dose (see above)

      🏥Alternative & adjunctive therapies▾
      
        #### Penile vacuum erection devices (penis pump)
        
          **Mechanism:** External suction creates negative pressure → draws blood into corpus cavernosum; constriction ring placed at base maintains tumescence
          **Efficacy:** 60–90% achieve functional erection; works across all aetiologies (psychogenic, organic, medication-induced)
          **Advantages:** No medication side effects, no systemic absorption, reusable, non-invasive, combines well with PDE5i
          **Limitations:** User skill required, loss of spontaneity, cold/numb sensation, reduced penile sensation, mechanical discomfort, limited by pelvic pain
          **Counselling:** Educate on proper technique; partner involvement improves compliance; often first-line for post-prostatectomy ED
        
        #### Intracavernosal injections (alprostadil, papaverine, phentolamine)
        
          **Alprostadil (Caverject, Edex):** Prostaglandin E1; intracavernosal injection 1–20 μg; onset 5–20 min; erection lasts 1 hour
          **Efficacy:** 70–80%; works when PDE5i fails; often used post-prostatectomy or severe vascular ED
          **Side effects:** Penile pain (20–60% depending on dose), bruising, fibrosis/nodules (with repeated use), systemic hypotension (rare)
          **Contraindications:** Priapism history, balanitis, penile implant, anticoagulation (relative)
          **Combination therapy:** PDE5i + intracavernosal injection synergistic; useful for refractory cases
        
        #### Topical agents
        
          **Topical alprostadil (MUSE urethral pellet):** Less effective than injection; variable absorption; mainly used in unsophisticated patients unwilling to self-inject
          **Topical nitrates:** Nitroglycerin ointment to penile skin; minimal evidence for efficacy; systemic absorption risk
        
        #### Penile prosthesis
        
          **Indications:** Refractory ED (failed medical/mechanical therapy), severe fibrosis, Peyronie's, post-prostatectomy with severe neurogenic component
          **Types:** Inflatable (more natural, high patient satisfaction, but mechanical failure risk) vs malleable (simpler, more durable, less concealment)
          **Satisfaction:** ~90% patient satisfaction; requires surgery; cost barrier in NZ; typically private
        
        #### Lifestyle & psychosocial interventions
        
          **Modify cardiovascular risk:** Smoking cessation (major impact), weight loss, BP/lipid/glucose control, regular aerobic exercise (30+ min 5×/week improves ED ~40%)
          **Psychotherapy:** Cognitive-behavioural therapy (CBT) for psychogenic ED; couples counselling; useful adjunct to pharmacotherapy
          **Address depression/anxiety:** Treat underlying mood disorder; some antidepressants cause ED (SSRIs), switch if needed; bupropion preferred if depression + ED
          **Medication review:** Deprescribe/switch offending agents (β-blockers, thiazides, SSRIs) if possible; taper rather than cease abruptly

      🧬Testosterone & other hormonal therapy▾
      
        #### Testosterone deficiency in ED
        
          **Prevalence:** Hypogonadism affects ~10–20% of men with ED; more common in older men, obesity, metabolic syndrome, depression
          **Role in ED:** Testosterone ↑ NO production, corpus cavernosum responsiveness, libido; mild deficiency may contribute to ED; severe deficiency (T 
          **Diagnosis:** Clinical suspicion (low libido, fatigue, gynacomastia, small testes) + morning total testosterone 
        
        #### Testosterone replacement therapy
        
          **Indications:** Documented hypogonadism (low T) + symptoms (low libido, ED, fatigue). Testosterone monotherapy for isolated ED without documented deficiency lacks strong evidence.
          **Formulations:** Intramuscular (testosterone enanthate 250 mg IM Q2–3 weeks), transdermal gel/patches (daily; more physiologic), oral (less common, poor absorption)
          **Efficacy on ED:** Improves ED 20–30% in hypogonadal men; often requires PDE5i co-therapy for adequate response
          **Monitoring:** PSA (if >40 years, PSA baseline 
          **Contraindications:** Prostate cancer (active), high PSA without urology clearance, severe untreated OSA, uncontrolled HTN, thrombotic event risk
          **Side effects:** Acne, hair loss, mood changes, fertility (azoospermia with exogenous T), polycythaemia, liver enzyme elevation
        
        #### Other hormonal conditions
        
          **Hyperprolactinaemia:** Dopamine agonist (bromocriptine, cabergoline) if pituitary adenoma; also improves ED
          **Hypothyroidism:** Levothyroxine replacement; corrects ED if T4 deficiency was driver

      🇳🇿NZ prescribing framework & funding▾
      
        #### PHARMAC funding status (2024)
        
          **Sildenafil (Viagra):** Funded (generic available) for eligible men; specific indications: diabetes, spinal cord injury, post-prostatectomy, on certain medications (including SSRIs)
          **Tadalafil (Cialis), avanafil:** Not PHARMAC-funded; available privately (~NZ$30–50/tablet)
          **Alprostadil injection:** Typically not funded; specialist use only; cost ~NZ$50–100/dose
        
        #### Eligibility for funded sildenafil
        
          - Type 1 or 2 diabetes on medication
          - Spinal cord injury
          - Post-prostatectomy (radical, transurethral resection)
          - On antidepressant or antipsychotic (at therapeutic dose ≥3 months)
          - GP must screen for cardiovascular contraindications; obtain patient consent
        
        #### Prescribing process
        
          **GP role:** Take sexual history, assess cardiovascular risk, screen for contraindications (nitrates, severe HTN, recent MI, hepatic/renal disease). If low/intermediate risk, issue prescription; if high risk or uncertain, refer to specialist.
          **Specialist referral:** Urology or sexual medicine for complex cases (priapism history, Peyronie's, post-prostatectomy failure, intolerance to PDE5i)
          **Documentation:** Record baseline IIEF-5 if possible; monitor response at 4–6 weeks; document cardiovascular assessment in notes
        
        #### Primary care counselling framework
        
          **Normalise ED:** Common, treatable condition; not sign of virility loss; many men experience it
          **Explain mechanism:** Simple language: "stress, aging, vessels narrow like heart disease" reduces shame; emphasises organic basis
          **Set expectations:** PDE5i not magic; works ~60–80% of time; requires stimulation; benefits partner communication, stress reduction, lifestyle change
          **Medication counselling:** Timing (empty stomach for sildenafil, 30–60 min before intercourse), no aphrodisiac effect, side effects (headache, flushing), red flags (vision loss = stop, seek help)
          **Address underlying causes:** Smoking, hypertension, depression, relationship conflict warrant targeted intervention
          **Offer referral to sexual medicine/counselling if:** Psychogenic component clear, relationship distress, severe anxiety, failure with PDE5i, patient preference for psychotherapy

      💡Clinical pearls & pitfalls▾
      
        #### Pearls
        
          ✦**ED is often a warning sign of cardiovascular disease.** Men with ED have ~50% higher risk of MI/stroke within 5 years. Use it as entry point to assess CVD risk; treat aggressively.
          ✦**Screen for depression.** Depression & ED are bidirectional; SSRIs help mood but worsen ED. Consider bupropion or tricyclics if SSRI-induced ED. Untreated depression = therapy failure.
          ✦**Allow 4–6 trials before concluding PDE5i failure.** Optimal timing, stimulation, partner involvement, stress level all matter. Educate thoroughly before writing off therapy.
          ✦**Nitrates are absolute contraindication.** PDE5i + nitrate = severe, unpredictable hypotension → MI/stroke. Document clearly; warn patient; consider alternative angina therapies (e.g., β-blockers instead of nitrates).
          ✦**Lifestyle modifications have real efficacy.** Exercise, smoking cessation, weight loss improve ED by 40–60% alone. Combine with pharmacotherapy for best outcomes; not just drug-centric.
          ✦**Combination therapy works.** Vacuum device + PDE5i, intracavernosal injection + PDE5i, testosterone + PDE5i are synergistic. Don't abandon man after single modality fails.
          ✦**Partner communication matters.** Couples counselling & partner involvement improve outcomes substantially. Sexual dysfunction is relationship dysfunction; treat as dyad when possible.
        
        #### Pitfalls
        
          **Missing cardiovascular disease.** ED is sentinel for CAD; cursory CVD risk assessment before PDE5i is dangerous. Ask about exertional symptoms, palpitations, syncope; ECG if uncertain.
          **Prescribing with nitrates.** Catastrophic interaction; fatal in rare cases. Always double-check cardiac medications before issuing PDE5i; document in notes.
          **Ignoring depression/anxiety overlay.** Even organic ED improves less if untreated mood disorder present. Screening for PHQ-9, GAD-7 essential.
          **Not exploring psychogenic factors.** Shame, performance anxiety, relationship conflict often present; quick "here's a pill" approach misses opportunity for meaningful intervention.
          **Single-agent failure = giving up.** Many men respond to adjusted dose, different agent, or combination therapy. Persistence yields results.
          **Overlooking medication-induced ED.** β-blockers, thiazides, SSRIs, opioids, finasteride all implicated. Switch if possible before escalating to specialised therapy.
          **Not addressing modifiable risk factors.** Smoking, sedentary, obesity, uncontrolled HTN/DM perpetuate ED. Medication works better in context of lifestyle change.
