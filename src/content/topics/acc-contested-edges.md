## Overview


ACC is one of New Zealand's most distinctive public institutions. The 1967 Woodhouse Royal Commission set out two principles that still anchor the scheme: community responsibility and comprehensive entitlement [#ref-1]. The Accident Compensation Act 2001 operationalises those principles into something far more bounded - cover for personal injury caused by accident, treatment injury, work-related gradual process, and a small number of other defined categories, with a long list of carve-outs [#ref-2].

        The boundaries do enormous work. They determine whether a back injury after lifting is the community's responsibility or the patient's. Whether a vaginal tear during labour is an accident or biology. Whether a person who develops PTSD after watching their partner die in a car crash gets state-funded therapy or has to fund it themselves.

        For working GPs, these boundaries are not abstract policy matters. They are the daily friction points that emerge in clinical practice. A patient presents with a musculoskeletal injury, you examine them, you formulate a diagnosis, and you reach for the ACC45 form. But the question "is this covered?" often cannot be answered cleanly. The boundary between an injury caused by accident and one caused by underlying degeneration is not a bright line; it is a zone of clinical and medicolegal uncertainty. The same is true for treatment injury, for work-related mental injury, and for a dozen other contested categories.

        This essay maps where those lines currently fall and examines case histories that show how they shift when the evidence and the courts demand it.

        ## A short list of contested conditions

        Where ACC and clinicians most often clash:

          **Pure mental injury.** Unless caused by a covered physical injury, a work-related event meeting the strict s21B test, or a Schedule 3 criminal act (mostly sexual offences), it isn't covered [#ref-2,#ref-3].
          **The accident/degeneration boundary in MSK claims.** A shoulder or back that fails during a specific event but is also degenerate on imaging is the daily friction point. ACC can decline if injury was wholly or substantially caused by an underlying condition or ageing [#ref-2,#ref-4].
          **Treatment injury carve-outs.** "Ordinary consequence" of treatment, "necessary part" of the procedure, and "underlying health" exclusions still narrow the field [#ref-5,#ref-6].
          **Surgical mesh complications.** Historically declined as ordinary consequence or no causal link; a major reassessment exercise has been underway since 2020 [#ref-7,#ref-8].
          **Maternal birth injuries before October 2022.** Tears, prolapses, and obstetric fistula were excluded because the force was internal to the body. Fixed prospectively in 2022, but not retrospectively [#ref-9,#ref-10].
          **Babies' birth injuries.** Covered only as a treatment injury, not under the new maternal birth injury pathway [#ref-9].
          **Long COVID and other infections.** Only covered if work-related gradual process can be shown - very hard during community transmission [#ref-11,#ref-12].
          **Childhood sexual abuse and Loss of Potential Earnings (LOPE).** The boundary moved in 2023 with TN v ACC [#ref-13,#ref-14].
          **Suicide and self-inflicted harm.** Generally excluded unless flowing from a covered injury [#ref-2].

        Three of these contested areas warrant closer examination, because they show the system actually changing.

        ## Case 1: Surgical mesh and the limits of "ordinary consequence"

        Pelvic surgical mesh was offered widely in New Zealand from the early 2000s for stress urinary incontinence and pelvic organ prolapse. The procedure promised to be minimally invasive and durable. It was heavily marketed to surgeons as a standard of care. But for some women - estimates vary from 10 to 30 percent depending on the population - the outcomes were devastating: chronic pain, mesh erosion into surrounding tissues, dyspareunia, recurrent infection, and the need for multiple revision surgeries.

        Between 2005 and 2019, women with mesh complications lodged ACC treatment injury claims. ACC accepted over 1,000 claims but declined 372 [#ref-8]. Common grounds for declining included "no causal link," "no injury," and "ordinary consequence of treatment" - the last reflecting a statutory exclusion that an injury cannot be a treatment injury if it is an ordinary consequence of treatment given the clinical knowledge at the time [#ref-2,#ref-5].

        The problem with "ordinary consequence" is that it depends entirely on what the medical literature says is ordinary. And what the literature says depends on what is published. And what is published depends on whose harms get measured and reported. For many years, mesh complications were underreported in surgical literature - adverse event rates were lower in published studies than in patient registries. Surgeons were publishing their good outcomes; patients were suffering their bad ones in silence.

        In 2019, the Ministry of Health commissioned Victoria University's Diana Unwin Chair in Restorative Justice to run a national listening process. Over 600 mesh-injured people shared their experiences across 32 forums. The resulting report described severe and sustained harm: women who could not work, whose relationships had fractured, who had undergone 5, 10, or more revision surgeries in pursuit of pain relief that never came [#ref-15].

        In October 2020, ACC announced it would reassess every declined surgical mesh claim under updated cover guidance reflecting the latest evidence [#ref-7,#ref-16]. The reassessment process has been lengthy and contentious. Some women have had their claims accepted; others remain in disputes that have stretched over years. But the principle shifted: ACC acknowledged that "ordinary consequence" had been defined too narrowly, and that the published literature had systematically underestimated harm.

        In August 2023, the Director-General of Health imposed a time-limited pause on transvaginal mesh procedures for stress urinary incontinence, contingent on credentialling, structured informed consent, and a procedure registry being in place [#ref-17]. A national Female Pelvic Mesh Service was established to support women with complications [#ref-18].

        The medicolegal lesson is uncomfortable. What counts as an "ordinary consequence" can systematically exclude exactly the people whose injuries the scheme was designed to cover - when those harms are common but under-recognised, or published at lower rates than they actually occur. The restorative justice process was, in effect, the corrective mechanism when the statutory definition failed.

        ## Case 2: Maternal birth injury and the 2022 amendment

        For nearly fifty years, the Accident Compensation Act defined an accident as requiring an external force. A torn perineum during labour did not qualify, because the force came from inside the body - it was the baby's head, your own body, the natural force of contractions. A pelvic organ prolapse from delivery was, in legal terms, the same as one from chronic constipation: a consequence of bodily function, not an injury.

        This created a striking asymmetry. If you were injured during labour by an external force - a clinician's forceps applied negligently, a fetal monitor left in place too long causing a pressure necrosis - that was potentially a treatment injury. But if you were injured by the labour process itself - which is to say, by the normal mechanics of vaginal delivery - you had no cover.

        The result was that birthing parents who needed surgery for obstetric anal sphincter injury, who had obstetric fistula, who experienced uterine rupture, who had significant perineal tears - these people paid for their care privately or went without. A woman who needed pelvic floor physiotherapy for prolapse resulting from delivery had to fund it herself. The broader population's responsibility stopped at the edge of the birth canal.

        The Accident Compensation (Maternal Birth Injury and Other Matters) Amendment Act 2022 came into force on 1 October 2022 [#ref-10,#ref-19]. It added a new limb to the definition of accident in section 25(1)(f): an application of force or resistance internal to the human body, from the onset of labour to the completion of delivery, resulting in an injury listed in the new Schedule 3A [#ref-10,#ref-19]. Schedule 3A contains around twelve injury categories, including anterior, posterior, and uterine prolapse; obstetric anal sphincter injury; obstetric fistula; uterine rupture; pubic ramus fracture; and tears to the perineum, labia, vagina, vulva, clitoris, cervix, rectum, anus, or urethra.

        But three significant exclusions remain, and they matter clinically.

        **First, the change is not retrospective.** A birthing parent injured on 30 September 2022 has the same status as one in 1985: no cover. The select committee process heard from women - some in their 60s and 70s - whose applications to the scheme had been declined decades earlier, who would not benefit from their own submissions. The inequity is stark.

        **Second, episiotomies and caesarean sections are not covered** under the new maternal injury pathway, because they involve clinical incision rather than internal force from labour. They remain potentially claimable as treatment injuries on the usual criteria, but the bar is higher. A woman who had an episiotomy that healed poorly and caused long-term dyspareunia or incontinence has to argue treatment injury, not maternal birth injury - which means arguing that the episiotomy was unnecessary or negligently performed, a more demanding threshold.

        **Third, standalone postpartum mental injury** - severe postpartum depression, birth-trauma PTSD without a listed physical injury - is not covered. Mental injury can be added consequentially to a covered physical injury (e.g., depression following recovery from obstetric fistula), but cannot stand alone. This creates an odd situation: a woman with a perineal tear and secondary depression gets cover for both; a woman with equivalent depression triggered by birth trauma but without a listed physical injury gets no cover.

        ## Case 3: TN v ACC and the date-of-injury rule

        TN was sexually abused from the age of two. Her family prevented her from accessing medical care unaccompanied, so she could not disclose the abuse or seek help before turning 18. Decades later, her abusers were convicted and imprisoned. She lodged an ACC claim for Loss of Potential Earnings, which compensates someone whose earning capacity was permanently reduced by an injury sustained as a child.

        Under ACC's prior interpretation, a LOPE claim arising from childhood sexual abuse required the claimant to have first sought treatment for the resulting mental injury before they turned 18 [#ref-14]. TN couldn't meet that test, through no fault of her own. The abuse itself - and the family control that prevented disclosure - made early treatment impossible. ACC declined her claim.

        In 2023, the New Zealand Court of Appeal handed down TN v ACC, holding that the date of injury for these claims is the date of the abuse itself, not the date of first treatment [#ref-14]. The decision opened LOPE eligibility to a population previously excluded - survivors who, for entirely understandable reasons, were unable to seek help as children.

        The logic is straightforward but profound: you cannot require someone to prove an injury occurred before the age of 18, when the injury itself prevented them from disclosing and getting help until adulthood. To do so is to use the trauma as the basis for excluding the person from compensation.

        ACC estimated that TN, combined with another Court of Appeal decision expanding cover, would add roughly $3.6 billion to its outstanding claim liabilities, contributing to a $7.2 billion annual deficit [#ref-13]. As of mid-2025, around 262 people had been accepted for LOPE under the new approach, totalling approximately $50 million [#ref-13]. Critics - including TN's own lawyer - have argued ACC has been slow to identify and contact eligible claimants, instead waiting for them to come forward [#ref-13].

        The ethical core is subtle. A scheme cannot promise comprehensive entitlement and simultaneously make eligibility contingent on a behaviour that the injury itself prevented. The Court of Appeal didn't expand the scheme; it corrected an interpretation that had narrowed it below what the legislation actually provided.

        ## Case 4: The degeneration/accident boundary in musculoskeletal injury

        A 52-year-old man lifts a box at work - an ordinary movement, one he has done a thousand times. His back gives way. Sharp pain radiates into his leg. He cannot walk. He presents to his GP with acute low back pain and sciatica. Imaging shows a prolapsed disc at L4-5 with nerve root compression.

        So far, straightforward. But the imaging also shows degenerative changes: disc bulging at multiple levels, facet joint hypertrophy, some narrowing of the spinal canal. He lodges an ACC claim. ACC accepts initially, but then requests further investigation. An ACC medical assessor reviews the imaging and writes: "The degenerative changes are longstanding. The prolapse is likely the result of underlying degeneration exacerbated by age, not caused by the lifting incident." ACC declines the claim, arguing the injury was wholly or substantially caused by an underlying condition.

        This is the daily friction point in MSK claims. The statutory test is clear: ACC can decline if injury was wholly or substantially caused by an underlying condition or ageing [#ref-2,#ref-4]. But the clinical reality is murkier. Most people over 40 have degenerative changes on imaging - disc bulges, facet hypertrophy, ligamentous thickening. Most never develop symptoms. The presence of degenerative changes does not mean they caused the injury; it means the spine was already compromised when the injury occurred.

        The dispute often hinges on language. Was the disc "prolapsed" by the lifting, or did it "extrude" on top of pre-existing degenerative changes? Did the lifting "cause" the injury, or did it "precipitate" it in someone who was biomechanically vulnerable? The distinction matters enormously to ACC liability, but it is often not clinically meaningful. From the patient's perspective, they were well enough to work before the lifting incident, and injured afterward. The causal chain seems obvious.

        What makes this contested is that degenerative changes are progressive and inevitable. Everyone's discs degenerate over time. The question ACC asks is: would this person have been injured without the lifting, eventually? If the answer is probably yes - if degenerative changes were going to cause symptoms anyway - then is the lifting truly the cause of injury, or just the timing?

        Courts have wrestled with this. The test is whether the underlying condition was a "substantial" cause, not just a background factor. But "substantial" is not defined. A 2015 Court of Appeal decision (Williams v ACC) clarified that the presence of an underlying condition does not automatically make it a substantial cause - the claimant's degenerative changes need to be shown to have actually contributed to the injury, not merely existed [#ref-22]. But the standard remains contentious.

        The practical effect is that many MSK claims are declined or disputed. A patient with imaging evidence of degenerative change faces an uphill battle, even if the injury was clearly triggered by a specific incident. ACC assessors are incentivised to find that the degenerative changes were "substantial causes," because doing so removes ACC liability. The burden of proof falls on the claimant to show that the degenerative changes were incidental, that their spine was functioning adequately until the injury occurred.

        For GPs, this means the ACC45 form requires careful phrasing. Documenting the acute mechanism of injury, the timing of symptom onset, and the fact that the patient was working without incident before the event all strengthen a claim. Acknowledging degenerative changes on imaging without explaining why they are not substantial causes weakens it. The narrative matters more than the imaging.

        ## Case 5: Work-related gradual process and the causation threshold

        A 48-year-old nurse develops carpal tunnel syndrome. She has worked in nursing for 25 years. The last five years have involved high-acuity ward nursing with frequent IV insertions, blood draws, and patient handling. Her symptoms developed gradually over 18 months: initial pins and needles at night, progressing to daytime numbness, then weakness and grip loss.

        She lodges an ACC claim under the work-related gradual process category [#ref-2]. ACC's criteria are stringent: the injury must be caused by a gradual process arising from the work, the injury must be a recognisable medical condition, and the work must be the significant contributor to the injury [#ref-2].

        On paper, this seems to fit. Carpal tunnel syndrome is a recognisable medical condition. Work-related repetitive strain is a well-established risk factor. But ACC declines, arguing that carpal tunnel syndrome has multiple causes - age, sex, genetics, obesity, thyroid disease, rheumatoid arthritis - and that the claimant cannot demonstrate that work was the "significant" (not merely contributing) cause.

        The dispute centres on a threshold question: what proportion of the injury must work explain in order to be the "significant" cause? Is 50%? 70%? The legislation does not define it. ACC's interpretation tends to be restrictive: unless the claimant can show that work was the predominant cause, the claim is declined.

        The problem intensifies when the condition has multiple risk factors. A person with obesity who develops carpal tunnel syndrome - did work cause it, or did their weight? A person with hypothyroidism - is the thyroid disorder the cause, or the work? A menopausal woman with new-onset carpal tunnel - is it hormonal or occupational? The causation question becomes impossible to answer with certainty, and ACC's threshold test means uncertainty usually favours declining the claim.

        The clinical evidence suggests that occupational repetitive strain is indeed a significant risk factor for carpal tunnel syndrome, that cumulative trauma contributes meaningfully to symptom development, and that reducing exposure (ergonomic modification, job change) often improves symptoms. But translating "significant risk factor" into "significant cause" for an individual claimant is difficult.

        Work-related gradual process claims have low acceptance rates across New Zealand, even for conditions with strong occupational links (repetitive strain injuries, noise-induced hearing loss, occupational dermatitis). The statutory threshold of "significant contributor" creates a high bar, and ACC's tendency to emphasise competing causes rather than weighing their relative importance makes acceptance difficult.

        For GPs, documenting the temporal relationship between work exposure and symptom onset, the specificity of the symptoms to the occupational exposure (e.g., symptoms worse after high-acuity shifts, better during leave), and the response to exposure reduction all strengthen the claim. But the threshold remains high, and many occupational injuries are declined.

        ## The mental injury frontier

        The largest unfinished argument is pure mental injury. Under the current Act, you are covered for mental injury only if it flows from a covered physical injury, arises from a Schedule 3 criminal act (mostly sexual offences), or meets the narrow test for work-related mental injury under section 21B [#ref-2,#ref-3].

        Outside those three windows, nothing. A bystander to a fatal car crash develops PTSD and is unhurt physically - no cover. A parent whose child dies in an accident in front of them - no cover, except for any physical injury they suffer themselves. A nurse off-duty witnesses a violent assault and develops a recognised mental injury - no cover, because the trauma wasn't work-related under s21B. The 2014 High Court decision in Toomey v ACC, involving a self-employed worker who assisted with the Christchurch earthquake recovery and witnessed multiple deaths, illustrated how strained the boundary becomes when interpreted strictly - and how much hinges on employment status [#ref-3,#ref-21].

        There are principled defences for the line where it sits. ACC is an accident scheme, not a general welfare scheme. Mental health support is provided through the public health system. Expanding cover would significantly expand levies. There are also principled critiques. The line between mental and physical incapacity isn't morally meaningful in the way the statute treats it; what matters is whether a person's life has been derailed by something the community arguably has some responsibility for [#ref-3].

        ## The shape of it

        Each contested condition is, at root, a story about who counts and what counts as an injury worth the community's collective backing. The boundaries are not natural facts. They are choices. The scheme's real test is whether it can revise its own choices when the evidence and the courts demand it.

        Surgical mesh and maternal birth injuries are slow, partial answers in the affirmative. TN v ACC is another. The mental injury frontier is the largest open question, and probably the next one.

        For working GPs, the everyday boundaries we push against - the back with concomitant degenerative change, the shoulder we can't quite call traumatic, the treatment injury form for a complication that may or may not be ordinary - are the operational face of these structural choices. The phrasing in our ACC45s does real work. So does the willingness to lodge claims for things that might be declined, because that's how the boundaries get tested. The system is designed to resist change. But the cases that reach the courts, the listening processes that force reassessment, the new legislation that shifts the definitions - those all start with someone, somewhere, saying: "this boundary is wrong."
