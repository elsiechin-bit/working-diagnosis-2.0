## Overview


**Cervical cancer is caused by persistent infection with oncogenic human papillomavirus (HPV).** Nearly all cases (>99%) are HPV-associated; causation established for decades. Preventable via vaccination (pre-exposure) & screening (early detection of precancerous changes).

        #### HPV epidemiology
        
          **Transmission:** Sexually transmitted; peak acquisition age 15–25 years (shortly after sexual debut). ~80% of sexually active people infected at some point in lifetime.
          **Viral persistence & clearance:** Most HPV infections transient (clear within 6–24 months via immune system). Persistent infection (>2–3 years) → risk of malignant transformation. Risk factors for persistence: smoking, immunosuppression, high viral load, certain HPV genotypes (HPV16, HPV18 highest oncogenic risk).
          **Genotypes:** ~200 HPV types identified; ~15 oncogenic. HPV16 & HPV18 most carcinogenic (~70% of cervical cancers). Gardasil 9 vaccine covers 9 genotypes (HPV16, 18, 31, 33, 45, 52, 58, 6, 11).
          **Progression timeline:** Infection → CIN1 (cervical intraepithelial neoplasia grade 1) → CIN2/3 → invasive cancer typically takes 5–15 years (longer than for many other cancers; screening window substantial).
        
        #### NZ cervical cancer incidence & mortality
        
          **Annual incidence:** ~200–250 cases/year (declining over decades due to screening programme; lowest rates globally among developed nations).
          **Mortality:** ~50–70 deaths/year. 5-year survival (2010–2014): 67.4% (variable by stage at diagnosis; stage I ~95%, stage IV ~10%).
          **Age at presentation:** Peak incidence age 40–45 years (after decades of potential HPV exposure); occurs across reproductive age range (teens–postmenopausal).
          **Vaccine impact (emerging):** Cohorts vaccinated age 12–13 starting 2009 now reaching screening age (2025+); cervical cancer incidence expected to decline further (modelling predicts 50–70% reduction by 2040 as vaccinated cohorts mature).
        
        #### Equity & disparities
        
          **Māori & Pacific women:** Higher incidence & mortality than European NZ women (2–3x higher rates in some age groups). Barriers: lower screening uptake, healthcare access, health literacy, limited cultural responsiveness of services.
          **HealthPathways regional variation:** Screening participation rates vary by DHB (50–70% uptake in eligible populations); Māori/Pacific participation particularly lower.
          **Transgender & non-binary individuals:** NCSP now explicitly includes transgender men & non-binary people with cervix (culturally responsive phrasing; use of pronoun-inclusive language; recognition of dysphoria around genital exams).

      💉HPV vaccination & immunisation schedule▾
      
        **Gardasil 9 (recombinant HPV vaccine) is free & recommended for all NZ individuals aged 9–26 years.** Part of National Immunisation Schedule; school-based programme + catch-up availability.

        #### Vaccine details
        
          **Type:** Recombinant, non-infectious (virus-like particles [VLPs]; no live virus). Covers 9 HPV genotypes (16, 18, 31, 33, 45, 52, 58, 6, 11); 6 and 11 cause genital warts.
          **Efficacy (in pre-exposed populations):** ~95–99% protection against vaccine-covered HPV types when given before exposure (ie, in never-sexually-active individuals). Effectiveness in sexually active individuals variable (depends on prior infection status; ~70–90% protection against novel infections).
          **Duration of immunity:** Long-lasting (≥10 years data; likely lifelong, though exact duration still being defined in post-licensure follow-up).
          **Adverse effects:** Mild-to-moderate local reactions (injection site pain, swelling, redness) common; systemic effects (fever, myalgia) less common. No serious safety signals; millions vaccinated globally since 2006.
        
        #### Immunisation schedule (NZ)
        
          **Primary target (school-based):** Year 8 (age ~12–13 years); 2-dose schedule (0, 6–12 months).
          **Catch-up programme:** Free vaccination available to ages 14–26 who missed school programme; can access via GP, sexual health clinics, or pharmacist vaccination service.
          **Eligibility beyond age 26:** Private vaccination available (cost variable ~$150–250 per dose × 2 doses); insurance may cover; not publicly funded. Some guidelines internationally recommend vaccination to age 45 (evidence evolving); individual risk assessment may support vaccination in older unvaccinated, sexually active individuals.
          **Immunocompromised individuals:** Vaccination still recommended; response may be suboptimal; timing relative to immunosuppressive therapy important (discuss with immunology/treating team).
        
        #### Expected impact of vaccination cohorts
        
          **Cohorts vaccinated at age 12–13 (2009+):** Now reaching screening age (2025+). Modelling predicts dramatic reduction in cervical cancer incidence (50–70% by 2040) as high-uptake vaccinated cohorts progress through screening ages. May enable less frequent screening, higher age cutoff, or screening de-escalation in future decades.
          **Herd immunity:** Vaccination also protects males (protection against genital warts, anal cancer, oropharyngeal cancer); sexual partners of vaccinated women benefit; population-level benefits emerge.

      🔬National Cervical Screening Programme: HPV primary testing▾
      
        **HPV primary screening replaced liquid-based cytology (Pap smear) as the primary test in NZ from September 2023.** This represents major shift toward earlier, more sensitive detection of high-risk HPV (the actual cause of cervical cancer).

        #### Eligibility & age range
        
          **Core eligibility:** Age 25–69 years; have cervix (including transgender men, non-binary people); ever been sexually active.
          **Start age 25:** Changed from age 20 in 2019; rationale: peak HPV acquisition in late teens/early 20s; waiting until age 25 avoids over-detection/over-treatment of transient infections that clear spontaneously.
          **End age 69:** Risk negligible after age 70 if prior adequate negative screening.
          **Extension to ages 70–74:** Recommended for women unscreened or inadequately screened (gaps >5 years); one-time screening may identify missed cancers. Beyond age 74: screening not recommended (low risk; higher comorbidity burden).
          **Never-screened or under-screened (any age 25–74):** Eligible for screening; may need more frequent initial screening depending on catch-up protocol.
        
        #### HPV test modality & results
        
          **Sample type:** Vaginal swab (liquid-based cytology [LBC] or self-collected swab). Either provider-collected or patient self-swab (self-collection particularly improves uptake in under-screened populations; ~85–90% sensitivity equivalent to provider collection).
          **HPV test:** Detects DNA from oncogenic HPV types (typically 13–14 carcinogenic types assayed; exact panel varies by laboratory).
          **HPV-positive (any type):** Reflex to genotyping (identify HPV16/18 vs other oncogenic types). HPV16 or HPV18 detected → direct colposcopy referral. Other oncogenic HPV (OHR; types 31, 33, 35, 39, 45, 51, 52, 56, 58, 59, 66, 68) → cytology triage (LBC performed on same specimen).
          **HPV-negative:** Reassuring result; low risk of cancer development over next 5 years. Screening interval 5 years (or 3 years if immunocompromised).
          **Unsatisfactory sample:** Repeat sample required; poor technique, insufficient cells.

        #### Advantages of HPV primary screening vs cytology
        
          **Higher sensitivity:** Detects ~60–70% more cervical cancers/high-grade lesions (CIN2+) than cytology screening (>95% sensitivity vs ~80% for Pap).
          **Longer safe interval:** 5-yearly screening (vs 3-yearly Pap) equally safe; better adherence due to longer interval.
          **Better specificity in older women:** Fewer false-positives in women >30 years (cytology had high false-positive rate in older populations).
          **Improved equity:** Self-collection option reduces barriers (privacy, anxiety, transport); emerging evidence that self-collection particularly improves uptake in under-screened populations (Māori, Pacific, refugee, low-income women).
          **Cost-effectiveness:** Fewer colposcopies overall (lower specificity harms reduced vs cytology-based screening); extended interval reduces per-woman screening burden & healthcare costs.

      🩺Management of abnormal screening results▾
      
        **HPV-positive results require triage & further evaluation.** HPV16/18 highest priority (direct colposcopy); other oncogenic types triaged by cytology.

        #### HPV 16 or 18 detected
        
          **Action:** Referral for colposcopy (ideally within 6–8 weeks; urgent if symptomatic). No cytology triage; HPV16/18 sufficient cancer risk to warrant direct visualization.
          **Rationale:** HPV16 confers ~15–20% 5-year risk of CIN3+ (vs ~1–2% for other oncogenic types); HPV18 associated with adenocarcinomas (harder to detect on colposcopy, higher progression risk).
        
        #### Other oncogenic HPV (OHR) detected
        
          **Reflex cytology:** LBC cytology performed on same specimen; classified per Bethesda System (normal, ASC-US, LSIL, HSIL, AGC, AIS/cancer).
          **Cytology normal or ASC-US:** Return to routine screening (repeat HPV test in 12 months). If HPV negative at 12 months, return to 5-yearly interval.
          **Cytology LSIL:** Manage per guidelines: some recommend repeat HPV/cytology at 12 months; others direct colposcopy depending on risk factors & age (age 
          **Cytology HSIL/AGC/cancer:** Urgent colposcopy referral.
        
        #### Colposcopy & biopsy
        
          **Procedure:** Magnified inspection of cervix under colposcope; application of acetic acid (whitens abnormal areas); biopsy of suspicious lesions. Accuracy ~90–95% in detecting CIN2+.
          **Findings & management:**
            
              **Normal or CIN1:** Typically no treatment; return to screening. CIN1 often regresses spontaneously (60–80% over 2–3 years), especially in younger women.
              **CIN2/3:** Excisional treatment recommended (loop electrosurgical excision procedure [LEEP], cold-knife conization, laser conization). Aim: complete excision with negative margins (reduce recurrence risk). Pathology review essential (confirms diagnosis, assesses margin status).
              **Invasive cancer:** Staging (imaging, labs) and referral to gynaecologic oncology for treatment (surgery ± chemotherapy ± radiation depending on stage).

        #### Test of cure & follow-up after treatment for CIN
        
          **Post-excision (after CIN2/3 treatment):** Repeat HPV testing at 6 months & 18 months ("test of cure"). Both negative → return to routine screening (5-yearly interval). If HPV positive at either timepoint → refer for repeat colposcopy (higher recurrence risk; ~15–20%).
          **Adenocarcinoma in situ (AIS) treated:** Higher recurrence risk (20–30%); usually recommend annual co-testing (HPV + cytology) for ≥25 years post-treatment.

      ⚠️Symptomatic disease & red flags▾
      
        **Symptoms warrant urgent evaluation regardless of recent negative screening.** Cervical cancer can present symptoms between screening intervals.

        #### Presenting symptoms
        
          **Abnormal vaginal bleeding:** Post-coital bleeding (after intercourse); irregular or heavy menses; intermenstrual spotting; post-menopausal bleeding. Most sensitive symptom (~70% of symptomatic presentations).
          **Vaginal discharge:** Bloody, foul-smelling, or purulent discharge. May indicate advanced disease or cervical necrosis.
          **Pelvic pain:** Localised or referral pain; often late sign (advanced disease). Tenesmus (painful defecation) may indicate rectosigmoid involvement.
          **Constitutional symptoms (late):** Unintentional weight loss, fatigue, lower limb edema (pelvic lymph node involvement impacting venous/lymphatic drainage).
        
        #### Evaluation of symptomatic women
        
          **History & examination:** Careful history (duration, severity, associated symptoms). Pelvic examination including speculum (visualise cervix for lesions, bleeding, discharge). Pay attention to bleeding source (cervical, endometrial, other).
          **Presumption of cervical origin warranted if:** Visible cervical lesion, abnormal cervix on exam, or post-coital bleeding + risk factors (immunosuppression, smoking, prolonged smoking exposure, unvaccinated).
          **Cervical samples (despite negative recent screening):** Take LBC sample for HPV & cytology testing even if recent screening negative. Infection/disease may have developed since last test. Urgent processing; communicate clinical concern to laboratory.
          **Referral:** If visible cervical lesion or high clinical suspicion → direct gynecology/colposcopy referral (don't wait for HPV results). Biopsy gold standard for diagnosis.

      💊Treatment & management overview▾
      
        **Cervical cancer treatment depends on stage, histology, and patient factors.** Overview below; detailed treatment protocols managed by gynecologic oncology.

        #### Staging & prognostic factors
        
          **FIGO staging (International Federation of Gynecology & Obstetrics):** Stage I: confined to cervix; II: extends beyond cervix but not to pelvic wall/lower third vagina; III: extends to pelvic wall/lower vagina/regional lymph nodes; IV: invades bladder/rectal mucosa or distant metastasis. Stage affects treatment intensity & prognosis.
          **Histology:** ~80% squamous cell carcinoma (generally better prognosis, more predictable spread); ~15% adenocarcinoma (higher grade, worse prognosis, often diagnosed later); other types rare.
          **Grade:** Influences treatment; high-grade (poorly differentiated) associated with worse outcomes.
        
        #### Precancerous lesions (CIN1, CIN2/3)
        
          **CIN1 (mild dysplasia):** Often regresses spontaneously (60–80% over 2–3 years, especially in younger women); observation with HPV/cytology follow-up often preferred over immediate treatment. Treatment (LEEP, laser, cold knife conization) if persistent, high-grade features, or patient preference.
          **CIN2/3 (moderate-to-severe dysplasia):** High progression risk if untreated (~30–50% over 5 years for CIN3); excisional treatment standard (LEEP, conization). Goal: complete excision, negative margins. Pathology review essential.
          **Recurrence risk post-treatment:** ~15% for CIN2/3 (within 5 years); higher for AIS (~20–30%). Longer-term follow-up (test of cure, then surveillance) necessary.
        
        #### Early-stage invasive cancer (Stage IB–IIA)
        
          **Surgery (radical hysterectomy ± lymph node dissection):** First-line for small tumours (≤4 cm) in young women (better preservation of ovarian/sexual function). Equivalent cure rates to radiotherapy for early-stage disease.
          **Radiotherapy (brachytherapy ± external beam):** Alternative to surgery; particularly for older women, those with comorbidities precluding surgery, or bulkier tumours. Efficacy ~85–90% for stage IB/IIA.
          **Chemotherapy:** Increasingly used in early-stage disease with high-risk features (lymphovascular invasion, parametrial spread, positive margins). Concomitant chemoradiotherapy standard for stages IB2+ (concurrent cisplatin with radiation improves outcomes ~15% vs radiation alone).
        
        #### Advanced-stage cervical cancer (Stage IIB–IV)
        
          **Radiotherapy + chemotherapy (concomitant chemoradiotherapy, CCRT):** Gold standard for locally advanced disease. Weekly cisplatin during course of external beam radiotherapy + brachytherapy. ~15–20% improvement in overall survival vs radiotherapy alone.
          **Palliative chemotherapy:** Recurrent/metastatic disease. Traditional platinum-based regimens (cisplatin/paclitaxel); emerging data on immunotherapy (pembrolizumab, others) in select patients (PD-L1 expression, high tumour burden).
          **Palliative care:** Advanced disease; symptom management (pain, bleeding, discharge, pelvic masses causing urinary/bowel symptoms). Multidisciplinary approach; advance care planning.

        #### Prognosis
        
          **5-year survival by stage (globally):** Stage I: 80–90%; II: 60–75%; III: 30–45%; IV: 5–15%. NZ outcomes generally comparable to developed countries.
          **Early detection impact:** Stage I cancers (detected via screening or early symptomatic diagnosis) have excellent prognosis (~90% 5-year survival); late-stage presentation dramatically worsens outcomes.

      📋GP role & clinical pearls▾
      
        #### Screening counselling & uptake promotion
        
          **Target age 25–69:** Proactive recall/invitation via NCSP. GPs responsible for counsel regarding benefits (prevents ~95% of cervical cancers through early detection) & potential harms (false positives, anxiety, colposcopy procedures, rare treatment complications).
          **Myth-busting:** Address common misconceptions (cervical screening painful/dangerous [false; minor discomfort only], only for sexually active women [false; cervical cancer risk throughout reproductive years], screening replaces contraception [false; separate issues]).
          **Barrier identification & solution:** Ask about barriers (fear, cultural/religious concerns, language, transport, childcare, previous negative experiences). Offer solutions: self-collection option (reduces anxiety, improves access), female provider option if preferred, translated materials, transport assistance via PHO if available.
          **Equity-focused approach:** Māori & Pacific women under-screened; culturally respectful communication essential (use correct te reo Māori terminology if fluent, avoid paternalism, acknowledge historical healthcare inequities, involve whānau in decision-making if requested).
        
        #### HPV vaccination advocacy
        
          **All patients age 9–26:** Offer free HPV vaccination (Gardasil 9) if not yet vaccinated. Emphasise: prevents ~95% of cervical cancers if given before sexual exposure; also protects against genital warts & HPV-related cancers (anal, oropharyngeal).
          **Older patients (27+):** Can access private vaccination; discuss cost-benefit in individual at-risk (unvaccinated, ongoing sexual partner changes, occupational exposure). Some international guidelines now recommend to age 45; individual risk assessment.
          **Immunocompromised:** Vaccination still recommended; discuss timing with treating team; response may be suboptimal but benefit still present.
        
        #### Symptomatic women & urgent referral
        
          **Post-coital bleeding, unusual discharge, pelvic pain:** Don't assume benign. Take cervical sample (HPV/cytology even if recent negative screening); examine cervix carefully. If visible lesion or strong clinical suspicion → direct gynecology referral (don't wait for test results).
          **All-ages consideration:** Cervical cancer can present at any age within screening range (or beyond in unscreened populations). Maintain suspicion even in "young" women with post-coital bleeding.
        
        #### Management of abnormal results & patient communication
        
          **HPV-negative results:** Reassure patient (low cancer risk over next 5 years); no urgent follow-up; schedule 5-yearly screening. Clear communication reduces anxiety.
          **HPV-positive (requires colposcopy):** Inform patient promptly; explain rationale (finding abnormal cells before cancer develops). Emphasise: colposcopy is visual inspection (not painful; minor discomfort possible), majority will have normal colposcopy or minor changes. Arrange referral promptly; provide colposcopy information sheet.
          **CIN2/3 diagnosed (requires excisional treatment):** Counsel re: treatment options (LEEP, conization); explain risks (infection, bleeding, rare uterine perforation, very rare cervical stenosis). Emphasise: early treatment highly effective (~95% cure); follow-up test of cure essential. Discuss any fertility concerns.
          **Shared decision-making:** Where options exist (CIN1 observation vs treatment; some LSIL management pathways), involve patient in decision; discuss her preferences, concerns, ability to adhere to follow-up.
        
        #### Follow-up coordination
        
          **Colposcopy referral:** Ensure prompt referral (2-week urgent pathway for abnormal HPV results); follow up on colposcopy results; ensure patient aware of findings & next steps.
          **Post-treatment surveillance:** Patient referred to gynecology for test-of-cure protocol; GP role: ensure patient attends, coordinates care, monitors for complications or persistent symptoms.
          **Long-term follow-up:** After successful treatment of CIN, patient returns to routine NCSP screening (5-yearly HPV) once test of cure negative; GP ensures re-entry into screening pathway & adherence to intervals.
        
        #### Clinical pearls
        
          **HPV primary screening is now standard:** Familiarise yourself with new protocol; explain to patients that HPV (virus causing cancer) now targeted rather than Pap cytology (cell changes).
          **Self-collection option available:** Encourage in under-screened populations (equity lever); patient comfort & uptake often improve with self-swab.
          **Early vaccination dramatically reduces future cervical cancer burden:** Vaccinate all age-eligible patients; generational impact profound (modelling predicts 70% reduction in cervical cancer by 2040 in fully vaccinated cohorts).
          **5-year screening interval (HPV negative) safe & improves adherence:** Explain to patients; longer interval doesn't mean lower risk if HPV negative.
