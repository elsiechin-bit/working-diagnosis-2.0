## Overview


**Parasomnias are undesired physical events or experiences occurring during sleep or sleep–wake transitions,** without EEG evidence of full seizure activity or daytime cognitive impairment (distinguishing them from nocturnal seizures or somnambulism with injury). Classification per ICD-11 & DSM-5 divides parasomnias into three domains:

        #### NREM arousal parasomnias (disorders of arousal; confusional arousals)
        
          **Sleep terrors (pavor nocturnus):** Abrupt arousal from NREM (typically N3/deep sleep), accompanied by intense autonomic discharge (heart rate ↑↑, respiration laboured, diaphoresis), vocalisations (screaming, crying), and apparent panic. Patient appears conscious but remains amnestic post-episode. Safety risk if patient leaves bed or becomes aggressive.
          **Sleepwalking (somnambulism):** Complex motor behaviour (walking, eating, dressing, sometimes violent gestures) during partial arousal from NREM deep sleep, with preserved ability to navigate environment. Amnesia on awakening; can persist minutes to hours.
          **Confusional arousals:** Prolonged confusion or disorientation upon awakening (natural or from external stimuli), with automatic behaviours (picking at clothes, slow speech, incoherence). Common in young children after partial arousal, less dramatic than sleep terrors or sleepwalking.
        
        #### REM sleep behaviour disorder (RBD)
        
          **Loss of REM muscle atonia:** During REM sleep, skeletal muscles are normally atonic (paralysed); brainstem cholinergic/aminergic systems enforce this. RBD involves failure of atonia, allowing patient to act out dream content—violent punching, kicking, running—without awareness.
          **Clinical significance:** RBD strongly associated with future neurodegenerative disease (Parkinson's disease, dementia with Lewy bodies, multiple system atrophy); idiopathic RBD progresses to synucleinopathy in ~80% within 10–14 years.
        
        #### Sleep-related movement disorders (brief mention)
        
          - Periodic leg movements, REM sleep laughter/groaning, sleep-related hypoventilation—less dramatic but clinically relevant in older adults.
        
        **Pathophysiology:** NREM arousal parasomnias result from dissociated sleep states—partial arousal where motor centres activate while consciousness remains suppressed. Genetic predisposition evident (high familial clustering); environmental triggers (sleep deprivation, stress, fever, upper airway obstruction, sleep apnoea) lower arousal threshold. RBD involves loss of inhibitory pontine cholinergic input to motor nuclei, sparing sensorimotor pathways.

      👶Infancy & early childhood (0–5 years)▾
      
        #### Developmental context
        Infants & toddlers cycle through sleep–wake states frequently; NREM N3 (deep sleep) comprises ~30% of sleep in infants, declining slightly by age 3–5. Arousals & partial awakenings common & developmentally normal. Parasomnias in this age group are predominantly confusional arousals & isolated sleep terrors, reflecting immature arousal regulation.

        #### Confusional arousals & sleep talking
        
          **Presentation:** Child partially awakens, often following parental intervention (diaper change, noise) or spontaneously during transition from deep NREM. Appears confused, may cry or whimper, sometimes picks at clothing or makes repetitive movements. Lasts minutes to half-hour; memory absent next morning.
          **Incidence:** ~15–20% of children age 2–5 years experience occasional confusional arousals.
          **Triggers:** Sleep deprivation, illness/fever, medications, irregular sleep schedule.
          **Distinction from seizure:** No tongue biting, incontinence, or post-event fatigue/confusion; EEG (if done) shows arousal pattern, not seizure discharge.
          **Management:** Reassurance (benign, self-limited); optimise sleep (adequate hours, consistent schedule); avoid triggers. No pharmacotherapy indicated. Ensure bedroom safety if child gets up.
        
        #### Sleep terrors
        
          **Presentation:** Abrupt awakening 1–3 hours into sleep (during first NREM cycle N3 predominance), with piercing scream or cry. Intense autonomic features: rapid heart rate, diaphoresis, pupil dilation. Child appears terrified but is physically inaccessible to comfort; attempts to console often escalate distress. Episode lasts 5–20 minutes; child returns to sleep, typically amnestic.
          **Incidence:** ~3–8% of children; more common age 2–8 years (peak 4–6 years).
          **Family history:** Highly familial (60–80% have first-degree relative with parasomnia history).
          **Triggers:** Fever/acute illness (classically precipitates first episodes), sleep deprivation, stress, CNS medications (stimulants, some antihistamines).
          **Distinction from nightmare:** Nightmares occur in REM (latter sleep cycles, early morning), child fully arousable & can recall dream. Sleep terrors in NREM (early night), child unresponsive during episode, amnestic after.
          **Management:** Parental reassurance (self-limited, benign prognosis). Safety precautions (secure windows, remove hazards near bed). Optimise sleep hygiene & fever management. Medication (benzodiazepines, tricyclic antidepressants) reserved for severe, frequent episodes disrupting family sleep or causing daytime fatigue. Scheduled awakening 15–30 min before usual episode time may interrupt cycle.
        
        #### Sleepwalking (somnambulism)
        
          **Presentation:** Child gets out of bed, walks around house or room with eyes open but vacant expression. May attempt complex tasks (opening cupboards, trying to leave house, occasionally toileting in inappropriate places). Usually coordinated enough to navigate obstacles. Difficult to arouse; if awakened, confused & disoriented.
          **Incidence:** ~10–15% of children have ≥1 sleepwalking episode; persistent somnambulism ~1–5% of childhood population.
          **Timing:** Usually first 1–3 hours of sleep (NREM N3-dominant cycles); may recur multiple times per night.
          **Triggers:** Sleep deprivation, irregular sleep schedule, stress, fever, sleep apnoea (if present).
          **Safety risk:** Generally low in early childhood (weak motor control limits distance wandering); increases with age/strength. Rarely exits house or injures self; injuries usually minor (bumping furniture).
          **Management:** Parental reassurance & safety measures. Ensure bedroom safety, lock doors/windows, clear floor of hazards. Consistent sleep schedule, adequate sleep. Avoid triggers (illness, sleep deprivation). Avoid forcibly awakening (can worsen confusion, cause aggression). Gently guide back to bed if safe. Pharmacotherapy (benzodiazepines, imipramine) rarely indicated in young children; considered only if frequent, injurious episodes or severe disruption to family.

      🧒Childhood & early adolescence (5–13 years)▾
      
        **Peak incidence of NREM arousal parasomnias occurs in this decade.** Sleep architecture stabilises; NREM N3 remains 15–20% of sleep. Parasomnias become more complex & potentially hazardous as motor control improves.

        #### Sleep terrors & sleepwalking: consolidation phase
        
          **Epidemiology:** Sleep terrors peak age 5–8 years; persistence past age 8 associated with greater family history loading. Sleepwalking peak 8–12 years; frequency & complexity increase.
          **Clinical features expand:** Sleep terrors may include semi-purposeful escape behaviours (running, trying to leave house). Sleepwalking episodes lengthen; patient may perform intricate tasks (cooking, dressing, leaving house if doors unsecured) with coordinated gait.
          **Safety risk elevates:** Risk of injury increases with size, strength, speed, and household environment. Rare but documented: sleepwalker exits window, scales dangerous structures, or engages violent behaviour (hitting, kicking sibling/parent who intervenes).
          **Triggers amplified:** School stress, sports injury, sleep deprivation (homework, extracurriculars), fever, sleep-disordered breathing (enlarged adenoids/tonsils).
          **Management escalation:** Sleep deprivation & stress reduction paramount. Screen for sleep-disordered breathing (snoring, witnessed apnoeas, witnessed gasps during sleep, daytime somnolence)—if present, adenotonsillar hypertrophy or obstructive sleep apnoea must be evaluated & treated as parasomnia trigger. Safety measures: secure windows/doors, remove weapons/hazards from bedroom, consider baby monitor (auditory alert if child leaves bed), or door alarm. Medication (benzodiazepines: clonazepam 0.25–0.5 mg HS; tricyclics: imipramine 0.5–1 mg/kg HS) reserved for frequent, injurious episodes. Imipramine may carry anticholinergic side-effects & overdose risk; clonazepam carries dependence risk. Scheduled awakenings effective but labour-intensive (wake child 15–30 min before usual episode time, keep aroused 5 min, 4–6 weeks duration). Hypnosis & stress counselling anecdotal benefit.
        
        #### Nightmare disorder & REM parasomnias
        
          **Nightmares (vs night terrors):** Vivid, dysphoric dreams occurring in REM (latter sleep cycles, often early morning). Child fully arousable, recalls dream content vividly. Post-episode alert but emotionally distressed. Common following trauma, stress, or scary media exposure.
          **REM sleep behaviour disorder (rare in childhood):** RBD in this age group is rare (typically adult/older adult condition); when present, investigate for underlying neurological disorder, medication effect (SSRI-induced in rare cases), or narcolepsy-cataplexy (which disrupts REM regulation). Sleep study with full polysomnography warranted.
        
        #### Psychoeducation & parental counselling
        
          - Explain dissociated sleep state & genetic predisposition. Reassure parents that parasomnias are self-limited (often resolve by adolescence), that children are not conscious/misbehaving, and that forgiving/punishing the child is counterproductive. Screen for parental guilt ("caused by screen time, sugar, etc.") & correct misconceptions.

      👤Adolescence & young adulthood (13–35 years)▾
      
        #### Natural history & remission
        **Most childhood NREM arousal parasomnias remit by late childhood or early adolescence.** Persistence into adulthood (>5% of population with ongoing NREM parasomnias) signals either genetic predisposition, unresolved triggers, or unmasking of underlying sleep disorder (e.g., sleep apnoea).

        #### Persistent or recurrent NREM parasomnias
        
          **Adolescent-onset or exacerbation:** Stress (academic pressure, occupational changes, relationship conflict), sleep deprivation (common in adolescents; shift work in young adults), alcohol use (disinhibits arousal thresholds), medications (stimulants, some antidepressants).
          **Sleepwalking in young adulthood:** Episodes may persist or recur. Risks shift: young adult may drive car, operate machinery, or engage in sexual behaviour (rare but documented sexsomnia: inappropriate sexual acts during sleep, potential legal/relationship consequences). Occupational implications for military, police, pilots, or healthcare workers require case-by-case assessment.
          **Management:** Lifestyle optimisation (sleep hygiene, stress management, substance avoidance). Sleep study (polysomnography) indicated if diagnostic uncertainty, if NREM parasomnias coincide with daytime somnolence (suggesting comorbid sleep apnoea), or if episodes are violent/injurious. Pharmacotherapy (clonazepam 0.5–1 mg HS, paroxetine) considered for frequent, disruptive episodes. Cognitive-behavioural therapy for underlying anxiety/stress.
        
        #### REM sleep behaviour disorder: emergence
        
          **Idiopathic RBD onset:** RBD can present in adulthood (mean age ~50 years for males; females later ~60s). Earlier presentation (20s–40s) should prompt investigation for secondary cause: narcolepsy-cataplexy, medication effect (antidepressants, particularly SSRIs & SNRIs, in ~5% of treated individuals; sympathomimetics; anticholinergics), or neurological disease.
          **Clinical presentation:** Patient or bed partner reports violent dreams—punching, kicking, leaping from bed—during sleep. Partner may report being injured (bruised, kicked). Patient often recalled dream content (contrary to NREM parasomnias). No recollection of acting out in morning; bed often dishevelled, patient may report arm/leg soreness.
          **Diagnostic workup:** Polysomnography with video recording confirms loss of REM atonia + motor activity corresponding to REM phase. Sleep study also screens for sleep apnoea, periodic leg movements, narcolepsy. Neurological examination to exclude tremor, rigidity, hypokinesia, or other Parkinson-like features. Consider MRI brain if clinical suspicion for structural lesion (stroke, neoplasm).
          **Management:** Counsel on injury risk to self & bed partner. Secure bedroom (remove breakables, move bed away from window, padding). Bed partner may require temporary sleep relocation if injury risk high. Clonazepam (0.5–2 mg HS) highly effective (~90% efficacy in reducing RBD episodes); mechanism unclear (possibly improves sleep stability or modulates brainstem). Melatonin (3–12 mg HS) emerging alternative with good evidence in several case series (safety profile excellent). Avoid REM-suppressing agents (tricyclics, SSRIs) unless necessary for mood disorder; paradoxically can worsen RBD in some patients. Follow-up neurology referral & periodic neuropsychological screening for prodromal Parkinson's features (olfactory loss, constipation, orthostatic hypotension, mood changes, rapid eye movement density).
        
        #### Occupational & legal implications
        
          **Driving & machinery operation:** Persistent parasomnias (especially sleepwalking with confusional microarousals) may impair fitness to drive if episodes occur during/around sleep periods. Case-by-case assessment; some jurisdictions require formal driving assessment.
          **Sexsomnia:** Rare variant of somnambulism; sexual advances or intercourse without consent during sleep. Legal implications complex; diagnosis via polysomnography + witnessed history essential. Medical/legal team consultation recommended.

      👴Later adulthood & older age (>65 years)▾
      
        #### Shift in parasomnia epidemiology
        **NREM arousal parasomnias (sleep terrors, sleepwalking) diminish with age,** but new parasomnias emerge: REM sleep behaviour disorder increases steeply (affecting 0.5–1% of older adults; up to 10% in Parkinson's disease), sleep-related hypoventilation, and complex sleep-related behaviours. Sleep architecture changes: NREM N3 reduces dramatically (often **Epidemiology:** RBD prevalence increases with age; mean age presentation ~60 years. Male predominance 2:1. Strong correlation with neurodegenerative disease: ~80% of idiopathic RBD patients develop Parkinson's, dementia with Lewy bodies, or multiple system atrophy within 10–14 years of RBD onset (some earlier).
          **Clinical phenotype in older adults:** Episodes may be less violent (weaker motor control) but more frequent (fragmented sleep increases REM intrusion risk). Patient reports repeated arousals, vivid nightmares, sometimes acting out dreams. Bed partner crucial historian (patient often unaware of movement severity).
          **Prodromal markers for neurodegeneration:** Watch for hyposmia (olfactory loss; 90% of Parkinson's), constipation (80%, precedes motor symptoms by years), mood changes (depression, anxiety), orthostatic hypotension, or subtle tremor/rigidity. RBD is one of earliest markers of synucleinopathy.
          **Polysomnography & neuroimaging:** Sleep study essential (confirms REM atonia loss, screens for sleep apnoea, periodic leg movements). Brain MRI to exclude structural pathology. Neurology referral & baseline neuropsychological assessment indicated. Dopamine transporter scan (DaTscan) if diagnostic uncertainty between Parkinson's & other parkinsonian syndromes; helpful if subtle motor signs present.
          **Management:** Clonazepam (0.5–2 mg HS) first-line, effective in majority. Melatonin (10–15 mg HS) reasonable alternative, particularly if clonazepam contraindicated (fall risk, cognitive effects, drug interactions). Combination clonazepam + melatonin sometimes necessary. Consider IsoJohnson sodium oxybate (Xyrem) if clonazepam ineffective or contraindicated; used off-label for RBD (anecdotal reports; not standard in NZ). Avoid REM-suppressing antidepressants unless treating comorbid depression (may paradoxically worsen RBD). Safety precautions remain essential: bedroom modifications, bed partner relocation if injury risk high.
          **Neurology co-management:** Monitor for emergent parkinsonian features; neuropsychology testing for cognitive changes. Shared decision-making around medication burden & prognosis.
        
        #### Sleep-related hypoventilation & complex sleep apnoea
        
          **Prevalence:** Sleep-disordered breathing increases with age; comorbidity with RBD increases injury risk (periodic arousals + REM muscle atonia loss = paradoxical sleep-related apnoea).
          **Clinical implication:** Older adult presenting with new sleep-related movement or behavioural change warrants sleep study to screen for multiple simultaneous sleep disorders.
        
        #### Secondary parasomnias in older age
        
          **Medication-induced:** Sedative-hypnotics (benzodiazepines, Z-drugs), anticholinergics, steroids, some antidepressants can trigger or exacerbate arousal parasomnias & complex sleep behaviours. Deprescribing & substitution warranted if feasible.
          **Delirium & sleep-related confusion:** Acute illness, infection (UTI, pneumonia), medications (anticholinergics, opioids) may cause confusion, agitation, or complex behaviours during sleep that superficially resemble parasomnias. Distinguish by timeline (acute onset vs lifelong history), diurnal pattern (delirium typically worse evenings/nights but present all day), and resolution with treatment of underlying condition.
          **Nocturnal seizures:** Older adults at higher risk of seizure disorders (stroke, dementia, tumours). Seizures may present as nocturnal stereotyped behaviours (tongue biting, incontinence, post-event fatigue/confusion, EEG abnormality). Video-EEG telemetry indicated if high suspicion; clonazepam (often first-line for RBD anyway) would provide seizure prophylaxis, but anti-seizure medication specific to seizure type may be necessary.
        
        #### Psychosocial & safety considerations
        
          **Injury prevention:** Fall risk elevated; bedroom modifications paramount (clear path to bathroom, night light, remove trip hazards, bed rails if needed). Bed partner safety paramount. Consider in-home sleep assessment or supervised sleep setting (respite care) if injury risk extreme.
          **Driving & independence:** Assess fitness to drive; parasomnias + sleep apnoea + medications (clonazepam) may impair alertness. Discuss candidly; involve family in decision-making.
          **Prognosis discussion:** RBD in older adult associated with future cognitive decline & neurological disease. Counsel patient & family on likely trajectory, symptom surveillance, and importance of neurology follow-up.

      🔍Diagnostic approach & differential diagnosis▾
      
        #### Clinical history & red flags
        
          **Age of onset:** Childhood onset (peak 2–12 years) suggests NREM arousal disorder or nightmare disorder. Adult or late-life onset RBD warrants investigation for secondary cause (medication, neurological disease).
          **Timing within sleep cycle:** NREM parasomnias cluster in first 1–3 hours (N3-dominant); REM parasomnias later in night/early morning (REM density increases). Ask about timing relative to sleep onset & typical sleep duration.
          **Autonomic features:** Sleep terrors → intense ANS activation (heart pounding, sweating, dilated pupils). RBD → minimal ANS change; focus on movement/dream-acting.
          **Memory:** NREM arousal parasomnias → complete or near-complete amnesia for episode. RBD → often vivid dream recall. Nightmares → full recall with clear narrative.
          **Witness account:** Bed partner/family crucial; ask specifically about violence, injury, complexity of motor behaviour, vocalisation, response to intervention.
          **Red flags for seizure rather than parasomnia:** Stereotyped repetitive behaviour (lip smacking, hand automatisms), tongue biting, incontinence, prolonged post-event confusion/fatigue, recent head injury, family hx of epilepsy, other seizure-like events during waking.
          **Red flags for secondary RBD:** Adult/older adult with new RBD; current SSRI/SNRI use; prior narcolepsy diagnosis; tremor/rigidity/hypokinesia on examination; anosmia; orthostatic hypotension; REM sleep latency 
        
        #### Polysomnography (PSG) indications
        
          **Indicated:** Suspected RBD (definitive diagnosis requires loss of REM atonia + motor activity in REM stage); diagnostic uncertainty (parasomnias vs seizures vs obstructive sleep apnoea); daytime somnolence accompanying parasomnias (suggests comorbid sleep-disordered breathing); violent/injurious parasomnias; new onset in adulthood or older age; any change in character/frequency of longstanding parasomnia.
          **Not always necessary:** Straightforward childhood sleep terrors/sleepwalking with clear history, strong family history, no red flags, and normal examination can often be managed clinically without PSG. However, if diagnostic uncertainty, comorbid daytime symptoms, or failure to respond to standard interventions, proceed to PSG.
          **PSG features:** Full montage (EEG, EOG, chin EMG, bilateral anterior tibialis EMG, respiratory effort, oxygen saturation, cardiac rate). Video recording essential for RBD & seizure differentiation. Scoring per AASM criteria: REM atonia loss (chin EMG or limb EMG activity during REM sleep ≥50% of REM duration); sleep architecture (assess N3 quantity); arousals & periodic events (sleep apnoea, periodic leg movements).

      💊Management principles & pharmacotherapy▾
      
        #### Non-pharmacological first-line
        
          **Sleep hygiene & safety:** Consistent sleep schedule, adequate sleep duration (7–9 hours adults; age-adjusted for children), regular exercise, avoidance of stimulants/alcohol near bedtime. Bedroom environment: dark, quiet, cool. Remove hazards; secure windows/doors; night lighting.
          **Trigger avoidance:** Identify & manage sleep deprivation, stress, fever, medications (stimulants, some antidepressants), alcohol (lowers arousal threshold). Sleep apnoea screening; if present, treat urgently (CPAP, surgical).
          **Stress management & psychological therapy:** Cognitive-behavioural therapy for underlying anxiety/PTSD (if nightmares predominate). Mindfulness, relaxation techniques. Referral to sleep psychologist if available.
          **Scheduled awakenings (NREM parasomnias):** Anticipatory awakening 15–30 min before typical episode time; maintain arousal 5 minutes; repeat nightly for 4–6 weeks. Effective in some; labour-intensive; less commonly used now.
        
        #### Pharmacotherapy: NREM arousal parasomnias
        
          **Benzodiazepines (clonazepam, diazepam):** First-line medication. Clonazepam 0.25–0.5 mg HS (children); 0.5–1 mg HS (adults); up to 2 mg HS if needed. Mechanism unclear (possibly stabilises sleep, raises arousal threshold). Efficacy 60–90% reduction in episodes. Onset within 3–7 days. Concerns: dependence risk with long-term use; daytime somnolence/cognitive effects; fall risk in older adults. Avoid abrupt withdrawal (rebound insomnia, seizures if high dose). Taper slowly over weeks-months.
          **Tricyclic antidepressants (imipramine, amitriptyline):** Imipramine 0.5–1 mg/kg HS (children); 10–75 mg HS (adults). REM-suppressing effect may reduce dreaming/arousals. Efficacy reported in some studies; less predictable than clonazepam. Anticholinergic side-effects (dry mouth, constipation, urinary retention); weight gain; cardiac conduction effects (QTc prolongation if overdose/toxicity). Overdose risk (esp. children); requires monitoring. Use with caution if cardiac history.
          **SSRIs/SNRIs (paroxetine, fluoxetine, venlafaxine):** Anecdotal efficacy; less studied than clonazepam/tricyclics. May worsen parasomnias in some individuals (REM-related arousal). Reserved for comorbid mood disorder + parasomnias. Monitor for paradoxical worsening.
          **Melatonin & melatonin agonists:** Melatonin (3–10 mg HS) emerging evidence for reducing NREM parasomnia frequency; excellent side-effect profile. Ramelteon (melatonin MT1/MT2 agonist) limited NZ availability. May be useful adjunct; not yet standard first-line but increasingly used off-label.
        
        #### Pharmacotherapy: REM sleep behaviour disorder
        
          **Clonazepam (gold standard):** Clonazepam 0.5–2 mg HS. Efficacy ~90% in reducing motor activity & injury risk. Mechanism unknown (possibly improves REM sleep stability or reduces phasic REM activity). Concerns as above (dependence, fall risk). Regular monitoring (vital signs, cognitive screen) in older adults.
          **Melatonin (emerging first-line alternative):** Melatonin 3–15 mg HS. Growing evidence (multiple case series, small RCTs) supporting efficacy (~60–80% reduction in RBD episodes). Excellent safety profile; no dependence risk; minimal side-effects. May be preferred for older adults or those with medication sensitivities. Can combine with clonazepam if inadequate response. Off-label use; requires informed consent & baseline/follow-up assessment.
          **Avoid REM-suppressing antidepressants:** SSRIs/SNRIs may paradoxically worsen RBD in some (increased dream intensity, fragmented REM). If depression comorbid, selective agents with less REM effect (mirtazapine, bupropion) preferred; clonazepam/melatonin as primary RBD therapy.
          **Sodium oxybate (Xyrem):** GHB precursor; consolidates sleep, reduces REM fragmentation. Anecdotal reports of RBD improvement; not standard practice in NZ; schedule III controlled drug; compliance monitoring essential. Reserved for refractory cases.
        
        #### Medication monitoring & long-term management
        
          **Efficacy assessment:** Reassess after 2–4 weeks (diary of episode frequency/severity; bed partner feedback). If adequate response (≥50% reduction in episodes, improved safety, no adverse effects), continue 3–6 months, then reassess for deprescribing (parasomnias often recur when medications stopped; low-dose continuation often necessary).
          **Tolerance development:** Some patients report waning efficacy over months-years; dose escalation or medication switch may be necessary.
          **Drug interactions:** Clonazepam + CNS depressants (opioids, alcohol, other sedatives) increase overdose risk. SSRIs may inhibit metabolism of tricyclics. Always check interactions in older patients on multiple medications.
          **RBD-specific monitoring:** Older adults on clonazepam require periodic cognitive assessment (MMSE, MoCA) & gait evaluation (fall risk). Neurology co-management essential for prodromal neurodegenerative disease screening. If Parkinson's emerges, dopaminergic agents may complicate RBD management (sometimes improve RBD; sometimes worsen it); coordinated care necessary.

      ⚠️Clinical pearls & pitfalls▾
      
        #### Pearls
        
          **Family history is the rule in NREM parasomnias.** Always ask about parasomnias in first-degree relatives; high heritability reassures families that this is not behavioural/traumatic in origin.
          **Sleep deprivation is a powerful trigger.** Parents & adolescents often don't link inadequate sleep to parasomnia exacerbation. Optimising sleep duration (and consistency) frequently resolves or minimises episodes.
          **Fever precipitates first episodes.** Classic presentation: child with viral illness develops first sleep terror episode night of fever. Reassures family (time-limited, benign condition) & guides management (fever treatment, comfort).
          **Sleep terrors are not nightmares.** Enormous parental confusion on this point. Sleep terrors = NREM arousal (early night, intense ANS, amnestic, impossible to console during episode). Nightmares = REM dream (later night, full recall, alert after). Different etiologies, management, prognosis.
          **RBD is a neurological red flag.** Any adult/older adult with new RBD warrants investigation for secondary cause & neurology referral. Idiopathic RBD is a known prodrome of Parkinson's & synucleinopathies; patient counselling on prodromal symptoms (hyposmia, constipation, mood changes, orthostasis) & periodic neurology surveillance essential.
          **Sleepwalking + daytime somnolence warrants sleep study.** Combination suggests comorbid obstructive sleep apnoea or narcolepsy; untreated sleep apnoea perpetuates arousal parasomnias via fragmentation.
          **Medication review essential in older adults with new parasomnias.** Anticholinergics, stimulants, steroids, some antidepressants can trigger arousals. Deprescribing often resolves symptoms.
        
        #### Pitfalls
        
          **Misdiagnosing seizure as parasomnia (or vice versa).** Stereotyped automatisms, tongue biting, post-event drowsiness, or EEG abnormality favour seizure. Polysomnography with video & full EEG montage clarifies; when in doubt, refer to neurology.
          **Overlooking obstructive sleep apnoea.** OSA exacerbates arousals; CPAP treatment sometimes resolves parasomnias entirely. Screen all parasomnia patients for snoring, witnessed apnoeas, daytime somnolence, witnessed gasps.
          **Over-reassuring families in dangerous parasomnia.** While most NREM parasomnias are self-limited & benign, rare cases involve violent injury (leaving house, driving car during sleepwalking). Honest safety assessment & bedroom modifications essential.
          **Failing to monitor older adults on clonazepam.** Fall risk, cognitive decline, dependency issues. Regular assessment needed; consider deprescribing trials if injury risk resolves or alternative available.
          **Missing medication-induced RBD.** SSRI/SNRI use can provoke or worsen RBD in susceptible individuals. Medication review & potential substitution (e.g., mirtazapine, bupropion, or non-pharmacological mood intervention) warranted before assuming idiopathic RBD.
          **Attributing RBD behavioural change to dementia or delirium without polysomnography.** RBD may precede cognitive decline; polysomnography differentiates RBD from sleep-related hypoventilation, apnoea, or seizures that might be contributing to confusion.
