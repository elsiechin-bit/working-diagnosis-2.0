#### How sleep disorders are grouped (ICSD-3)
        The International Classification of Sleep Disorders groups problems by their dominant feature rather than by cause. In primary care the practical categories are:

          **Insomnia:** difficulty falling or staying asleep, or early waking, with daytime consequences
          **Sleep-related breathing disorders:** obstructive and central sleep apnoea
          **Circadian rhythm sleep-wake disorders:** the clock is misaligned with the desired schedule
          **Central disorders of hypersomnolence:** excessive daytime sleepiness despite adequate opportunity (e.g. narcolepsy)
          **Sleep-related movement disorders:** restless legs syndrome and periodic limb movement disorder
          **Parasomnias:** unwanted events during sleep or arousals (sleepwalking, night terrors, REM sleep behaviour disorder)

        #### The three questions that orient the assessment
        
          **Can't sleep?** Points toward insomnia or a circadian disorder
          **Too sleepy?** Points toward sleep apnoea, insufficient sleep, or hypersomnolence
          **Odd things at night?** Points toward a parasomnia or movement disorder

        #### Core history
        
          Sleep schedule, latency, awakenings, total sleep time; a 1-2 week **sleep diary** is invaluable
          - Snoring, witnessed apnoeas, choking arousals (bed-partner account)
          - Daytime sleepiness (Epworth Sleepiness Scale), naps, mood, caffeine/alcohol, shift work
          - Medications and substances, comorbid pain, mental-health and respiratory conditions

          Sleep hygiene underpins everything
          Consistent wake time, a wind-down routine, a cool dark bedroom, limiting screens, caffeine and alcohol, and using the bed only for sleep are foundational across nearly every sleep disorder - but on their own they rarely fix established chronic insomnia.

      🌙Insomnia disorder▾
      
        #### Definition
        **Chronic insomnia disorder** is dissatisfaction with sleep quantity or quality - difficulty initiating sleep, maintaining sleep, or early-morning waking - with associated daytime impairment, occurring at least 3 nights per week for at least 3 months, despite adequate opportunity to sleep.

        #### Common drivers
        
          **Perpetuating factors:** conditioned arousal, irregular schedules, excessive time in bed, clock-watching, daytime napping
          **Comorbidity:** depression, anxiety, chronic pain, RLS, OSA, alcohol and stimulant use
          **Medications:** stimulants, some antidepressants, corticosteroids, decongestants, beta-agonists

        #### Management - CBT-I first
        
          **CBT-I is first-line:** stimulus control, sleep restriction (consolidation), cognitive restructuring, relaxation and sleep-hygiene work; effective and durable, available in person or via digital programmes
          **Pharmacotherapy is adjunctive and short-term:** reserve hypnotics for short courses; avoid routine long-term use

          OptionNotes
          
            CBT-IFirst-line; superior long-term outcomes; no pharmacological harms
            Z-drugs / short-acting benzodiazepinesShort-term only; tolerance, dependence, falls in the elderly, next-day impairment
            Sedating antidepressants (e.g. low-dose amitriptyline, mirtazapine)Used where comorbid mood symptoms; off-label for insomnia; weigh side-effects
            Melatonin (prolonged-release)Option in adults over 55 and in circadian misalignment; modest effect

          Avoid the hypnotic habit
          Hypnotics treat the symptom, not the cause. Long-term use risks tolerance, dependence, rebound insomnia, falls and cognitive impairment - particularly in older adults. Steer toward CBT-I and deprescribe where possible.

      😴Obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA)▾
      
        #### What happens
        **OSA** is repetitive collapse of the upper airway during sleep, causing apnoeas and hypopnoeas with oxygen desaturation and arousals. The result is fragmented, non-restorative sleep and daytime sleepiness, with cardiovascular and metabolic consequences over time.

        #### Suggestive features
        
          - Loud habitual snoring, witnessed apnoeas, gasping or choking arousals
          - Excessive daytime sleepiness, morning headache, unrefreshing sleep, nocturia
          - Risk factors: obesity, large neck circumference, male sex, increasing age, retrognathia, alcohol and sedatives
          - Associations: resistant hypertension, atrial fibrillation, type 2 diabetes, stroke

        #### Screening and diagnosis
        
          **Screening tools:** STOP-Bang, Epworth Sleepiness Scale
          **Diagnosis:** polysomnography or validated home sleep apnoea testing; severity by the apnoea-hypopnoea index (AHI) - mild 5-15, moderate 15-30, severe >30 events/hour

        #### Management
        
          **Behavioural:** weight loss, reduce evening alcohol and sedatives, treat nasal obstruction, positional therapy for positional OSA
          **CPAP:** first-line for moderate-severe OSA and symptomatic mild OSA; adherence is the key determinant of benefit
          **Mandibular advancement device:** for mild-moderate disease or CPAP intolerance
          **Surgery/ENT:** selected anatomical cases
          **Safety:** address driving risk where sleepiness is significant (follow local fitness-to-drive guidance)

          Central sleep apnoea is different
          In central sleep apnoea the drive to breathe is intermittently lost (no airway effort), often with heart failure or opioid use or as Cheyne-Stokes breathing. It needs specialist assessment - management differs from OSA.

      🕰️Circadian rhythm sleep-wake disorders▾
      
        #### The pattern
        Sleep itself is normal, but the timing of the body clock is misaligned with the desired or required schedule, so sleep happens at the wrong time.

          TypeFeatures and approach
          
            Delayed sleep-wake phaseClock runs late ("night owl"); common in adolescents. Timed morning light, evening melatonin, gradual schedule shift
            Advanced sleep-wake phaseClock runs early; more common with age. Timed evening light exposure
            Shift work disorderInsomnia and/or sleepiness from non-standard hours. Strategic napping, light management, scheduling, judicious caffeine
            Jet lagTransient; light timing and short-term melatonin aid re-entrainment

        #### Tools
        
          **Sleep diary / actigraphy:** confirm the timing pattern over 1-2 weeks
          **Timed bright light and melatonin:** the two levers that shift the clock; timing relative to the individual's rhythm is what matters

      ⚡Hypersomnolence and narcolepsy▾
      
        #### Excessive daytime sleepiness with adequate opportunity
        When a person sleeps enough yet remains pathologically sleepy, consider a central disorder of hypersomnolence - after excluding OSA, insufficient sleep, circadian misalignment, medication effects and depression.

        #### Narcolepsy
        
          **Type 1:** excessive sleepiness plus **cataplexy** (sudden emotion-triggered loss of muscle tone); linked to loss of hypothalamic orexin (hypocretin)
          **Type 2:** sleepiness without cataplexy
          **Other features:** sleep paralysis, hypnagogic hallucinations, fragmented night sleep
          **Diagnosis:** specialist - polysomnography followed by the multiple sleep latency test (MSLT); CSF orexin in selected cases
        
        #### Management (specialist-led)
        
          - Scheduled naps and sleep hygiene
          - Wake-promoting agents (e.g. modafinil) for sleepiness; specific agents for cataplexy

          Always exclude the common causes first
          Insufficient sleep, untreated OSA and sedating medications cause far more daytime sleepiness than narcolepsy. Confirm sleep duration with a diary before pursuing rare diagnoses.

      🌜Parasomnias and movement disorders▾
      
        #### NREM parasomnias (arousal disorders)
        
          **Sleepwalking, sleep terrors, confusional arousals:** arise from deep non-REM sleep early in the night; common in children and usually outgrown
          **Management:** reassurance, safety-proofing the environment, ensuring adequate sleep, and removing triggers (sleep deprivation, alcohol, some medications)
        
        #### REM sleep behaviour disorder (RBD)
        
          **Acting out dreams** with loss of normal REM atonia - punching, kicking, shouting; risk of injury to self or bed-partner
          **Significance:** strong association with later alpha-synuclein neurodegeneration (Parkinson's disease, dementia with Lewy bodies) - warrants neurology referral
          **Management:** bedroom safety; specialist pharmacotherapy (e.g. melatonin, clonazepam)
        
        #### Sleep-related movement disorders
        
          **Restless legs syndrome** and **periodic limb movement disorder** - covered in detail in their own explainers; check ferritin and review aggravating drugs
          **Bruxism, sleep-related leg cramps** - usually managed conservatively

      ⚠️Red flags and when to refer▾
      
        #### Refer for sleep-medicine / specialist assessment
        
          - Suspected moderate-severe OSA, or OSA with significant sleepiness affecting driving or safety-critical work
          - Excessive daytime sleepiness unexplained after addressing sleep duration and OSA (possible narcolepsy/hypersomnolence)
          - Dream-enactment behaviour suggestive of REM sleep behaviour disorder
          - Insomnia refractory to CBT-I, or where complex comorbidity dominates
          - Suspected central sleep apnoea (heart failure, opioids, Cheyne-Stokes pattern)
        
        #### Safety priorities
        
          **Driving and sleepiness:** counsel and document; follow local fitness-to-drive rules
          **Cardiometabolic risk:** untreated OSA contributes to hypertension, arrhythmia and metabolic disease - treat actively
          **Deprescribing:** taper long-term hypnotics and review drugs that fragment sleep or cause daytime sedation
