## Overview


**LUTS in men encompasses obstructive (voiding) and irritative (storage) symptoms.** Common in ageing populations; majority benign (BPH), but prostate cancer and neurogenic causes must be excluded. LUTS significantly impacts quality of life, sleep, and erectile function.

        #### LUTS categories & symptoms
        
          **Voiding (obstructive) symptoms:** Weak stream, hesitancy (difficulty initiating), prolonged voiding time, incomplete emptying, straining, dribbling post-void
          **Storage (irritative/overactive) symptoms:** Frequency (>8 voids/24 h), nocturia (≥2 episodes/night), urgency (sudden compelling urge), urgency incontinence
          **Post-micturition:** Dribbling, sensation of incomplete emptying

          LUTS ≠ BPH
          LUTS is symptom-based; BPH is histological diagnosis. Many men with BPH are asymptomatic; many with LUTS have normal prostate. The link is probabilistic, not deterministic.

        #### Differential diagnosis
        
          DiagnosisKey featuresTypical LUTS pattern
          
            **Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia (BPH)**Age >50, gradually progressive, large prostate on DRE, PSA elevated (prostate size correlates)Voiding symptoms predominant; may have some storage; gradual onset
            **Prostate cancer**Age >60, family history, PSA >4 ng/mL (or high-risk markers), hard/nodular prostate on DREOften asymptomatic (early); voiding obstruction if locally advanced; haematuria/haemospermia possible
            **Overactive bladder (OAB)**Storage symptoms predominant, no obstructive findings, normal post-void residual (PVR)Frequency, urgency, nocturia; normal flow rate & post-void residual
            **Neurogenic bladder (Parkinson's, DM, spinal cord lesion)**Known neurological condition, history of spinal cord trauma or diseaseMixed voiding/storage; may have incomplete emptying & high PVR
            **Urinary tract infection (UTI) / prostatitis**Dysuria, frequency, nocturia, acute onset; fever/rigors/perineal pain possibleAcute storage symptoms; PSA may be elevated transiently
            **Medication-induced**Antihistamines (diphenhydramine), anticholinergics (antispasmodics), sympathomimetics (pseudoephedrine), opioidsStorage or voiding symptoms depending on agent; onset after medication start

        #### Red flags requiring urgent evaluation
        
          - Acute urinary retention (inability to void, bladder distension, suprapubic pain)
          - Recurrent UTI (≥3 in 12 months) → suggests incomplete emptying or abnormal post-void residual
          - Haematuria (gross) → urology referral to exclude malignancy
          - Suspected prostatitis (fever, perineal/testicular pain, dysuria, rigors)
          - Elevated PSA (>4 ng/mL, or age-adjusted) + abnormal DRE → prostate cancer workup
          - Known spinal cord disease or neurological condition with new/worsening LUTS → consider cauda equina syndrome or neurogenic bladder progression

      🔍Assessment & diagnostic approach▾
      
        #### History & symptom scoring
        
          **Onset & duration:** Gradual (BPH) vs acute (infection, retention); progressive vs stable
          **Symptom impact:** IPSS (International Prostate Symptom Score) is gold-standard 7-item questionnaire; scores 0–7 (mild), 8–19 (moderate), 20–35 (severe). HealthPathways provides IPSS calculator; available from NZF/primary care tools.
          **Nocturia pattern:** How many times per night? When did it start? Impact on sleep/work?
          **Bother score:** Not all LUTS warrant intervention; ask "How much does this bother you?" Some men tolerate moderate LUTS; others seek treatment for mild.
          **Medications:** Review for anticholinergics, antihistamines, sympathomimetics, opioids (all worsen LUTS)
          **Neurological history:** Diabetes, Parkinson's, spinal cord injury, stroke, MS
          **Urinary retention episodes:** Any episodes of acute inability to void? Need for catheterisation?
        
        #### Physical examination
        
          **Abdominal exam:** Palpate suprapubic region for distended bladder (retention); costovertebral angle tenderness (upper tract pathology)
          **Digital rectal exam (DRE):** Assess prostate size (small, normal, large), consistency (smooth vs nodular), tenderness. Abnormal DRE (nodule, hard areas, >3 cm) → refer for PSA/imaging
          **Neurological exam:** Lower limb strength, reflexes, saddle sensation, anal tone (screen for cauda equina if retention or severe lower-limb signs)
          **External genitalia:** Meatal stricture, phimosis, penile curvature (history of Peyronie's affects treatment choice)
        
        #### Investigations
        
          **Urinalysis & culture:** Rule out UTI, haematuria, proteinuria; if haematuria present → urology referral
          **PSA (prostate-specific antigen):** Screen for prostate cancer in men with LUTS >50 years (or >40 if family history or Black ethnicity). PSA >4 ng/mL warrants further investigation (DRE reassessment, consider biopsy if PSA velocity high or DRE abnormal).
          **Post-void residual (PVR):** Ultrasound or catheterisation; normal 100 mL. High PVR suggests incomplete emptying (neurogenic, severe obstruction) & increases UTI risk.
          **Uroflowmetry:** Non-invasive; assess flow rate. Obstructed pattern (low peak flow 
          **Cystoscopy:** Indicated if haematuria, recurrent UTI with high PVR, or failed medical therapy with severe LUTS (assess for stricture, trabeculation, diverticula)
          **Urodynamic studies:** Not first-line; reserved for complex cases (neurogenic, prior failed surgery, diagnostic uncertainty)
        
        #### NZ-specific HealthPathways pathway
        
          **Primary care:** IPSS scoring, symptom characterisation, DRE, PSA if appropriate, basic investigation (urinalysis, PVR)
          **Urology referral criteria:** Acute retention, recurrent UTI, haematuria, elevated PSA with abnormal DRE, refractory LUTS despite medical therapy, or planned surgical intervention
          **Secondary care:** Imaging (transrectal ultrasound/MRI for prostate size), cystoscopy, urodynamics, surgical planning

      🛁Conservative & lifestyle management▾
      
        **Lifestyle modifications are first-line for all LUTS severity and should precede or accompany pharmacotherapy.** Often effective, cost-free, and improve symptom burden significantly.

        #### Fluid management
        
          **Total fluid intake:** Advise 1.5–2 L/day (consistent hydration); excess fluids (>2.5 L/day) worsen frequency & nocturia
          **Fluid timing:** Front-load intake (2/3 of daily fluid before 6 PM); minimize fluid 2–3 hours before bed
          **Caffeine & alcohol:** Both are diuretics & bladder irritants; reduce or eliminate (particularly evening consumption)
          **Carbonated drinks:** Acidic, may irritate bladder; encourage plain water
        
        #### Bladder retraining
        
          **Voiding schedule:** Empty bladder at regular intervals (e.g., 3–4 hourly); resist urge to void between scheduled times to increase capacity
          **Timed voiding (for nocturia):** Set alarm at night if waking >2× per night; resist urge to void until scheduled time; gradually extend interval
          **Double voiding:** After initial void, wait 30 seconds, then void again to minimise post-void residual
          **Pelvic floor exercises (PFE):** Kegel exercises (squeeze pelvic floor muscles for 3 seconds, release for 3 seconds, repeat 10×, 3 sets/day) improve continence & may reduce urgency. Efficacy modest but evidence-based.
        
        #### Weight loss & exercise
        
          **Weight loss (if BMI >25):** 5–10% reduction improves LUTS symptoms in obese men; probably mediated by reduced intra-abdominal pressure & improved metabolic state
          **Regular aerobic exercise:** 30 min moderate activity 3–5× weekly associated with improved LUTS scores & reduced progression
          **Avoid straining:** Chronic constipation worsens voiding obstruction; maintain bowel regularity with fibre & fluids
        
        #### Medication review
        
          **Discontinue exacerbating agents:** Antihistamines (diphenhydramine), anticholinergics (hyoscyamine, dicyclomine), sympathomimetics (pseudoephedrine), opioids. If needed, substitute with alternatives (e.g., loratadine instead of diphenhydramine; switch opioid to lower-dose fentanyl patch if chronic pain).
          **Review diuretic timing:** If on loop diuretic (furosemide), can dose be shifted to morning? Fluid restriction alternative if tolerated by cardiac/renal status?
        
        #### Addressing nocturia specifically
        
          **Desmopressin (DDAVP):** Synthetic vasopressin; reduces nocturnal urine production. Modest benefit (0.5–1 episode reduction per night). PHARMAC-funded; consider if nocturia ≥2 episodes significantly impacting sleep & other measures failed. Caution: hyponatraemia risk (especially >65 years), fluid retention; monitor electrolytes.
          **Sleep hygiene:** Early-evening fluid cutoff; optimize sleep quality (exercise, avoid screens 1 hour before bed); may improve perceived nocturia burden even if actual episodes unchanged

          Lifestyle modifications take 4–6 weeks
          Do not expect immediate improvement. Counsel patients that adherence to fluid management & bladder retraining often reduces symptoms by 30–50% over 1–2 months, potentially deferring or eliminating need for medications.

      💊Pharmacotherapy: alpha-blockers, 5-alpha reductase inhibitors, antimuscarinics▾
      
        **Medical management is second-line after lifestyle measures.** Efficacy is symptom-dependent: alpha-blockers work well for voiding obstruction; antimuscarinics for storage symptoms. 5-ARIs slow BPH progression but have delayed onset.

        #### Alpha-adrenergic blockers (alpha-1 antagonists)
        **Mechanism:** Block alpha-1 receptors on smooth muscle in prostate stromal tissue & bladder neck → smooth muscle relaxation → reduced outlet resistance & improved voiding.

          Agent (brand)Dose (NZ/PHARMAC)OnsetEfficacy & notes
          
            **Tamsulosin (Flomax)**0.4 mg nocte (modified-release)1–2 weeksSelective alpha-1A; rapid effect; well-tolerated; dizziness/syncope uncommon due to selectivity. First-line in many guidelines.
            **Alfuzosin (Xatral)**2.5 mg BD or 5 mg nocte (modified-release)1–2 weeksAlpha-1A selective; similar efficacy to tamsulosin; may have marginally less sexual dysfunction; orthostatic hypotension possible.
            **Doxazosin (Cardura)**1 mg nocte, titrate to 2–4 mg nocte1–3 weeksNon-selective alpha-1; efficacy similar; more hypotensive effect than selective agents; useful if concurrent hypertension (treats both). Requires slow titration to minimise syncope risk.

          **Efficacy:** ~70% of men report symptom improvement; IPSS score reduction ~5–8 points on average. Work within 1–2 weeks; peak effect by 4–6 weeks.
          **Adverse effects:** Dizziness (especially first dose, non-selective agents), orthostatic hypotension, retrograde ejaculation (10–15% tamsulosin, more with others), rarely syncope. Headache, rhinitis (non-selective agents).
          **Contraindications:** Symptomatic hypotension, syncope, QT prolongation (rare with these agents)
          **Deprescribing:** Gradual reduction if symptoms resolve or intolerance develops; abrupt cessation may cause rebound hypertension (less relevant with alpha-blockers than older agents). Can safely cease after 3–6 months if symptom-free.
        
        #### 5-Alpha reductase inhibitors (5-ARIs)
        **Mechanism:** Inhibit enzyme that converts testosterone → dihydrotestosterone (DHT); DHT drives prostate cell growth. Slow progression of BPH but take 6–12 months for maximal effect.

          Agent (brand)Dose (NZ/PHARMAC)OnsetEfficacy & notes
          
            **Finasteride (Proscar)**5 mg nocte3–6 months; maximal at 12 monthsType II 5-ARI only; reduces prostate volume ~20% over 1–2 years; slows BPH progression & acute retention risk. ~3–4 mm IPSS reduction (modest).
            **Dutasteride (Avodart)**0.5 mg nocte3–6 months; maximal at 12 monthsType I & II 5-ARI; slightly more efficacious than finasteride; longer half-life (allows weekly dosing off-label in some countries, but 0.5 mg nocte standard in NZ). ~4–5 mm IPSS reduction.

          **Efficacy:** Modest symptom relief alone; most value in preventing progression (reducing acute retention risk by ~50% over 3–4 years in large-prostate BPH). Best used as long-term preventive agent, not acute symptom relief.
          **Adverse effects:** Sexual dysfunction (erectile dysfunction 5–10%, reduced libido, ejaculatory dysfunction); gynaecomastia (breast tenderness/enlargement) rare. PSA falls ~50% over 6 months (important for cancer screening—adjust baseline).
          **Duration:** Long-term (years); effects persist only if continued
          **Combination therapy:** Alpha-blocker + 5-ARI more effective than either alone for moderate–severe LUTS; synergistic (combined IPSS reduction ~8–10 points). Reserved for large prostate (>30–40 g on imaging) or acute retention history.
        
        #### Antimuscarinics (for storage symptoms/OAB)
        **Mechanism:** Block muscarinic receptors on detrusor smooth muscle → reduce urgency, frequency, urgency incontinence. Efficacy for storage symptoms better than alpha-blockers; caution re urinary retention risk if significant post-void residual.

          AgentDose (NZ)Efficacy & notes
          
            **Oxybutynin**5 mg BD–TDS; extended-release 10 mg nocteEffective for urgency/frequency; significant anticholinergic side-effects (dry mouth, constipation, confusion in elderly)
            **Tolterodine**2 mg BD or 4 mg nocte (extended-release)Better tolerability profile than oxybutynin; similar efficacy; preferred in older men
            **Solifenacin**5–10 mg nocteOnce-daily dosing; efficacy comparable to tolterodine; anticholinergic side-effects dose-dependent
            **Mirabegron (Myrbetriq)**25–50 mg nocteBeta-3 agonist (non-antimuscarinic); stimulates detrusor relaxation; fewer anticholinergic effects; slightly more tachycardia risk; PHARMAC-funded; newer option

          **Caution:** Antimuscarinics increase post-void residual; avoid if PVR already >100 mL or history of retention (risk of acute retention)
          **Efficacy:** Reduce urgency episodes by ~2–3/day; modest effect on incontinence. Better for pure OAB than mixed LUTS.
          **Adverse effects:** Dry mouth (50%+), constipation, blurred vision, cognitive effects (confusion in elderly, risk of dementia with long-term use—debated); tachycardia (mirabegron)

          Do not combine alpha-blocker + antimuscarinic + 5-ARI empirically
          Risk of urinary retention & overly complex regimen. Use: alpha-blocker for obstructive LUTS; +5-ARI if large prostate or retention risk; antimuscarinics only if pure storage symptoms & low PVR. Always reassess need after 3–6 months.

      🔨Surgical & minimally invasive options▾
      
        **Surgical intervention reserved for refractory LUTS, acute retention, or significant complications (recurrent UTI, bladder stones, upper tract changes).** Choice depends on prostate size, patient age/comorbidities, and patient preference.

        #### Traditional transurethral resection of prostate (TURP)
        
          **Gold standard; endoscopic resection of prostate adenoma.** Best outcomes for prostate 80%).
          **Risks:** TURP syndrome (hyponatraemia, fluid overload; rare, ~1%), retrograde ejaculation (70%+), erectile dysfunction (5–10%), bleeding, infection, perforation, recurrent strictures (10% by 10 years).
          **Recovery:** 4–6 weeks catheter possible; 6–8 weeks to peak symptom relief; 7–10 days to normal activities for most.
          **NZ access:** DHB-funded; waitlist typically 3–6 months; appropriate for IPSS >20 with failed medical therapy
        
        #### Minimally invasive procedures (emerging, variable funding)
        
          **Transurethral microwave thermotherapy (TUMT):** Microwave energy destroys prostate tissue; outpatient, minimal anesthesia. Modest efficacy (IPSS improvement ~5–7 points); fewer sexual side-effects; durable ~5 years before potential re-treatment.
          **Transurethral needle ablation (TUNA):** Radiofrequency energy; outpatient. Similar efficacy & durability to TUMT; less common than TURP.
          **Laser procedures (HoLEP, GreenLight):** Holmium or KTP laser resection; good for large prostates (>45 g); less TURP syndrome risk; retrograde ejaculation still common. Emerging in NZ public sector.
          **Prostate urethral lift (PUL):** Temporary sutures lift lateral lobes; minimally invasive; preserves ejaculation. Efficacy 40–60% (less impressive than TURP); durability ~2 years; emerging technology with variable access.
        
        #### Indications for referral to urology for surgical consultation
        
          - Failed medical therapy (IPSS still >8 after 12 weeks alpha-blocker ± lifestyle) with severe bother
          - Recurrent acute urinary retention (≥2 episodes, or any episode if patient strongly symptomatic)
          - Recurrent UTI attributable to LUTS (≥3 in 12 months with high PVR)
          - Bladder stones or upper tract dilatation (hydronephrosis) secondary to outlet obstruction
          - Patient desire for definitive treatment (symptom scores high, informed of permanence of sexual side-effects)

      🚨Acute urinary retention: management & prevention▾
      
        **Acute urinary retention (AUR) is a urological emergency.** Painful, distressing; requires urgent decompression. ~10% of men with LUTS experience AUR; BPH, medications, UTI/prostatitis, and neurogenic bladder are common precipitants.

        #### Acute management (ED/acute care)
        
          **Diagnosis:** Inability to void despite urge; suprapubic distension & tenderness; ultrasound confirms distended bladder (>400–500 mL bladder volume)
          **Catheterisation (urethral or suprapubic):** Immediate relief; standard treatment. Drain slowly (250 mL/30 min intervals) if retention >1 L to avoid post-obstructive diuresis complications (electrolyte abnormalities, cardiovascular stress).
          **Investigations:** Urinalysis & culture (UTI common precipitant), PSA (often transiently elevated post-AUR; not reliable for cancer screening immediately), PVR if voiding restored
          **Medications to review:** Antihistamines, anticholinergics, sympathomimetics, opioids—often precipitate AUR in men with marginal BPH; discontinue if possible
          **Trial of void (TrOV) after 24–48 hours:**** If precipitant reversible (UTI treated, offending medication ceased), attempt spontaneous voiding after catheter removal. ~50% succeed on first trial. If failure, repeat catheterisation & consider alpha-blocker or 5-ARI initiation.
        
        #### Chronic retention (CRR) with recurrent episodes
        
          **Management:** Initiate alpha-blocker immediately (tamsulosin 0.4 mg nocte) + consider 5-ARI if large prostate; early urology referral for assessment & possible surgical intervention (TURP or minimally invasive procedure)
          **Intermittent self-catheterisation:** If recurrent episodes & patient declines/unsuitable for surgery, teach clean intermittent catheterisation (CIC) 3–4 times daily; reduces UTI risk vs chronic indwelling catheter
        
        #### Prevention of AUR in high-risk patients
        
          **Identify risk factors:** IPSS >15, age >75, prostate >30 g, prior retention episode
          **Prophylactic alpha-blocker:** Offer tamsulosin or alfuzosin if symptoms present & high risk; reduces AUR incidence by ~25%
          **Avoid precipitants:** Counsel on avoiding antihistamines, anticholinergics, sympathomimetics; if pain relief needed, use non-NSAID alternatives (paracetamol, topical agents) to avoid opioid-induced urinary retention
          **5-ARI + alpha-blocker:** Combination therapy reduces AUR risk by ~50% in high-risk, large-prostate BPH; consider if prior retention or high risk

      💡Clinical pearls & pitfalls▾
      
        #### Pearls
        
          ✦**Lifestyle first, every time.** Fluid management & bladder retraining reduce LUTS 30–50%; cost-free, no side-effects. Precede or accompany medications; always counsel before prescribing.
          ✦**LUTS severity ≠ bother.** IPSS score matters clinically, but patient perception drives treatment. A man with IPSS 10 severely bothered warrants treatment; IPSS 25 who sleeps well may decline medication. Individualise.
          ✦**Alpha-blockers work fast; 5-ARIs work slowly.** Alpha-blockers: 1–2 weeks efficacy. 5-ARIs: 6–12 months for prostate shrinkage effect. Do NOT combine 5-ARI without alpha-blocker for immediate symptom relief; add alpha-blocker if >6 months symptoms uncontrolled, not upfront.
          ✦**Screen for prostate cancer upfront.** LUTS in men >50 warrants PSA (or >40 if family history/Black ethnicity) & DRE. Abnormal findings → urology referral before treating LUTS as "just BPH."
          ✦**Post-void residual matters.** Elevated PVR (>100 mL) indicates incomplete emptying; predisposes to UTI & retention. Antimuscarinics contraindicated. Consider alpha-blocker or 5-ARI instead; may need ultrasound follow-up.
          ✦**Nocturia is the most bothersome symptom for many.** Often least responsive to BPH treatment; often polypharmacy-related or sleep-disorder-related. Fluid management (evening cutoff), desmopressin (if refractory), and sleep assessment often more helpful than higher-dose alpha-blockers.
          ✦**Review medications ruthlessly.** Antihistamines, anticholinergics, sympathomimetics, opioids precipitate LUTS. Often the cheapest, safest intervention is ceasing the culprit drug rather than adding an alpha-blocker.
        
        #### Pitfalls
        
          **Treating asymptomatic haematuria as BPH.** Gross haematuria in setting of LUTS needs urology referral to exclude bladder cancer, regardless of BPH severity.
          **Missing prostate cancer while treating "LUTS."** Always assess PSA + DRE upfront; do not assume LUTS = benign.
          **Prescribing antimuscarinic without checking PVR.** High PVR + anticholinergic = acute retention risk. Always measure or estimate PVR (bladder scan) before adding antimuscarinics; contraindicated if >100 mL.
          **Empirical polypharmacy.** Alpha-blocker + 5-ARI + antimuscarinic + desmopressin is not evidence-based. Start with alpha-blocker ± lifestyle; add 5-ARI only if large prostate/retention risk; avoid antimuscarinics if outlet obstruction.
          **Not offering trial of void after AUR catheterisation.** Many patients void successfully after alpha-blocker initiation + catheter removal; reduces need for chronic catheterisation.
          **Ignoring recurrent UTI as LUTS complication.** High PVR from LUTS increases UTI risk. Investigate PVR; correct with alpha-blocker or surgical intervention if needed, not just repeated antibiotics.
