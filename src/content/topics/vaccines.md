#### The basic principle
        A vaccine teaches your immune system to recognise a germ before you ever meet the real thing — so if you encounter it later, your body already knows how to fight it.

        #### The five-step process
        
          **1. A safe preview:** The vaccine contains a harmless piece of a virus or bacteria — a weakened version, a killed version, a surface protein, or genetic instructions to make one. None of these can cause the disease itself.
          **2. Your immune system trains:** Your immune cells recognise the preview as foreign and learn its features. They make antibodies and "memory cells" that remember what they saw.
          **3. Mild side effects are normal:** A sore arm, tiredness, or mild fever for a day or two is the immune response doing its job. Serious side effects are rare and monitored closely.
          **4. Long-term protection:** If the real germ arrives years later, your memory cells launch a fast, strong response — often before you even feel ill. Some vaccines need boosters because immunity fades over time.
          **5. Community protection:** When most people are vaccinated, the germ struggles to spread. That protects people who can't be vaccinated — newborns, those on chemotherapy, the immunocompromised.

      🧬Types of vaccines▾
      
        Different germs need different approaches. Modern vaccines use a few core strategies.

        #### Live, weakened (attenuated)
        A live virus weakened so it can't cause illness in a healthy person. Triggers a strong, long-lasting response. **Examples:** MMR, varicella, rotavirus, yellow fever.

        #### Killed (inactivated)
        The whole germ, but killed. Cannot replicate. Often needs boosters. **Examples:** Inactivated polio, hepatitis A, most flu shots.

        #### Subunit / recombinant
        Just one piece of the germ — usually a surface protein. Very pure and well-tolerated. **Examples:** Hepatitis B, HPV, shingles, pertussis.

        #### Toxoid
        The bacterial toxin, but inactivated. Your body learns to neutralise the toxin. **Examples:** Tetanus, diphtheria.

        #### Conjugate / polysaccharide
        A sugar from the bacterial coat linked to a protein, so immune systems can recognise it. **Examples:** Hib, pneumococcal, meningococcal.

        #### mRNA
        A short genetic instruction your cells use briefly to make one harmless protein, then dispose of. mRNA does not enter your DNA. **Examples:** Comirnaty (Pfizer COVID-19), Spikevax (Moderna).

      📅NZ immunisation schedule▾
      
        Aotearoa New Zealand offers free vaccines from 6 weeks through to 65+. Key funded vaccines:

        #### Childhood (under 18)
        
          **6-in-1:** Diphtheria, tetanus, pertussis, polio, hepatitis B, Hib (at 6 weeks, 3 months, 5 months, booster at 4 years)
          **PCV13:** Pneumococcal (3 + 1 booster)
          **Rotavirus:** (2 oral doses before 25 weeks)
          **MenB:** (3 + 1 booster; added 2023)
          **MMR:** (12 months, 15 months)
          **Varicella:** (chickenpox, 15 months)
        
        #### Adolescent / adult (funded)
        
          **HPV (Gardasil 9):** Up to age 26 (school program at Year 7–8)
          **Tdap boosters:** At ages 11, 45, and 65
          **Influenza (annual):** Aged 65+; pregnant women; chronic conditions; Māori & Pacific 55+; children <5 with respiratory history
          **Shingles (Shingrix):** Age 65 (catch-up to 80)
          **COVID-19:** All ages 5+ per current guidance

      💰What's funded and what isn't▾
      
        #### Pharmac funds
        Childhood schedule, HPV (up to 26), Tdap, some flu, shingles (65+), COVID-19, and meningococcal ACWY for close-living settings (ages 13–25). Many high-risk medical groups also receive additional vaccines.

        #### Not routinely funded (self-pay or employer)
        
          - Travel vaccines: yellow fever, typhoid, hepatitis A, Japanese encephalitis, rabies, cholera
          - Meningococcal ACWY for general population outside funded groups
          - Meningococcal B outside the infant catch-up schedule and high-risk groups
          - Influenza if you don't meet funded criteria
          - Shingles outside the funded age window (self-pay often substantial)
          - BCG (tuberculosis) for general population — only for high-risk infants

          Funding decisions
          Pharmac assesses each vaccine on clinical benefit, cost-effectiveness, and population impact within a fixed budget. Safe and effective vaccines can take time to be added; not all recommended vaccines are funded.

      🌍How NZ compares internationally▾
      
        Vaccine schedules vary by country because disease patterns, regulations, and funding priorities differ. Most high-income countries cover the same core diseases.

        #### Things the US does that NZ doesn't
        
          - Hepatitis A routinely at 12–23 months
          - Meningococcal ACWY routinely at 11 and 16 years
          - Influenza annually for everyone 6 months and older
        
        #### Things NZ does that the US doesn't
        
          - MenB in routine infant schedule (US is risk-based or shared decision-making)
        
        #### UK specifics
        
          - Meningococcal ACWY at 14 years
          - Live nasal influenza for children 2–17 years annually
          - Maternal RSV vaccine from 28 weeks (rolled out 2024)
        
        #### Australia specifics
        
          - Meningococcal B on the NIP since mid-2024
          - Meningococcal ACWY at 12 months and 14–16 years

      ❓Common questions▾
      
        #### Are vaccines safe?
        Vaccines are among the most heavily tested medicines. Common side effects are mild: sore arm, tiredness, mild fever. Serious reactions (anaphylaxis) occur at roughly 1 per million doses and are quickly treatable.

        #### Can a vaccine give me the disease?
        Inactivated, subunit, toxoid, and mRNA vaccines: no — no live germ present. Live-attenuated vaccines can very rarely cause a mild form of the illness, which is not contagious and much milder than natural disease.

        #### Why so many vaccines so young?
        Early infancy is when babies are most vulnerable to several vaccine-preventable diseases. Maternal antibodies fade after 6 months. The schedule starts at 6 weeks because that's when the immune system responds well.

        #### If I missed a vaccine, can I catch up?
        Yes. Catch-up schedules exist for all ages. Previous doses count — you don't restart. Talk to your GP, nurse, or call 0800 IMMUNE (0800 466 863). Catch-up vaccines on the schedule are funded up to age 18.

        #### Can I get vaccinated in pregnancy?
        Two vaccines are recommended and funded: Tdap (16–38 weeks) and influenza (any trimester). COVID-19 vaccines are safe in pregnancy. Live vaccines (MMR, varicella) are not given during pregnancy.

        #### Do I need boosters as an adult?
        Yes — protection fades. Funded adult boosters: Tdap (45, 65), shingles (65+, two doses), flu (annual if eligible), COVID-19 (per guidance).

        #### I'm travelling — what do I need?
        Depends on destination. Common travel vaccines: hepatitis A, typhoid, yellow fever, Japanese encephalitis, rabies, cholera. Most are not funded. Book a travel consultation 6–8 weeks before departure.

        #### Can I have multiple vaccines at once?
        Yes — it's standard practice. The schedule is designed for multiple vaccines at the same visit. It doesn't reduce effectiveness or increase side effects.

        #### What about allergies?
        A previous severe allergic reaction to a vaccine or its components is the main contraindication. Most other allergies are not a reason to avoid vaccines. Even people with egg allergy can have flu vaccines.

        #### Where do I find my vaccination history?
        Records are held in the National Immunisation Register (NIR) and Aotearoa Immunisation Register (AIR). Ask your GP for a printout or check My Health Record.
