#### IgE-mediated (immediate, classic allergy)
        **Immune system produces IgE antibodies against milk proteins** (beta-lactoglobulin, alpha-lactalbumin, casein, lactoferrin). On re-exposure, IgE cross-links on mast cells and basophils, triggering degranulation and release of histamine, tryptase and other mediators.

          **Onset:** Minutes to 2 hours after milk exposure
          **Symptoms:** Urticaria, angioedema, anaphylaxis, acute vomiting, wheezing, bronchospasm
          **Testing:** Skin prick test or specific IgE (CAP/phadiatop) positive
          **Prevalence:** ~40–50% of cow's milk allergy cases
        
        #### Non-IgE-mediated (delayed, cell-mediated)
        **T-cell mediated inflammation without IgE.** Immune attack is slower and affects gastrointestinal lining; symptoms delayed hours to days.

          **Onset:** Hours to days after milk ingestion
          **Symptoms:** Chronic diarrhoea, vomiting, poor weight gain, abdominal distension, blood/mucus in stool, eczema flares
          **Testing:** Skin prick test and specific IgE typically negative; diagnosis is clinical (symptoms resolve on exclusion, recur on challenge)
          **Prevalence:** ~50–60% of cow's milk allergy cases
        
        #### Mixed IgE and non-IgE
        Symptoms span both immediate and delayed reactions; example: urticaria plus delayed GI symptoms.

      📋Clinical presentation▾
      
        #### IgE-mediated presentations
        
          **Urticaria and angioedema:** Wheal-and-flare reaction on skin; swelling of lips, tongue or face
          **Acute vomiting:** Minutes to 1–2 hours after feeding
          **Anaphylaxis:** Stridor, wheezing, hypotension, pallor, loss of consciousness. Rare but life-threatening. Treat with IM adrenaline immediately.
          **Atopic dermatitis (eczema):** Can be triggered or worsened by cow's milk
          **Acute allergic colitis:** Blood and mucus in stool, diarrhoea
        
        #### Non-IgE-mediated presentations
        
          **Food protein-induced enterocolitis syndrome (FPIES):** Delayed-onset vomiting (1–3 hours post-feeding), pallor, lethargy, diarrhoea. Can progress to severe volume depletion and shock if untreated.
          **Food protein-induced allergic colitis:** Bloody diarrhoea, mucus in stool, possible anaemia from chronic blood loss
          **Food protein-induced enteropathy:** Chronic diarrhoea, villous atrophy on biopsy, failure to thrive, iron deficiency anaemia
          **Gastro-oesophageal reflux symptoms:** Frequent vomiting, irritability, feeding refusal
        
        #### Red flags warranting urgent assessment
        
          - Signs of anaphylaxis (stridor, hypotension, altered consciousness)
          - Severe dehydration (decreased urine, sunken fontanelle, lethargy)
          - Failure to thrive (weight loss or plateau despite adequate intake)
          - Bloody diarrhoea with anaemia

      🔍Diagnosis▾
      
        #### Clinical history
        
          - Timing of symptoms relative to milk exposure (immediately vs delayed)
          - Family history of allergy or atopy (eczema, asthma, allergic rhinitis)
          - Age of onset (typically 2–12 weeks for cow's milk formula; later if breastfed)
          - Pattern of symptoms (with every feed vs intermittent)
          - Associated symptoms (respiratory, skin, GI severity)
        
        #### Skin prick testing (SPT)
        
          - Done at any age; safe in infants. Wheal >3 mm is suggestive of IgE
          - Positive SPT supports IgE-mediated allergy
          - Negative SPT does not exclude non-IgE or mixed allergy
          - Must stop antihistamines 3–5 days before testing
        
        #### Specific IgE testing (sIgE / CAP)
        
          - Blood test for IgE to milk proteins or components (beta-lactoglobulin, casein, alpha-lactalbumin, lactoferrin)
          Positive sIgE suggests IgE-mediated allergy; can be quantified (low 2)
          - Component-resolved diagnostics (e.g., to casein vs whey) may predict resolution or severity
        
        #### Elimination and challenge test
        
          - Gold standard for non-IgE allergy; also confirms diagnosis in IgE allergy if testing unclear
          **Elimination phase:** 2–4 weeks on cow's milk-free formula (hydrolysed casein or amino acid). If breastfed, mother avoids dairy. Assess symptom improvement.
          **Challenge phase:** Reintroduce cow's milk gradually under supervision (hospital-based for IgE-mediated or severe cases). Watch for symptom recurrence.
          - Positive challenge (symptom recurrence) confirms diagnosis
        
        #### Additional tests
        
          **FBC:** Check for anaemia in non-IgE allergic colitis (chronic blood loss)
          **Stool studies:** Faecal calprotectin elevated in allergic colitis; may help distinguish from other causes of bloody stool
          **Endoscopy/colonoscopy:** Rarely needed; if done (severe GI symptoms), shows eosinophilic infiltration or villous atrophy

      🍼Formula alternatives and nutrition▾
      
        #### Hydrolysed protein formulas
        
          **Extensively hydrolysed (EH):** Proteins broken into small peptides and amino acids. ~90% of cow's milk allergic infants tolerate. Examples: Nutramigen, Alimentum.
          **Partially hydrolysed (PH):** Less hydrolysis; tolerated by ~70–80%. Use as stepping stone towards cow's milk introduction or if cost is barrier. Examples: HA formulas.
          **Soy-based formula:** NOT first choice; ~10–15% cross-react with cow's milk, and soy allergy is also possible. Consider if hydrolysed intolerance confirmed.
        
        #### Amino acid-based formula
        
          - Elemental formula; purely amino acids and carbs/fat. For severe/refractory cases or FPIES. Nearly 100% tolerated. Expensive; less palatable. Examples: Neocate, Elecare.
        
        #### Goat's milk formula
        
          - Cross-reactivity with cow's milk is high (~90%) due to structural similarity. NOT recommended for diagnosed cow's milk allergy.
        
        #### Nutrition monitoring
        
          - Ensure adequate calories, protein, calcium and vitamin D intake on alternative formula
          - Most hydrolysed and amino acid formulas are fortified appropriately; check labels
          - Monitor weight and length gain; assess growth velocity every 4–8 weeks
          - If failure to thrive despite allergy management, consider other diagnoses (coeliac, other food allergies, infection, metabolic disorder)

      🥣Introduction of cow's milk and weaning▾
      
        #### Weaning and reintroduction
        
          - First foods (vegetables, fruits, iron-fortified cereal) can be introduced at 4–6 months as usual. Avoid cow's milk products until diagnosis confirmed to resolve.
          - Once symptoms well-controlled on exclusion diet (typically 6–12 months old), formal reintroduction challenge should be arranged
          - Challenge preferably done in hospital setting with IgE-mediated allergy history or severe reactions
          - If challenge successful, introduce cow's milk gradually (small amounts in food first, e.g., butter, yoghurt, then small amounts of milk in porridge, then plain milk)
        
        #### Oral immunotherapy (OIT)
        
          - Not routinely used for cow's milk allergy; reserve for highly motivated families with IgE-mediated disease
          - Involves gradual escalating doses of milk protein under medical supervision. Desensitises but does not reliably induce tolerance.
          - Risk of anaphylaxis during treatment; not standard care in most countries
        
        #### Tolerance development and repeat challenges
        
          - ~80% of cow's milk allergic children develop tolerance by age 3–5 years
          - Repeat formal challenge tests (typically in hospital) at 12–24 months to check for tolerance development
          - Earlier tolerance more likely if non-IgE allergy, low specific IgE levels, and no anaphylaxis history
          - Once tolerance confirmed (no symptoms on challenge), maintain cow's milk intake to preserve tolerance

      ⚠️Anaphylaxis management▾
      
        #### Recognition
        
          **Respiratory:** Stridor, wheezing, coughing, dyspnoea, cyanosis
          **Cardiovascular:** Pallor, poor perfusion, hypotension, syncope
          **Skin:** Widespread urticaria, angioedema of face/lips
          **GI:** Severe vomiting, abdominal pain, diarrhoea
          **Neurological:** Lethargy, loss of consciousness
        
        #### Immediate management
        
          **Call emergency services (999/111 in NZ)**
          **Give IM adrenaline (epinephrine):** 0.3 mg (0.3 mL of 1:1000 solution) IM into lateral thigh, can repeat every 5–15 minutes as needed
          **Lie flat** (unless vomiting) with legs elevated
          **IV access and fluids** (once paramedics/hospital available)
          **Antihistamine (H1):** Chlorphenamine 2 mg IM/IV (after adrenaline)
          **Corticosteroid:** IV hydrocortisone 100 mg (prevents biphasic reaction)
        
        #### After anaphylaxis
        
          - Admit for observation (at least 4–6 hours; longer if severe or late-onset symptoms)
          - Prescribe adrenaline auto-injector (e.g., EpiPen) and educate parents on use
          - Strict cow's milk avoidance; dietitian review for safe alternatives
          - Consider referral to specialist allergy clinic for long-term management and tolerance testing

      ❓Common questions▾
      
        #### Can I breastfeed if my baby has cow's milk allergy?
        Yes. Continue breastfeeding and mother should avoid dairy products if symptoms persist. Breastmilk is optimal nutrition and provides immune support. Most babies can remain exclusively breastfed without supplementary formula for the first months.

        #### How do I know if my baby has cow's milk allergy vs colic or reflux?
        Colic is self-limited (improves by 3–4 months); allergy persists. Reflux may improve with positional changes. Allergy symptoms improve quickly (days–weeks) on milk elimination, whereas colic and reflux do not. Allergy typically shows a clear temporal relationship to milk feeds.

        #### Is cow's milk allergy the same as lactose intolerance?
        No. Allergy is immune-mediated reaction to milk proteins. Lactose intolerance is inability to digest milk sugar (lactase deficiency). Lactose intolerance is rare in infants; allergy is far more common.

        #### Can my baby outgrow this allergy?
        Yes. ~80% of cow's milk allergic children develop tolerance by age 3–5 years. Those with non-IgE allergy, low IgE levels, and no anaphylaxis history tend to resolve earlier. Repeat challenges at 12–24 months to check.

        #### Are hydrolysed formulas safe and nutritionally complete?
        Yes. Extensively hydrolysed formulas are nutritionally complete and safe. ~90% are tolerated. Taste may be unpalatable to some infants (bitter), but most adapt. Amino acid formulas are even better tolerated if hydrolysed insufficient.

        #### Should I avoid dairy in my diet while pregnant or breastfeeding to prevent allergy?
        No strong evidence. Exclusive breastfeeding without formula introduction delays but does not prevent cow's milk allergy. Early cow's milk exposure via formula may increase allergy risk in susceptible infants, but avoiding dairy in breastfeeding mothers is not recommended routinely.

        #### What if my baby reacts to hydrolysed formula?
        ~10% fail to tolerate extensively hydrolysed formula. Switch to amino acid (elemental) formula. Also consider other diagnoses: multiple food allergies, severe eczema flares from other triggers, GERD, food protein-induced enteropathy unrelated to milk.
