## Overview


"Nootropic" is a coined word with a specific origin, a modest and testable original meaning, and a present-day usage that has drifted a very long way from both. Patients now arrive describing personal "stacks" of five or six substances - some prescription, some over-the-counter, some ordered as "research chemicals" from overseas websites - assembled from a mix of forum threads, podcasts and their own trial and error. Understanding where that culture came from, and how loose the modern term has become, is useful before trying to have a clinical conversation about it.

        ## Coining a word: Giurgea and the 1972 criteria

        The word was coined in 1972 by Romanian psychologist and chemist Corneliu Giurgea, who had synthesised piracetam a few years earlier and was looking for a term to describe a proposed new class of drug - from the Greek *noos* (mind) and *trepein* (to bend or turn). Giurgea's original definition was narrow and, notably, more rigorous than almost anything that gets called a nootropic today: a true nootropic, he proposed, should enhance learning and memory, protect the brain against physical or chemical injury, work even under conditions that normally impair cognition, have low toxicity, and lack the sedative or stimulant side effects of classical psychoactive drugs.1 Piracetam itself - a cyclic derivative of GABA - never convincingly demonstrated cognitive enhancement in healthy people, and remains, at best, a modest and contested intervention in specific clinical contexts like post-stroke aphasia or myoclonus; it never received FDA approval and is not marketed as a drug in several major jurisdictions, including the US.

        ## The academic and military interest, and the internet's uptake
        Racetams and related "cognitive enhancers" attracted continued research interest through the 1980s and 90s from groups interested in ageing and dementia, and separately from military-funded research into fatigue countermeasures - the latter is part of the lineage that eventually produced modafinil, developed initially for narcolepsy and later trialled by militaries as an alertness aid for sleep-deprived personnel. Alongside this formal research track, an early online enthusiast culture formed on Usenet (alt.drugs.nootropics from the early-to-mid 1990s) trading synthesis routes, dosing anecdotes and mail-order sources for piracetam, hydergine, and vitamin-adjacent compounds like DMAE - largely unregulated as dietary supplements in the US under the framework the Dietary Supplement Health and Education Act established in 1994.

        ## The stacking culture
        The defining practice of the online nootropics community is the "stack": a personally assembled combination of several substances taken together on the theory that their effects are complementary or synergistic. A representative stack might combine a racetam (piracetam or a newer analogue such as oxiracetam or phenylpiracetam), a source of choline (alpha-GPC or citicoline, on the theory that racetams deplete acetylcholine), caffeine paired deliberately with L-theanine to blunt jitteriness, and one or more adaptogenic herbal extracts (rhodiola, bacopa, ashwagandha). Some go further, incorporating prescription drugs obtained without a prescription - modafinil, or in some cases low-dose stimulants - alongside unscheduled synthetic compounds sold explicitly as "not for human consumption" by grey-market research chemical suppliers, a labelling fiction intended to sidestep medicines regulation.

        What distinguishes this culture from simple supplement-taking is its self-experimental, quantified ethos: n=1 trials, structured logging of subjective effects, cognitive testing apps, and an explicit awareness that most of the evidence base for any given compound is thin, animal-only, or drawn from small trials in cognitively impaired populations rather than healthy adults. That awareness coexists, somewhat uneasily, with genuine enthusiasm and considerable commercial promotion from supplement brands that sponsor much of the content describing these stacks.

          The honest evidence picture is narrower than the culture suggests. Systematic reviews of racetams and most "smart drug" supplements in healthy adults show weak, inconsistent, or absent effects on the cognitive domains people actually care about (working memory, sustained attention, executive function).3 The best-supported effects in the whole category belong to established stimulants and modafinil - which reliably improve vigilance and simple task performance under sleep deprivation, with much less consistent benefit on complex cognition in well-rested people4 - and to caffeine, which is not a fringe discovery so much as the original nootropic hiding in plain sight.

        ## The regulatory grey zone
        Much of the modern nootropics market sits deliberately in regulatory gaps. Racetam analogues and novel "research chemicals" are frequently unscheduled simply because no regulator has evaluated them, not because they have been assessed as safe; vendors exploit this by selling them as laboratory reagents rather than supplements or medicines, a labelling strategy that has previously been used for novel psychoactive substances more broadly and that regulators have struggled to keep pace with. In New Zealand this overlaps with the Psychoactive Substances Act framework and Medsafe's authority over unapproved medicines, but enforcement against small-parcel imports from overseas nootropic vendors is inconsistent, and the practical reality is that patients can and do order compounds with essentially no independent quality control, dosing standardisation, or safety data.

        ## What this means in the consultation room
        A patient describing a stack is often engaged, informed in a specific and idiosyncratic way, and not looking to be told the whole enterprise is pointless - that framing tends to shut down disclosure rather than improve it. More useful is to work through the stack ingredient by ingredient: distinguishing genuinely low-risk items (caffeine, theanine, most standardised herbal extracts at label doses) from those that carry real interaction or safety signal (unlicensed modafinil use, unregulated racetam analogues of unknown purity, high-dose combinations with unpredictable pharmacokinetics), and asking specifically about sourcing, since compounds bought as "research chemicals" carry a materially different risk profile from a pharmacy-dispensed medicine even when the molecule is nominally the same.

          Worth asking about specifically
          Unlicensed modafinil (interacts with hormonal contraception via CYP3A4 induction, and carries a rare but serious severe-cutaneous-reaction risk), high-dose racetam-choline stacks in patients with mood disorders (case reports describe agitation and anxiety), and any injectable or nasal-spray "nootropic" purchased online, which raises the same sourcing and contamination concerns as other grey-market injectables.

        ## A nuanced position
        The nootropics subculture is, at its best, a genuinely inquisitive, self-experimental community engaging seriously with pharmacology most doctors never discuss with patients - and, at its more commercial edges, a wellness-marketing machine selling underpowered evidence with confident branding. Both things are true of the same online spaces, often the same threads. The clinically useful move is not to dismiss the whole category as pseudoscience, since some of what patients are taking (caffeine, creatine for cognition, adequately dosed omega-3s) has genuine if modest support, nor to accept every claim, but to engage with the specific stack in front of you, on its specific evidence and its specific risks.
