## Overview


There is a temptation, in writing about how NZ healthcare compares with other countries, to either oversell or undersell it. The oversell sounds like "NZ has one of the best health systems in the world," which is true on some metrics and badly misleading on others. The undersell sounds like "the system is broken," which is the experience of many patients on a Tuesday afternoon but doesn't survive contact with the international comparative data.

The honest version is more interesting than either. NZ has a system that is structurally unusual - two of its most important agencies, ACC and Pharmac, do things that no comparable country does in the same way. It performs unusually well on some standard measures of comparative health system performance, and unusually badly on others. It has just been through the largest structural reform in two decades, parts of which were reversed within twenty-four months. It funds primary care in a way that has been adequate for a long time and is becoming inadequate.

This piece is a guided tour of the system's structure, politics, and performance. The aim is descriptive and comparative rather than polemical, though the equity findings at the end are hard to read calmly.

## Part I. The structural shape

The NZ health system is, broadly, a tax-funded public system with a substantial private-delivery layer in primary care. About 80% of all health expenditure is publicly financed.1 Public hospitals are free at the point of service for citizens, residents, and most work-visa holders. Most specialty care is delivered through public hospital systems, with a private hospital sector handling a meaningful share of elective work, particularly orthopaedics, gastroenterology, cardiology, and elective general surgery, for patients with private health insurance or willingness to pay out of pocket.

### Primary care: the mixed funding model

Primary care is structurally different from most comparable systems. NZ general practices are predominantly privately owned (by GPs, by corporate groups, or by Māori and Pacific health providers), funded through a mixture of capitation payments from Health New Zealand via Primary Health Organisations (PHOs), and patient co-payments at the point of consultation.2 The capitation payment is calculated per enrolled patient per year, weighted (until recently) only for age and gender. Co-payments vary substantially by practice, capped at lower levels for Very Low Cost Access (VLCA) practices and Community Services Card holders, but free in some practices for under-14s and over-65s, and are a meaningful financial barrier for working-age patients on lower incomes.

This mixed model sits between the British model (NHS general practice fully publicly funded, free at point of use, GPs as independent contractors) and the Australian model (Medicare bulk-billing or rebates per consultation, mostly private practice). It has the disadvantages of both: the queue management of a capitation system, plus the access barrier of a fee-for-service system. It is the single biggest structural weakness when NZ is compared with peer systems.

### Hospital and specialty care

The hospital and specialty side is more conventional. Health New Zealand | Te Whatu Ora, established under the Pae Ora (Healthy Futures) Act 2022, replaced the previous structure of twenty regional District Health Boards with a single national entity. The reform consolidated planning, funding, and accountability at a national level, with the explicit goals of reducing postcode-lottery variation and producing a more equitable distribution of resources.

## Part II. Politics and the system: three pivotal moments

Three political decisions have shaped the current system in ways that are worth explicit acknowledgement.

### 1974: The no-fault accident scheme

The Accident Compensation Corporation, established under the Accident Compensation Act 1972 and operational from April 1974, is a no-fault personal injury insurance scheme that covers everyone in NZ - citizens, residents, work-visa holders, students, tourists - for personal injury caused by accident.3 The scheme covers treatment costs, rehabilitation, and income compensation for inability to work, funded through compulsory levies on employers, earners, motor vehicle owners, and the Crown.

The defining trade-off, set in the original Woodhouse Report of 1967 and embedded in the 1972 Act, is a "social contract": universal no-fault compensation in return for the relinquishment of the right to sue for personal injury.3,4 New Zealanders cannot sue an at-fault party for personal injury damages, except for rare cases of exemplary damages. In return, anyone injured in an accident in NZ - at work, at home, on the road, on a hike, during a medical procedure - receives funded treatment, funded rehabilitation, and (for those who were earning) income compensation, regardless of how the accident occurred or whose fault it was.

The system is unique. No other country has the combination of universal coverage, no-fault basis, and the abolition of the right to sue for personal injury.3,4 This was a political choice, made by a National government in the 1970s, to prioritise collective coverage over individual tort rights. The choice held across multiple changes of government. ACC is now a politically untouchable third rail of the NZ healthcare landscape.

### 1993: Pharmac and the monopsony

The Pharmaceutical Management Agency, established in 1993 and now governed under sections 67-74 of the Pae Ora Act 2022, is NZ's single national pharmaceutical purchaser.5 It manages a capped national budget and decides which medicines and pharmaceutical products are subsidised on the Pharmaceutical Schedule.5 Pharmac uses competitive tendering, sole supply contracts, reference pricing, bundling deals, risk-sharing agreements, and aggressive promotion of generics to extract favourable prices from international pharmaceutical suppliers.6 As a result, NZ pays some of the lowest prices in the world for many medicines.6

This was a political decision to prioritise price discipline over speed of access. The trade-off is real: operating within a fixed annual budget, Pharmac declines or delays funding for medicines whose cost-effectiveness is not favourable relative to existing alternatives. This includes a substantial proportion of newer cancer drugs, many drugs for rare diseases, and a number of medications widely funded in comparable systems. Coverage of cancer drugs in NZ has lagged Australia, the UK, and most European systems by a significant margin. Some of this gap has narrowed in recent years through targeted funding initiatives, but the structural gap persists.

### 2022–2024: The DHB merger and the reversal

The Pae Ora Act 2022 consolidated twenty District Health Boards into a single national entity, Health New Zealand | Te Whatu Ora. Simultaneously, it established Te Aka Whai Ora, a separate Māori Health Authority with its own commissioning budget and mandate to serve Māori populations.

This was a political decision, made by a Labour government, to address both system fragmentation (the postcode-lottery problem of twenty separate DHBs) and systemic inequity (the creation of a parallel Māori commissioning structure). The vision was elegant: one system for planning and delivery, a parallel structure specifically accountable to Māori health outcomes.

The coalition government elected in 2023 had a different political position. In 2024, it passed the Pae Ora (Healthy Futures) (Disestablishment of Maori Health Authority) Amendment Act, which disestablished Te Aka Whai Ora within twenty-four months of its establishment. Its functions were partially absorbed back into Te Whatu Ora and partially distributed elsewhere. The reform thus represented one of the more dramatic short-period structural changes in any developed health system in recent years: a major consolidation, creation of a parallel Māori commissioning structure, followed by disestablishment of that structure.

The political meaning is significant: the coalition government prioritised system unification over institutional accommodation of Māori perspectives. What this does to equity outcomes is still being measured.

## Part III. The distinctive features: ACC and Pharmac

If a New Zealander walks into a foreign health system and tries to use it, the two things they will miss are things almost nobody else has.

### ACC: universal no-fault injury

The defining feature of ACC is universality. Every person in NZ is covered, automatically, for accidental injury. There is no application process, no eligibility check, no waiting for approval. Coverage is instantaneous and unconditional. In most other countries, personal injury compensation is tied to employment status (workplace injury schemes), or to the type of injury (motor vehicle schemes), or requires winning a lawsuit. ACC covers all of these, plus falls at home, plus sports injuries, plus medical complications, plus (since 2022) maternal birth injury.

The administrative efficiency is substantial. Bismark and Paterson's 2006 analysis found administrative costs at approximately 10% of expenditure, compared with 50-60% under typical malpractice tort systems.4 Compensation is delivered faster, to more patients, with substantially less litigation. Patient safety improvements have been more contested. Research has not consistently demonstrated that no-fault systems improve safety more than tort systems do.4 But the overall picture is of a scheme that has become durable and politically untouchable.

The clinical implications for general practice are large. Most accidental injuries are managed within the ACC system rather than through standard health funding. The ACC45 form is a daily piece of NZ general practice. The categories of cover (treatment injury, gradual process injury, mental injury arising from physical injury, sexual abuse cover, maternal birth injury) shape clinical pathways that have no direct equivalent in the systems most international colleagues work in.

### Pharmac: the capped budget and negotiating power

Pharmac operates under a constraint that most countries avoid: a fixed annual budget. Every year, Pharmac receives an allocation of government funding. Within that fixed amount, it must decide which medicines to fund, and at what price it will purchase them. If a new drug costs more than the budget allows, something else must be defunded or access must be delayed. The budget is not adjusted if new medicines appear or costs increase. The constraint is absolute.

This constraint, combined with Pharmac's monopsony power (it is the only purchaser of subsidised medicines in NZ), gives Pharmac extraordinary negotiating leverage. Pharmaceutical companies can either accept Pharmac's price or have no subsidised access to the 5 million-person NZ market. For many drugs, NZ's negotiated price is substantially lower than the US price, the Australian price, or the UK price. This is why NZ healthcare is relatively inexpensive (we spend less than 10% of GDP on healthcare, compared with 17% in the US).

The trade-off is access. A cancer drug that costs more than Pharmac's threshold, relative to its benefit over existing alternatives, does not get funded. An expensive rare-disease treatment does not get funded. A newer diabetes drug, if its cost-effectiveness is marginal, may face a years-long delay before funding is approved. The equity implication is significant: patients who can afford to pay out-of-pocket can access these drugs. Patients who cannot afford to pay go without. This creates a system where access to some newer treatments is correlated with income.

## Part IV. International comparison: what the data show

The most-cited comparative analysis is the Commonwealth Fund's *Mirror, Mirror* series, which since 2004 has compared the performance of high-income health systems across access to care, care process, administrative efficiency, equity, and health outcomes. The 2024 edition compared ten countries: Australia, Canada, France, Germany, the Netherlands, NZ, Sweden, Switzerland, the UK, and the US.8

NZ ranked 4th overall out of the ten countries.8 The top three were Australia, the Netherlands, and the UK. The US was last by a substantial margin.

### The domain-level findings

**Care Process (1st):** NZ ranked first on a domain that includes prevention, safe care, coordination, patient engagement, and sensitivity to patient preferences.8 This is a striking result. The category captures most of what defines the experience of being a patient in a primary-care-led system: continuity of care, coordination between providers, preventive activity built into the consultation, the responsiveness of the clinical encounter to what the patient is actually concerned about. NZ general practice, despite the consultation-length pressure, is rated as delivering this domain better than any comparison system.

**Equity (tied last):** NZ tied with the US for worst on Equity.8,9 The Commonwealth Fund's equity domain captures differential access and experience by income: whether lower-income people report being unable to afford needed care, whether patients report unfair treatment or having concerns dismissed, whether physicians report patients being treated unfairly because of race or ethnicity. NZ's performance on this domain was consistently poor, with substantial gaps between higher- and lower-income access and experience, and significant levels of reported discrimination.9 Among the ten countries compared, only the US scored worse. On some specific physician-reported measures of patient discrimination, NZ scored worse than the US.9

**Health Outcomes:** NZ placed in the upper-middle of the comparison group on measures including life expectancy and avoidable mortality.

**Access to Care and Administrative Efficiency:** NZ placed broadly mid-range across the comparison.

### The paradox: first on care process, last on equity

The combination is striking: first on Care Process, tied last on Equity. This reflects something true about NZ general practice that most local clinicians would recognise. The system is, on the whole, well-organised for the patients who are within it: continuity of care is good, coordination works, the relationship is the centre of gravity. But the system has substantial difficulty with the access end of the equation: the people who are not in the system, who cannot afford the co-payment, who do not engage with primary care until the disease is advanced, who experience the encounter when it happens as not taking them seriously.

The 2024 Commonwealth Fund finding is that this is not a small problem. It is a problem on the scale of the worst-performing high-income comparator.

## Part V. The working position

A few things that are defensible.

**On ACC and Pharmac:** Both are durable successes. ACC delivers universality and administrative efficiency; Pharmac delivers pricing discipline. Both come with real and visible trade-offs, but these are trade-offs built into the design, not failures of execution. The political consensus around both has been broadly stable across changes of government.

**On the 2022-2024 reforms:** The consolidation of twenty DHBs into Health NZ was structurally sensible and has reduced postcode-lottery variation. The creation and rapid disestablishment of Te Aka Whai Ora was a setback for parallel Māori commissioning. The equity findings in the Commonwealth Fund 2024 data suggest that whatever replaces it has substantial work to do.

**On primary care funding:** The capitation-plus-co-payment model is the structural weak point of the NZ system. It is the part of the design that produces the equity findings. The 2025 capitation reweighting, including for the first time weighting for multimorbidity, rurality, and deprivation, is the most important intervention in this space in two decades.

**On the comparative picture:** NZ is, by international standards, a better-performing health system than most New Zealanders give it credit for, and a worse-performing one on equity than most New Zealanders are aware of. The first-place ranking on Care Process is real and worth defending. The tied-last ranking on Equity is real and worth taking very seriously.

The work of comparing health systems internationally is not, in the end, about ranking. It is about identifying what each country does that other countries can learn from. NZ's contributions to the global stock - the no-fault accident scheme of 1974, the monopsony pharmaceutical purchasing model of 1993, the integrated primary care framework that produces the Commonwealth Fund's top Care Process score - are real and underappreciated. NZ's deficits - the equity gap, the workforce shortfall, the structural co-payment barrier in primary care - are also real.

The next decade of system reform should be measured against progress on these deficits, not against maintaining the status quo.
