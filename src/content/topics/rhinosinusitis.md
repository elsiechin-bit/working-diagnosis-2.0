#### EPOS 2020 diagnostic criteria
        Diagnosis is clinical. Symptoms must be present for >5 days and 38°C) OR
          - Elevated inflammatory markers (ESR, CRP) OR
          - Purulent nasal exudate on examination.
        
        #### Severity grading
        
          **Mild:** Symptoms present but do not significantly impact daily activities.
          **Moderate:** Clearly impacts sleep, work, social activities.
          **Severe:** Significant functional impairment; consider investigation for complications.

      🧬Viral vs bacterial rhinosinusitis▾
      
        #### Viral rhinosinusitis (90% of acute cases)
        
          **Onset:** 
          **Symptoms:** Nasal congestion, rhinorrhoea (initially clear, may become purulent), mild facial pressure.
          **Resolution:** Self-limited; 80% recover within 7-10 days.
          **Treatment:** Supportive (saline irrigation, decongestants, paracetamol). Antibiotics not indicated.
        
        #### Bacterial rhinosinusitis (10% of acute cases)
        
          **Onset:** Often follows viral URTI; >7 days of symptoms with worsening or no improvement by day 7-10.
          **Features suggesting bacterial:** Fever >38.5°C, severe facial pain, unilateral symptoms, pus in nasal cavity.
          **Common pathogens:** Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae, Moraxella catarrhalis, group A Strep.
          **Treatment:** Antibiotics recommended (amoxicillin-clavulanate first-line). High-dose amoxicillin insufficient due to resistance.
        
        #### When to suspect bacterial superinfection
        
          - Worsening symptoms after 3-5 days of improvement (double-sickening).
          - High fever (>38.5°C) with severe facial pain and purulent nasal discharge.
          - Immunocompromised (diabetes, HIV, chemotherapy).

      📊Imaging▾
      
        #### When NOT to image (routine acute cases)
        
          - Uncomplicated acute rhinosinusitis without red flags.
          - Imaging does not change diagnosis or management in primary care.
          - Plain X-rays (Waters view) no longer recommended (poor sensitivity, overdiagnoses).
        
        #### When to consider imaging
        
          **Recurrent acute rhinosinusitis (>4 episodes/year):** CT to assess sinus anatomy and rule out structural abnormalities.
          **Chronic rhinosinusitis unresponsive to medical therapy:** CT is standard for pre-surgical assessment.
          **Complications suspected:** Orbital cellulitis, meningitis, intracranial abscess. Urgent imaging (CT or MRI).
          **Immunocompromised patient with fever and sinusitis:** CT to assess for invasive fungal sinusitis.
        
        #### Red flags requiring urgent imaging and referral
        
          - Periorbital oedema, proptosis, ophthalmoplegia (orbital involvement).
          - Severe headache, meningeal signs, altered consciousness (intracranial extension).
          - Facial swelling, forehead bulging (subperiosteal abscess).

      💊Management▾
      
        #### Supportive care (all acute cases)
        
          **Saline nasal irrigation:** Salt water rinse via neti pot or bulb syringe; reduces nasal congestion and promotes drainage. Evidence-based; safe and effective.
          **Nasal decongestants:** Xylometazoline, oxymetazoline spray (max 5-7 days to avoid rebound congestion).
          **Paracetamol or ibuprofen:** For fever and facial pain.
          **Intranasal corticosteroids:** Fluticasone or mometasone spray; modest benefit for symptom relief; secondary to antibiotics if bacterial.
        
        #### Antibiotics (bacterial rhinosinusitis or no improvement by day 7-10)
        
          **First-line:** Amoxicillin-clavulanate (Augmentin) 875/125mg BD for 10 days (high-dose amoxicillin + clavulanic acid to cover beta-lactamase producers).
          **Penicillin allergy (non-severe):** Cephalosporin (cefixime or cephalexin) 500mg TDS.
          **IgE-mediated allergy:** Fluoroquinolone (levofloxacin) 500mg daily.
          **Macrolide (if allergy):** Azithromycin 500mg day 1, then 250mg daily (limited efficacy; only if no alternatives).
          **Duration:** 10-14 days.
        
        #### When referral is needed
        
          **Acute complications:** Orbital cellulitis, meningitis, abscess (urgent ENT/ID referral).
          **Recurrent acute rhinosinusitis:** Consider CT and ENT referral for assessment of anatomy (septal deviation, nasal polyps, ostiomeatal complex obstruction).
          **Chronic rhinosinusitis (>12 weeks symptoms):** ENT assessment for endoscopy and possible functional endoscopic sinus surgery (FESS) if medical therapy fails.

      ⏱️Chronic rhinosinusitis (CRS)▾
      
        #### Definition and prevalence
        Symptoms present for >12 weeks. Affects 3-4% of population; significant quality-of-life impact (worse than asthma in some studies).

        #### Types
        
          **CRS without nasal polyps:** 60-70% of cases; usually from recurrent acute infections or structural obstruction.
          **CRS with nasal polyps:** 30-40% of cases; more likely in atopic/asthmatic patients. Polyps are benign but obstruct ostiomeatal complex.
        
        #### Management in primary care
        
          **Saline irrigation:** Daily use; high-volume (200-500mL per irrigation); most effective non-pharmacological treatment.
          **Intranasal corticosteroids:** Fluticasone or mometasone spray daily; reduce inflammation and polyp size.
          **Low-dose long-term antibiotics:** Doxycycline 50-100mg daily for 6-12 weeks; anti-inflammatory effect (not just antimicrobial).
          **Allergy assessment and treatment:** If allergic rhinitis coexists, treat with antihistamines and corticosteroids.
          **Referral to ENT:** If inadequate response to medical therapy; consider endoscopic assessment and FESS.

      ❓Common questions▾
      
        #### Do I need antibiotics for every sinus infection?
        No. 90% of acute cases are viral; most resolve without antibiotics. Reserve antibiotics for cases with high fever, severe facial pain, yellow-green nasal discharge, or no improvement by day 7-10.

        #### What's the difference between allergic rhinitis and sinusitis?
        Allergic rhinitis is inflammation of nasal mucosa (itching, sneezing, clear rhinorrhoea) often triggered by pollen, dust or pet dander. Rhinosinusitis involves paranasal sinuses (facial pain, purulent discharge, fever). Allergic rhinitis can predispose to bacterial sinusitis if ostiomeatal complex obstructed.

        #### Is saline irrigation safe?
        Yes. High-volume saline irrigation is evidence-based and safe. Use isotonic or hypertonic saline. Avoid tap water (risk of amoeba infection, rare but serious). Neti pots effective if used correctly.

        #### When should I refer to ENT?
        Recurrent acute rhinosinusitis (>4 episodes per year), chronic rhinosinusitis unresponsive to medical therapy, nasal polyps, or suspected complications (orbital, intracranial). Urgent referral if orbital or meningeal signs.
