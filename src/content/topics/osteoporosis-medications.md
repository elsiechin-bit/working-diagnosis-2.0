#### How they work
        Inhibit osteoclast-mediated bone resorption. Bisphosphonates are taken up by bone mineral, accumulate in osteoclasts, and trigger apoptosis. Effect is slow but persistent; benefits continue for years after stopping.

        #### Oral bisphosphonates
        
          **Alendronate (Fosamax):** 70mg weekly or 10mg daily. Most common oral agent. Good efficacy for hip, spine, forearm. Takes 3-5 years to show fracture risk reduction.
          **Risedronate (Actonel):** 35mg weekly or 5mg daily. Slightly faster onset than alendronate. May be preferred in some women with GI intolerance.
          **Ibandronate (Boniva):** 150mg monthly oral or 3mg IV every 3 months. Good for convenience; less hip/forearm data than alendronate.
        
        #### IV bisphosphonates
        
          **Zoledronic acid (Aclasta):** 5mg IV annually. Most potent bisphosphonate. Excellent for hip fracture prevention. Consider for poor GI tolerance, malabsorption, or compliance issues. Risk of acute phase reaction on first infusion (fever, myalgia, arthralgia).
        
        #### Dosing and administration
        
          - Oral bisphosphonates must be taken on empty stomach with plain water, 30 minutes before food.
          - Remain upright for 30 minutes (alendronate/risedronate) or 1 hour (ibandronate) to avoid reflux and oesophageal injury.
          - IV zoledronic acid given as single annual infusion; pre-treat with paracetamol/ibuprofen if first infusion.
        
        #### Efficacy
        
          - Hip fracture risk reduction: 35-40%
          - Spine fracture risk reduction: 45-70%
          - Non-vertebral fracture risk reduction: 20-25%
          - Increases BMD by 5-8% at hip, 8-12% at spine over 3 years.
        
        #### Adverse effects
        
          **Gastrointestinal:** Reflux, dysphagia, abdominal pain, nausea. More common with oral formulations; can be severe if taken improperly.
          **Acute phase reaction (IV):** Fever, myalgia, arthralgia 24-72 hours post-infusion; self-limited, preventable with NSAIDs.

          **Osteonecrosis of jaw:** Rare in osteoporosis patients (incidence ~1-5 per 10,000 person-years); higher with IV bisphosphonates and dental procedures. Risk factors: dental surgery, poor hygiene, immunosuppression.
          **Hypocalcaemia:** Rare but serious if untreated; ensure adequate calcium/vitamin D intake.
        
        #### Duration of therapy
        Standard duration 3-5 years. After 5 years, consider a 'drug holiday' to assess for loss of efficacy or accumulation. Some patients may need only 3 years if very high baseline fracture risk is reduced. Bone density benefits persist 5-8 years after stopping.

      ⚕️Hormone replacement therapy (HRT)▾
      
        #### Overview
        Oestrogen and testosterone both support bone formation and inhibit resorption. HRT is effective for osteoporosis prevention and treatment, but cardiovascular and breast cancer risks limit long-term use in many women.

        #### Oestrogen replacement
        
          **Efficacy:** 30-40% reduction in hip/vertebral fractures. Increases BMD by 5-10% at hip and spine.
          **Use:** Particularly valuable in early postmenopausal women (within 10 years of menopause) with high fracture risk or vasomotor symptoms.
          **Duration:** Current guidelines suggest using lowest effective dose for shortest duration needed (typically 5-7 years). Long-term use (>5-10 years) increases breast cancer and VTE risk.
          **Combinations:** Oestrogen + progestin reduces endometrial cancer risk in women with intact uterus.

      🧬Biological agents (RANKL inhibitors and PTH analogs)▾
      
        #### Denosumab (Prolia)
        Monoclonal antibody against RANKL (receptor activator of nuclear factor kappa-B ligand), a key osteoclast activator.

          **Dosing:** 60mg SC every 6 months (1-2 minute infusion).
          **Efficacy:** Similar to zoledronic acid; 35-40% hip fracture risk reduction, 70% spine fracture reduction.
          **Advantages:** SC injection, twice yearly; renal function-independent; effective in CKD.
          **Disadvantages:** Hypocalcaemia (must ensure adequate calcium/vitamin D), hypophosphataemia. RANKL inhibition extends indefinitely after stopping (unlike bisphosphonates); must ensure adequate calcium or risk severe hypocalcaemia.
          **Cost:** Expensive; often requires prior auth in public systems.
        
        #### Teriparatide (Forsteo)
        PTH1-34 (N-terminal fragment of parathyroid hormone); anabolic agent that stimulates osteoblasts and bone formation.

          **Dosing:** 20 micrograms SC daily.
          **Efficacy:** Fastest bone density gains; 65-69% spine fracture reduction, 53% hip fracture reduction.
          **Advantages:** Most potent for bone formation; approved for high-risk patients (prior fractures, high FRAX scores).
          **Disadvantages:** Daily injection; transient hypercalcaemia and hyperuricaemia; limited duration (max 24 months due to rodent studies showing osteosarcoma risk; not observed in humans but conservative).
          **Cost:** Very expensive; typically second-line after bisphosphonates.
        
        #### Abaloparatide (Tymlos)
        PTH1-34 analog; similar to teriparatide but possibly faster acting and with lower hypercalcaemia risk.

          - Daily SC injection; 80 micrograms.
          - Similar efficacy to teriparatide; limited long-term fracture data.
          - Max 24 months duration (same rodent osteosarcoma concern).

      🥛Calcium and vitamin D▾
      
        #### Calcium supplementation
        
          **Recommended intake:** 1000-1200mg daily for adults (diet + supplements).
          **Forms:** Calcium carbonate (requires acid; take with food) or calcium citrate (absorbed in neutral pH; take anytime).
          **Absorption:** Max 500mg per dose; split intake if >500mg daily.
          **Note:** Calcium alone does not reduce fracture risk; requires adequate vitamin D.
        
        #### Vitamin D3
        
          **Recommended:** 800-1000 IU daily minimum; some recommend 1000-2000 IU for high-risk patients.
          **Target 25-OHD:** >75 nmol/L (30 ng/mL) for bone health.
          **Supplementation:** Particularly important in winter months, for dark-skinned individuals, or those with limited sun exposure.
          **Combined calcium + vitamin D:** Modest 10-15% fracture risk reduction; essential background for all osteoporosis therapies.

      ⚖️Drug selection and monitoring▾
      
        #### FRAX scores and treatment thresholds
        Use FRAX (Fracture Risk Assessment Tool) to identify patients needing treatment. Treatment thresholds vary by age and country; generally treat if 10-year fracture risk >10% (major osteoporotic fracture) or hip fracture >3%.

        #### First-line choice
        
          **Postmenopausal women:** Oral alendronate or risedronate; IV zoledronic acid if GI intolerance or compliance issues.
          **Men:** Same as women; investigate secondary causes of osteoporosis.
          **Glucocorticoid-induced:** Start bisphosphonate + adequate calcium/vitamin D at doses ≥7.5mg prednisolone daily or expected >3 months; some use IV bisphosphonate or denosumab.
          **CKD:** Avoid IV bisphosphonates if eGFR <30; consider denosumab or anabolic agents.
        
        #### Response assessment
        
          - Recheck DXA after 2 years on treatment; expect >2-3% BMD increase at hip or spine (or no further decline if osteoporotic baseline).
          - Poor responders may benefit from switch to alternative agent (e.g., denosumab or PTH analog).
          - CTX and P1NP (bone turnover markers) can assess compliance and early response; not routine.
        
        #### Long-term management
        
          - After 3-5 years bisphosphonate therapy, consider a drug holiday (2-3 years) if fracture risk has reduced and bone density is stable.
          - Restart if bone density declines or fracture occurs during holiday.
          - For denosumab, discontinuation risks rapid bone loss and severe hypocalcaemia; transition to bisphosphonate or continue therapy.

      ❓Common questions▾
      
        #### How long do I take these drugs?
        Standard duration is 3-5 years for bisphosphonates. After that, consider a drug holiday if bone density is stable and fracture risk has reduced. For denosumab, stopping carries risk of rapid bone loss, so switch to bisphosphonate or continue indefinitely. PTH analogs max 24 months.

        #### Can I take bisphosphonates and calcium supplements together?
        No. Calcium binds bisphosphonates and prevents absorption. Take bisphosphonate on empty stomach 30 min before food/supplements, then wait 30 min before taking calcium.

        #### What's the risk of jaw necrosis?
        Rare in osteoporosis patients; ~1-5 per 10,000 person-years on oral bisphosphonates. Higher with IV agents and dental procedures. Risk factors: poor dental hygiene, extractions, implants, immunosuppression. Maintain good oral care and inform dentist of bisphosphonate use.

        #### Should I take a drug holiday?
        After 3-5 years of bisphosphonate therapy, a 2-3 year holiday is reasonable if fracture risk has reduced and BMD is stable. Bone benefits persist years after stopping. Restart if BMD declines or fracture occurs.

        #### Can I switch between agents?
        Yes. If intolerant to oral bisphosphonates, switch to IV or denosumab. If inadequate response after 2-3 years, consider denosumab or PTH analog. Always ensure adequate calcium and vitamin D.

        #### Do these drugs work for men?
        Yes, equally well. Alendronate, risedronate, and zoledronic acid all reduce fracture risk in men. Also ensure testosterone not severely low; if so, testosterone replacement may be beneficial.
