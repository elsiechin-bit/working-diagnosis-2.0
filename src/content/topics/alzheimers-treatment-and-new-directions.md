## Overview


For thirty years the honest answer to "what can you actually do for Alzheimer's disease?" was: a little. Two modest classes of symptomatic drug, the careful treatment of everything around the disease, and a great deal of support. That answer is, for the first time, beginning to change - but the change is smaller, costlier, and more complicated than the headlines suggest. This piece sets out what genuinely works today, what the new disease-modifying drugs do and do not deliver, and where the field is heading. It is a companion to the essays on managing early dementia in the community and on the wider epidemiology, history and culture of the condition.

        ## The two questions every treatment must answer

        It helps to keep two distinct questions apart. Does a treatment make the patient *feel or function better now* (symptomatic benefit)? And does it *change the course of the underlying disease* (disease modification)? For three decades we had only the former. The arrival of anti-amyloid antibodies is the first credible claim on the latter - which is why the debate about them matters out of proportion to their modest effect size.

        The **cholinesterase inhibitors** raise synaptic acetylcholine and produce a measurable but modest improvement in cognition and global function in a proportion of patients with mild-to-moderate Alzheimer's disease. They do not alter the trajectory; when stopped, any benefit is lost.1 **Memantine**, an NMDA-receptor antagonist, has a place in moderate-to-severe disease, alone or added to a cholinesterase inhibitor.2 Both are cheap, oral, and available - the workhorses of current practice.

        Around these drugs sits the work that arguably matters more: treating the behavioural and psychological symptoms of dementia with non-pharmacological approaches first and antipsychotics only for severe distress or risk (they raise stroke and death risk); treating depression, pain, constipation, and sensory impairment; rationalising anticholinergic and sedative burden; and managing vascular risk factors. None of this is glamorous, and all of it changes how well a person lives.

        ## The new era: anti-amyloid antibodies

        The disease-modifying story rests on the amyloid hypothesis - the idea that beta-amyloid accumulation drives the disease. After a long run of failures, monoclonal antibodies that clear amyloid from the brain have finally shown they can slow decline in *early* Alzheimer's disease (mild cognitive impairment or mild dementia, with amyloid confirmed on PET or cerebrospinal fluid).

        The benefits are real and were, for the field, a genuine milestone - but they are modest. A roughly one-quarter to one-third slowing of decline over 18 months is a statistical result whose meaning to an individual patient and family is uncertain and probably, at the group level, near the threshold of what a person would notice. Against that sit substantial demands and harms.

          **ARIA - the defining safety problem**
          Amyloid-related imaging abnormalities are the signature toxicity. ARIA-E (vasogenic oedema) and ARIA-H (microhaemorrhage and siderosis) are usually asymptomatic but can be serious and occasionally fatal. Risk is higher in APOE4 carriers, particularly homozygotes, which is why APOE genotyping and a schedule of surveillance MRIs are part of treatment. This is not a drug that can be started and forgotten.

        Put together, the practical reality is a fortnightly or monthly intravenous infusion, mandatory amyloid confirmation before starting, APOE genotyping, repeated monitoring MRIs, real ARIA risk, eligibility confined to early disease, and a very high price. For a small, dispersed health system, the infrastructure and cost questions are as decisive as the clinical ones. In New Zealand these agents are **not currently funded or routinely available**, and even where regulators have approved them, funders such as NICE have declined them on cost-effectiveness grounds.3,4 The honest framing for patients and families asking about "the new Alzheimer's drug" is: real, incremental, not a cure, and for now largely out of reach here.

        ## The quiet revolution: diagnostics

        Arguably the more transformative change is in diagnosis. Amyloid PET and CSF biomarkers already allow Alzheimer's pathology to be confirmed in life. The newer development is **blood-based biomarkers** - plasma phosphorylated tau (notably p-tau217) and amyloid ratios - which perform impressively at detecting Alzheimer's pathology and are far cheaper and more accessible than PET or lumbar puncture.5 A reliable blood test changes the whole shape of the field: it makes accurate diagnosis possible earlier and more equitably, it is essential for selecting and monitoring patients on amyloid-targeting drugs, and it raises its own hard questions about screening people who have no treatment available to them. Diagnostics may reach New Zealand practice well before the antibodies do.

        ## New directions

        The pipeline is the broadest it has ever been, and notably it is no longer all about amyloid.6

          **Anti-tau therapies.** Tau pathology correlates more closely with symptoms than amyloid does. Antibodies, anti-sense oligonucleotides, and aggregation inhibitors targeting tau are in trials - a logical next front if amyloid clearance alone is insufficient.
          **Metabolic and GLP-1 approaches.** GLP-1 receptor agonists (semaglutide and others), repurposed from diabetes and obesity, are in dedicated Alzheimer's trials on the basis of vascular, inflammatory and metabolic effects - results are awaited and could be significant given how widely these drugs are already used.
          **Neuroinflammation and the immune system.** Microglial and inflammatory pathways (including TREM2 and complement) are active targets, reframing Alzheimer's partly as a disorder of the brain's immune response.
          **APOE-directed and genetic strategies.** Approaches aimed at the APOE4 risk gene, and anti-sense and gene therapies for both common and rare familial forms, are in early development.
          **Combination and biomarker-staged treatment.** The likely future is not one drug but combinations matched to a person's biomarker profile and disease stage (the amyloid / tau / neurodegeneration framework), much as oncology evolved.
          **Multidomain prevention.** The FINGER trial showed that a structured programme of diet, exercise, cognitive training and vascular risk management can benefit cognition in at-risk older adults, and successor trials are testing this at scale.7 Coupled with the Lancet Commission's estimate that around 40% of dementia is associated with modifiable risk, prevention remains the most powerful and most equitable lever we have.8

          The pattern across the whole field is worth holding onto: the drugs that modify the disease are arriving, but slowly, expensively, and with modest effect; the diagnostics are improving fast; and the single largest opportunity - risk-factor modification across the life course - is ordinary primary-care work that needs no new technology at all.

        ## A working position for primary care

        For the GP, little of the cutting edge changes next Monday's clinic, but the framing does. Use the symptomatic drugs we have, realistically and with review. Do the unglamorous work around the disease - mood, pain, senses, deprescribing, vascular risk - because it reliably improves lives. Treat prevention as genuine treatment, because the evidence for it is now strong. Be able to answer the "new drug" question honestly: a real but incremental advance, with real harms and costs, not yet available here. And watch the diagnostics, because an accurate, accessible blood test is the change most likely to reach your patients first - and will bring its own questions about what it means to diagnose a disease we still cannot, for most people, treat.
