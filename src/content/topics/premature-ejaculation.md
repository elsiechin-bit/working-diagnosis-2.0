## Overview


PE is defined by three components: **persistent or recurrent ejaculation with minimal stimulation before, on, or shortly after vaginal penetration; inability to control or delay ejaculation; and associated distress to the man or partner.** Time cutoff varies: some guidelines cite 
          EAU 2024 emphasis: distinguish subtype before treatment
          Lifelong PE responds best to pharmacotherapy (SSRIs, dapoxetine). Acquired PE warrants underlying cause investigation (depression screening, medication review, inflammatory markers if prostatitis suspected). Mixed presentations are common: a man with LPE whose anxiety worsens ED/PE in one relationship vs another.

      🔍Assessment & investigations▾
      
        #### History
        
          **Age of onset:** lifelong vs acquired
          **Frequency & pattern:** consistent across partners/situations vs situational (anxiety, new partner, performance pressure)
          **Estimated IELT:** approximate time to ejaculation (rough guide, not diagnostic threshold)
          **Distress level:** self-reported; ask partner separately if possible—perceptions may differ
          **ED overlap:** performance anxiety can drive premature ejaculation in men with borderline erectile function
          **Medications:** SSRIs, stimulants, antipsychotics, alpha-blockers
          **Mood, relationship, stress**
          **Medical history:** diabetes, hypertension, prostatitis, urethritis
        
        #### Validation
        Consider **Premature Ejaculation Diagnostic Tool (PEDT)** or **Premature Ejaculation Profile (PEP)** questionnaire to grade severity and track response. Partners often report different satisfaction levels than men; screening both provides fuller picture.

        #### Investigations
        
          **Fasting glucose, HbA1c, lipids** (if acquired PE or diabetes history)
          **TSH** if thyroid disease suspected
          **Mood screening** (PHQ-9) — depression and anxiety commonly drive acquired PE
          **Prostate exam & urinalysis** if recurrent prostatitis or dysuria
          - No routine testosterone testing for PE alone (testosterone deficiency more linked to low libido than pure PE)

      💊SSRIs & dapoxetine▾
      
        SSRIs are first-line pharmacotherapy. **Mechanism:** increase serotonin signalling via 5-HT2C and 5-HT1A receptors, raising ejaculatory threshold and improving central control. Cochrane meta-analysis (31 RCTs, 8,254 men): SSRI vs placebo showed RR 1.92 for symptom improvement, RR 1.63 for satisfaction with intercourse, and mean IELT increase of 3+ minutes.

        #### Daily SSRIs (off-label)
        
          SSRIDoseEfficacy & notes
          
            Paroxetine20–40 mg dailyMost effective in meta-analyses; best IELT prolongation. Takes 1–2 weeks for effect.
            Sertraline50–100 mg dailyEffective; fewer sexual side effects than paroxetine in some men; slightly lower efficacy.
            Fluoxetine20 mg dailyEffective; longer half-life (may cause delay between stopping and rebound PE).
            Citalopram20 mg dailyEffective; comparable to others.
            Clomipramine (TCA)12.5–25 mg dailyComparable to SSRIs; older agent, anticholinergic side effects more common.

        #### Dapoxetine (Priligy) — on-demand first choice
        **Fast-acting SSRI specifically developed for PE.** Taken 1–3 hours before anticipated intercourse; rapidly absorbed and eliminated (minimal accumulation). **NZ status:** Dapoxetine is the only PE drug specifically approved in NZ (men 18–64 years). Unfunded; private pay only.

          **Efficacy:** 60–70% achieve ≥30% improvement in IELT. On-demand dosing (30 mg or 60 mg) vs daily sertraline shows comparable efficacy with fewer discontinuations due to sexual side effects.
          **Onset:** 1–3 hours; peak effect ~2 hours.
          **Side effects:** nausea (most common, ~10–20%), dizziness, headache, diarrhoea. Mild-to-moderate; low discontinuation rates compared to daily SSRIs.
          **Safety:** No interaction with PDE5i (sildenafil, tadalafil), so can be combined if ED coexists.
        
        #### Optimising SSRI response
        
          **Adequate trial:** 2–4 weeks at full dose before assessing. Maximum effect often takes 6–12 weeks.
          **Couples counselling:** reassure both partners that improved control often comes gradually.
          **Sexual side effects:** If SSRIs themselves cause delayed ejaculation or anorgasmia in a man without PE, switch to dapoxetine on-demand or add topical anesthetic.
          **Switching:** If one SSRI fails, try another—response varies unpredictably.

      🧴Topical anesthetics▾
      
        **Moderately effective** (~40–60% report improvement). Work by reducing glans sensitivity, allowing prolonged stimulation. Meta-analysis of 8 RCTs (860 patients): topical anesthetics (lidocaine-prilocaine cream or spray) delay ejaculation with variable efficacy but minimal systemic side effects.

        #### Agents & application
        
          AgentForm & doseNotes
          
            Lidocaine-prilocaine (EMLA®)2.5% cream; apply 10–15 min beforeEffective; wear condom to avoid partner numbness (risk of reduced sensation for her).
            Lidocaine-prilocaine spray (TEMPE)Aerosol; 7.5 mg per actuationFaster onset; less messy than cream.
            Benzocaine wipe/spray20% benzocaine; apply 3–5 min beforeLess studied; risk of allergic reaction.

        #### Caveats
        
          **Partner communication:** condom use essential; otherwise female partner experiences numbness and may feel less sensation.
          **Combination therapy:** topicals + SSRIs or dapoxetine often more effective than topical alone.
          **Long-term durability:** tolerance may develop; some men rotate between topicals and oral agents.
          **Practical:** requires planning; less suited to spontaneous encounters.

      🏃Behavioural therapies▾
      
        #### Stop-start technique (Semans, 1956)
        Stimulate penis until urge to ejaculate rises, then stop stimulation; resume when urge subsides. Repeat 3–5 cycles before allowing ejaculation. Practised solo initially, then with partner during foreplay/intercourse.

        #### Squeeze technique (Masters & Johnson, 1970)
        Similar to stop-start, but add gentle compression at glans base when urge peaks; pressure reduces arousal. Requires partner cooperation and coordination.

        #### Evidence & reality check
        
          **Moderate, mixed evidence.** 2015 systematic review (521 participants, 10 RCTs): some benefit, but effect sizes modest and long-term durability poor (~25% maintain gains at 3 years).
          **Best when combined with SSRIs or dapoxetine.** Behaviour alone is time-consuming, intrusive during sex, and limited for lifelong PE.
          **Pelvic floor training:** Kegel exercises (contract/relax pelvic floor) show short-term benefit in case series but not superior to SSRIs in head-to-head comparison.
          **Practical barriers:** requires high motivation, partner participation, and consistent practice over weeks; many couples abandon after initial difficulty.

          Behavioral therapy alone for lifelong PE is rarely sufficient
          Present it as a complementary tool, not first-line. Combined with SSRIs/dapoxetine, couples often see faster and more durable improvement. Reserve behaviour-only for men with clear situational/anxiety-driven acquired PE, or those refusing medication.

      🛣️Treatment algorithm & second-line options▾
      
        #### First encounter: subtype & red flags
        
          **Assess subtype (lifelong vs acquired).**
          **Screen for depression, anxiety, relationship conflict** — often treatable and improves PE.
          **Review medications** — stop obvious culprits (stimulants, antipsychotics, alpha-blockers with urinary retention).
          **Check for comorbid ED** — anxiety-driven rushing; treat ED first if present, then reassess PE.
          **Assess distress:** both patient and partner; some couples unconcerned despite short IELT.
        
        #### Treatment stepwise
        **Step 1: Counselling + behaviour (optional)** — education, couples discussion, offer squeeze/stop-start if motivated. Reasonable first step for mild, acquired PE or psychological overlay.

        **Step 2: First-line pharmacotherapy**

          **Dapoxetine (Priligy) on-demand** — preferred if available/affordable. 1–3 hours before sex; 30–60 mg.
          **Daily SSRI** (paroxetine 20–40 mg, sertraline 50–100 mg) — if cost/access favour daily dosing, or man prefers daily to on-demand.
        
        **Step 3: Topical anesthetic** — add if SSRIs/dapoxetine inadequate; usually combined with oral agent for better efficacy.

        **Step 4: PDE5i (if ED coexists)** — tadalafil or sildenafil can reduce performance anxiety and improve erectile rigidity, sometimes improving ejaculatory control.

        **Step 5: Combination therapy** — dapoxetine + topical + behavioural; or SSRI + topical.

        **Step 6: Specialist referral** — urology or psychosexual therapy if multifactorial/refractory.

        #### When SSRIs disappoint
        
          **Inadequate dose or trial length** — ensure paroxetine ≥30 mg daily, sertraline ≥75 mg, for ≥6 weeks.
          **Switch agent** — response varies; 30–40% respond better to paroxetine than sertraline or fluoxetine.
          **Add topical anesthetic** — boosts effect without increasing oral dose.
          **Consider underlying depression/anxiety** — treat concurrently; some cases respond to therapy alone once mood improves.

      💡Clinical pearls & pitfalls▾
      
        #### Pearls
        
          ✦**Distinguish lifelong from acquired.** Lifelong PE is neurobiological and responsive to SSRIs. Acquired PE signals reversible cause (depression, medication, relationship stress, prostatitis).
          ✦**SSRIs work, but slow.** 2–4 weeks minimum; full effect 6–12 weeks. Set expectations with the couple.
          ✦**Paroxetine is most effective.** RR ~2× better ejaculatory delay vs placebo; sertraline slightly less, but still good.
          ✦**Dapoxetine (Priligy) NZ available but unfunded.** On-demand; 1–3 hours before sex. Fast onset, fewer sexual side effects than daily SSRIs.
          ✦**Behavioural therapy alone rarely works long-term.** ~25% sustain gains at 3 years. Combine with medication for better outcomes.
          ✦**Topical anesthetics are adjunctive.** Moderate efficacy; best combined with SSRI/dapoxetine. Require condom; warn partner about numbness.
          ✦**Screen for depression & anxiety.** Often overlooked; treating mood often improves or resolves PE.
          ✦**ED and PE overlap.** Performance anxiety can cause rushing in men with borderline erectile function; treat ED first.
          ✦**Couples matter.** Partner satisfaction and distress differ from man's; involve partner in discussion and celebrate modest gains.
        
        #### Pitfalls
        
          **Skipping the subtype distinction.** Lifelong PE requires pharmacotherapy; acquired PE requires cause-finding first.
          **Giving up too early on SSRIs.** Many men declare failure after 2–3 weeks; ensure 6+ weeks and full dose.
          **Stopping topical anesthetics prematurely.** Some men underestimate efficacy when combined with oral agent; persistence often works.
          **Prescribing behaviour alone for lifelong PE.** False hope; combination therapy is evidence-based.
          **Overlooking ED or depression.** Both confound PE treatment response; address concurrently.
          **Ignoring partner perspective.** Some partners have lower distress than men; clarify both's goals before starting.
          **Forgetting medication review.** Stimulants or alpha-blockers can worsen; eliminate before attributing PE to baseline condition.
