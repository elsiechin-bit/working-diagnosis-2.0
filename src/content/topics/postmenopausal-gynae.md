## Overview


**Menopause marks the end of ovarian hormone production.** Estrogen withdrawal impacts vagina, vulva, lower urinary tract (embryologically related), bone, cardiovascular system, and CNS. Postmenopausal gynecological issues are common but often not discussed; patients may suffer in silence.

        #### Genitourinary syndrome of menopause (GSM)
        **GSM is the modern term replacing "atrophic vaginitis."** Encompasses vaginal, vulvar, and lower urinary tract symptoms due to estrogen deficiency. Affects ~45% postmenopausal women; prevalence increases with age & years post-menopause.

          **Vaginal symptoms:** Dryness, burning, irritation, dyspareunia (painful intercourse), reduced lubrication, shortening/narrowing of vaginal canal, loss of rugae (smooth, pale appearance on exam)
          **Vulvar symptoms:** Vulvovaginal itching, burning, soreness; vulvar dermatitis (lichenification, erythema); labial resorption (loss of fullness)
          **Lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS):** Frequency, urgency, nocturia, dysuria, recurrent UTI (due to urethral atrophy & loss of antimicrobial lactobacilli). Post-void dribbling possible.
          **Sexual dysfunction:** Dyspareunia → reduced sexual desire/arousal; relationship impact; often not volunteered unless directly asked
        
        #### Pathophysiology
        
          **Loss of estrogen receptors:** Vagina, vulva, urethra, bladder trigone are estrogen-sensitive tissues. After menopause, estrogen-dependent cells (lactobacilli, epithelial thickness, glycogen production) decline.

          GSM is progressive if untreated
          Symptoms worsen over years; vaginal fibrosis can permanently reduce capacity if very prolonged estrogen deficiency. Early intervention prevents this; long-term local hormone therapy maintains vaginal health indefinitely.

        #### Other postmenopausal gynecological issues
        
          **Postmenopausal bleeding (PMB):** Any vaginal bleeding ≥12 months after last menstrual period. Red flag; endometrial cancer risk ~5–10% (depending on character of bleeding). Requires investigation (pelvic ultrasound ± endometrial biopsy).
          **Vaginal/vulvar dermatitis:** Lichen sclerosus, contact dermatitis, lichen planus. Often misdiagnosed as "atrophy"; require specific management.
          **Pelvic floor dysfunction & prolapse:** Estrogen maintains collagen; loss → increased prolapse risk, stress incontinence, pelvic pressure symptoms
          **Recurrent UTI:** Estrogen deficiency + urethral atrophy = recurrent infection (≥3 UTI in 12 months, or ≥2 in 6 months). Often attributed to "bad luck"; actually hormone-driven.

      🚨Postmenopausal bleeding: assessment & red flags▾
      
        **Postmenopausal bleeding (PMB) is any vaginal bleeding ≥12 months after last menstrual period.** Endometrial cancer is present in ~5–10% of PMB cases; investigation is mandatory, not optional.

        #### Causes (frequency in PMB populations)
        
          **Endometrial causes (~80–90% of PMB):** Endometrial cancer (~10–20%), endometrial hyperplasia (~20%), endometrial polyps (~20%), endometritis (~5%), intrauterine synechiae/Asherman's syndrome (~2%)
          **Uterine causes (~10%):** Uterine fibroids (leiomyomas), adenomyosis (less common in truly postmenopausal uteri, but possible if HRT used)
          **Cervical causes (~5%):** Cervicitis, cervical polyps, cervical cancer (rare), cervical ectropion
          **Vaginal/vulvar causes (~5%):** Atrophic vaginitis (GSM), vaginal ulcer, vaginal trauma
          **Systemic/medication (~5%):** Anticoagulation (warfarin, DOACs, heparin), tamoxifen (estrogenic effect on endometrium), HRT use
        
        #### Clinical assessment
        
          **Character of bleeding:** Spotting (few drops), light bleeding, heavy bleeding; frequency; duration; associated symptoms (pain, discharge)
          **Timing post-menopause:** Early PMB (10 years) BUT still requires investigation
          **Prior uterine instrumentation or pathology:** D&C, curettage, myomectomy, endometrial ablation, previous polyps—increases hyperplasia/recurrence risk
          **Medications:** HRT, tamoxifen, anticoagulation
          **Pelvic exam:** Inspect external genitalia (atrophy, ulcer, laceration), inspect cervix (lesion, polyp, cervicitis), bimanual exam (uterine size, masses, tenderness)
          **Pap smear status:** If never screened or >5 years since last smear, concurrent cervical cytology prudent (though PMB workup focuses on endometrium)
        
        #### Investigation
        
          **Pelvic ultrasound (transvaginal, ideally):** Gold-standard first-line. Measures endometrial thickness; 
          **Endometrial biopsy:** Office procedure; low risk; histology definitive. Indicated if endometrial thickness ≥5 mm, ultrasound inconclusive, or high suspicion despite thin endometrium.
          **Hysteroscopy:** Allows visualisation of endometrial cavity, directed biopsy, polypectomy. Reserved for cases where imaging inconclusive or therapeutic intervention needed.
          **Endometrial aspiration (Pipelle):** Simpler than formal biopsy; acceptable for clinical decision-making; may not obtain adequate tissue in atrophic uteri.
        
        #### NZ clinical pathway
        
          **Primary care:** Take bleeding history, perform pelvic exam, rule out cervical/vaginal cause, arrange transvaginal ultrasound via radiology
          **Radiology:** Transvaginal ultrasound; if endometrial thickness 
          **Secondary care (gynaecology):** Assess imaging, perform biopsy (hysteroscopic or office), manage polyps/pathology

          Never dismiss PMB as "just atrophy"
          Atrophic vaginitis can cause bleeding (friable epithelium), but endometrial cancer can coexist. Always image. If bleeding from vaginal atrophy confirmed, treat both: local estrogen for atrophy + ensure endometrial pathology excluded.

      💊Local estrogen therapy for GSM & atrophic vaginitis▾
      
        **Local (topical) estrogen is first-line treatment for GSM.** PHARMAC-funded, safe, highly effective, minimal systemic absorption. Not contraindicated in women with estrogen-sensitive malignancy history (ongoing debate, but current evidence supports safety).

        #### Mechanism & efficacy
        
          **Local action:** Estrogen receptors in vaginal epithelium, bladder, urethra bind estradiol → restored epithelial thickness, glycogen production, lactobacilli, lubrication, elastic recoil
          **Systemic absorption:** Minimal (
          **Onset:** Symptomatic improvement within 2–3 weeks; maximal benefit by 12 weeks
          **Efficacy:** ~80–90% of women report improvement in dyspareunia, dryness, vaginal symptoms; recurrent UTI also reduced (~30–50% reduction in UTI episodes)
        
        #### Application technique
        
          **Cream:** Applicator pre-marked; insert high into vagina; avoid intercourse & tampons for 2 hours after application (wash out medication)
          **Pessaries:** Insert nightly; may require smaller insertion device if vaginal stenosis present; remove old pessary before inserting new (weekly check for residue)
          **Frequency adjustment:** Start daily × 2 weeks (loading phase), then reduce to 2–3× weekly for maintenance. Some women require more frequent dosing if symptoms recur; individualise.
        
        #### Safety considerations
        
          **Endometrial safety:** Systemic absorption negligible; no increased endometrial hyperplasia or cancer risk with local therapy. Safe long-term (years to lifetime).
          **Breast cancer history:** Ongoing controversy; most evidence supports safety of local estrogen in women with prior breast cancer (minimal systemic absorption), but some oncologists counsel caution. Shared decision-making appropriate; patient choice valid either way. Alternative if wanted: DHEA, vaginal moisturisers, or systemic therapy if tolerated.
          **Thromboembolism:** Not a concern with local therapy (no systemic absorption)
          **Adverse effects:** Rare; vaginal irritation or discharge if concentration too high; allergic reactions to vehicle (uncommon)
          **Monitoring:** Annual review; assess symptom control & side-effects; reassure that long-term use is safe

      💪Systemic hormone replacement therapy (HRT) for postmenopausal women▾
      
        **Systemic HRT is for menopausal symptoms (hot flushes, night sweats, mood, sleep) + long-term bone/cardiovascular health.** Does not treat localized GSM as effectively as local therapy; often combined with local estrogen for GSM if HRT used for systemic symptoms.

        #### Indications & benefits
        
          **Vasomotor symptoms:** Hot flushes, night sweats; HRT most effective treatment, ~80% efficacy
          **Mood/cognitive symptoms:** Anxiety, depression, "brain fog" in early-mid menopause; HRT improves mood in some
          **Bone health:** HRT slows bone loss; effective fracture prevention if started early (within 5–10 years of menopause); benefit wanes after cessation
          **Cardiovascular:** Early HRT (within 5–10 years of menopause) associated with improved CVD outcomes; late-initiation (>10 years post-menopause) may increase risk
          **Genitourinary:** Systemic HRT improves LUTS somewhat, but local therapy more effective for localised GSM
        
        #### Types of HRT
        
          **Estrogen-only HRT:** For hysterectomised women (no uterus, no endometrial cancer risk). Oral estradiol, transdermal patches, or vaginal formulations for systemic effect.
          **Estrogen + progestin (E+P):** For women with intact uterus (progestin protects endometrium from unopposed estrogen). Continuous combined (daily progestin) or cyclic (monthly withdrawal bleed pattern).
          **Tibolone:** Tissue-selective steroid; mimics estrogen, progestin, androgen effects; alternative if standard HRT not tolerated; NZ availability variable

        #### Contraindications to systemic HRT
        
          **Absolute:** Personal history of VTE/thrombophilia, estrogen-responsive cancer (breast, endometrium, ovarian), active liver disease, migraine with aura (stroke risk)
          **Relative (risk-benefit discussion):** Age >60 at initiation, hypertension (inadequately controlled), diabetes, smoking, family history of CVD/VTE

          **Start dose:** Lowest effective dose; titrate to symptom control. Patches preferred over oral (lower VTE risk, bypass hepatic metabolism).
          **Duration:** Individualise. Vasomotor symptoms typically resolve by 5–10 years; reassess annually; taper gradually (abrupt cessation → flare). Bone protection requires ongoing use; benefits wane post-cessation.
          **Monitoring:** Annual review; blood pressure check; symptom reassessment; risk stratification reassessment if prolonged use planned

      🌡️Non-hormonal options & conservative management▾
      
        #### For GSM & genitourinary symptoms
        
          **Vaginal moisturisers:** Hyaluronic acid or glycerol-based; non-hormonal; used 3× weekly to daily. Modest efficacy for dryness; less effective than estrogen for dyspareunia. PHARMAC-funded: Hyalo Gyn (hyaluronic acid), available OTC or via script.
          **Vaginal lubricants (non-hormonal):** Water-based (Replens), silicone-based (Eros); used as-needed during intercourse. Short-acting (hours); useful adjunct to hormone therapy or standalone for mild symptoms. Non-absorbable; safe but less effective than local estrogen long-term.
          **Selective estrogen receptor modulators (SERMs):** Ospemifene (oral) is ER-selective; improves dyspareunia & vaginal dryness; NZ access limited (not routine PHARMAC funding); consider if local therapy not tolerated or hormonal therapy contraindicated.
          **Vaginal DHEA (Intrarosa/Prasterone):** Metabolised locally to estrogen & testosterone; once-nightly insert; efficacy similar to estradiol cream/pessaries; emerging; NZ funding variable.
        
        #### For recurrent UTI (GSM-related)
        
          **Local estrogen:** Restores urethral mucosa & lactobacilli; ~30–50% reduction in UTI episodes; first-line if GSM present
          **Prophylactic antibiotics (long-term low-dose):** Cefalexin 125 mg nocte or trimethoprim 100 mg nocte for recurrent UTI (≥3 in 12 months). Used 3–6 months; escalate if UTI recurs after cessation. Less effective than local estrogen for hormone-driven recurrence, but option if estrogen declined.
          **Cranberry products:** Mixed evidence; not recommended as sole therapy; adjunct possible for some women
          **D-mannose:** Limited evidence; oral powder; not PHARMAC-funded; modest benefit for prevention in some studies
          **Pelvic floor physical therapy:** For stress incontinence related to prolapse or pelvic floor weakness; coordinated with local estrogen improves outcomes
        
        #### Lifestyle measures
        
          **Regular sexual activity:** Improves vaginal blood flow & lubrication; protective effect against GSM progression
          **Adequate hydration:** Supports systemic & genital hydration; general health benefit
          **Weight management:** Obesity associated with higher estrogen deficiency symptoms; modest weight loss may improve symptoms
          **Smoking cessation:** Smoking impairs vaginal blood flow & collagen; cessation improves GSM symptoms
          **Stress reduction & sleep:** Supports overall menopausal health; may reduce symptom severity

      📍Pelvic floor dysfunction, prolapse & stress incontinence▾
      
        **Estrogen withdrawal weakens pelvic floor support & urethral sphincter function.** Prolapse (uterine, cystocele, rectocele) and stress incontinence often emerge or worsen postmenopausal.

        #### Pelvic floor dysfunction in postmenopause
        
          **Pathophysiology:** Loss of estrogen → collagen loss, reduced elasticity of pelvic ligaments & fascia. Combined with childbirth trauma history, obesity, chronic cough, straining (constipation) → progressive supportive failure.
          **Presentations:** Stress incontinence (leakage with cough, sneeze, exercise), pelvic pressure/heaviness (prolapse symptoms), urinary frequency/urgency (irritable bladder), difficult defecation (posterior compartment involvement)
          **Assessment:** Assess with Valsalva maneuver during speculum/bimanual exam; assess pelvic floor strength (Oxford Grading System); identify compartment(s) involved (anterior cystocele, posterior rectocele, apical uterine prolapse)
        
        #### Management
        
          **Conservative (first-line):** Pelvic floor muscle training (PFMT, Kegel exercises) 3× daily; supervised by physiotherapist preferred (better outcomes than self-directed). Duration 12+ weeks; ~50% improvement in mild-moderate incontinence.
          **Local estrogen:** Restores pelvic floor collagen & elasticity; improves PFMT outcomes; should accompany physical therapy if GSM present
          **Pessaries (mechanical support):** Ring, cube, or shelf pessary; inserted vaginally to support prolapsing structures. Effective symptom relief; non-invasive; alternative to surgery. Requires fit & regular cleaning (user-cleaned or clinician-managed). Contraindicated if active infection or inability to manage hygiene.
          **Surgical repair (apical suspension, anterior/posterior repair):** For severe/bothersome prolapse failing conservative management. Reserved for women with significant bother & realistic expectations; higher success with supportive pelvic floor. NZ access via DHB gynaecology; usually requires significant functional impairment for funding.

      🩺Assessment approach & NZ referral pathway▾
      
        #### Primary care assessment
        
          **Screen for GSM:** Ask about vaginal dryness, dyspareunia, urinary frequency/urgency during routine women's health review (annual check-up, contraception renewal). "Any vaginal dryness or discomfort with intercourse?" simple opener.
          **Postmenopausal bleeding:** Any bleeding warrants investigation; document character, volume, duration; perform pelvic exam (cervical/vaginal source); order transvaginal ultrasound (HealthPathways pathway).
          **Recurrent UTI:** If ≥3 UTI in 12 months postmenopausal, consider GSM-related; trial local estrogen before prophylactic antibiotics. Confirm diagnosis (urinalysis & culture, not empirical treatment).
          **Sexual dysfunction:** Non-judgmental, direct question: "Are you sexually active? Any discomfort with intercourse?" Opens discussion; often not mentioned by patient.
          **Pelvic floor symptoms:** "Any leakage with coughing or exercise? Feeling of heaviness/pressure in pelvis?" Assesses incontinence & prolapse risk.
        
        #### Management initiation (primary care)
        
          **Mild GSM:** Trial vaginal moisturiser (Hyalo Gyn) 3× weekly & lubricant as-needed; review in 4 weeks
          **Moderate-severe GSM or failed conservative:** Prescribe local estrogen (vaginal cream or pessaries); counsel on application, duration, expectations (2–3 weeks onset); follow-up in 6–8 weeks
          **Systemic menopausal symptoms requiring HRT:** Initiate if not contraindicated; discuss with patient; refer to HealthPathways if complex (known VTE, breast cancer history, etc.)
          **Recurrent UTI (GSM-related):** Local estrogen first-line; prophylactic antibiotics if local therapy declined or failed
        
        #### Referral to gynaecology (secondary care)
        
          **Urgent/same-week:** Postmenopausal bleeding with heavy volume or repeated episodes; abnormal imaging (endometrial thickness ≥5 mm, visible polyp, adnexal mass)
          **Routine (2–4 weeks):** Failed medical management of GSM after 3 months local estrogen + lifestyle; significant pelvic floor symptoms (bothersome prolapse/incontinence); HRT contraindications with severe vasomotor symptoms (consider SERMs or other options)
          **Complex cases:** Prior breast/endometrial cancer with GSM requiring individualised HRT decision; recurrent/suspicious postmenopausal bleeding; pelvic floor failure requiring surgical consideration

          - Menopause and Hormone Therapy pathway: assessment, HRT prescribing, monitoring
          - Postmenopausal Bleeding pathway: investigation algorithm, imaging interpretation
          - Recurrent UTI pathway: diagnosis, GSM assessment, first-line management

      💡Clinical pearls & pitfalls▾
      
        #### Pearls
        
          ✦**Ask about GSM—patients rarely volunteer.** Dyspareunia, dryness, LUTS often silently endured. Direct question unlocks discussion; highly treatable.
          ✦**Local estrogen is PHARMAC-funded, effective, safe.** Vaginal cream/pessaries ease GSM symptoms & reduce recurrent UTI. Safe long-term; minimal systemic absorption. First-line for localized atrophy.
          ✦**Recurrent UTI in postmenopausal woman = consider GSM first.** Before prophylactic antibiotics, trial local estrogen 3–6 months. ~30–50% UTI reduction; sustainable; treats underlying cause, not symptom.
          ✦**Postmenopausal bleeding is endometrial cancer until proven otherwise.** Always image (transvaginal ultrasound). Atrophic vaginitis can bleed, but cannot exclude cancer clinically.
          ✦**HRT timing matters.** Early initiation (
          ✦**Combine local estrogen + pelvic floor exercises for incontinence.** Physical therapy alone modest; estrogen + PFMT synergistic; stronger collagen support + muscle strength = better continence outcomes.
          ✦**Local estrogen safe in breast cancer survivors.** Minimal systemic absorption; no proven increased recurrence risk. Shared decision-making reasonable; patient autonomy supported either way.
        
        #### Pitfalls
        
          **Dismissing dyspareunia as "normal aging."** GSM is treatable; dyspareunia not inevitable. Prescribe local therapy; transforms sexual function & relationship satisfaction.
          **Attributing all postmenopausal LUTS to "overactive bladder."** Estrogen deficiency + urethral atrophy = frequency/urgency. Local estrogen often more effective than antimuscarinics; try before adding anticholinergics (which impair PVR clearance).
          **Using antimuscarinics for GSM-related LUTS without local estrogen.** Antimuscarinics suppress urgency but don't address underlying atrophy. Risk: urinary retention, incomplete efficacy. Combine with local estrogen if using.
          **Empirical prophylactic antibiotics for recurrent UTI without GSM assessment.** Antibiotic resistance develops; local estrogen addresses root cause. Always evaluate for GSM first; trial 3 months before long-term prophylaxis.
          **Prolonged unopposed estrogen (E-only) in women with intact uterus.** Increases endometrial hyperplasia/cancer risk. Always add progestin if uterus present. If using local therapy + systemic E-only HRT, review immediately.
          **Stopping HRT abruptly.** Flare of vasomotor symptoms; bone loss acceleration. Taper gradually; discuss long-term plan upfront; individualise duration.
