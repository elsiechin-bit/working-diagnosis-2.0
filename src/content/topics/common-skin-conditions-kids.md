## Overview


**Paediatric skin conditions are common, mostly benign, and heavily driven by age, immune development, and exposure.** Differentiation between infectious (requiring treatment/exclusion) and non-infectious (reassurance + conservative care) is clinically critical. Many conditions resolve spontaneously; parental anxiety often exceeds clinical concern.

        #### Age-related epidemiology
        
          **Infancy (0–2 years):** Nappy rash, infantile seborrhoeic dermatitis, atopic dermatitis (early-onset eczema), cradle cap, miliaria, candida intertrigo
          **Early childhood (2–5 years):** Atopic dermatitis, viral exanthems (hand-foot-mouth, chickenpox), molluscum contagiosum, warts, impetigo, scabies, fungal infections (tinea corporis)
          **School age (5–12 years):** Warts, molluscum contagiosum (declining), atopic dermatitis (may improve), impetigo, scabies, fungal infections, pityriasis alba, lichen nitidus
          **Adolescence (12+ years):** Acne, atopic dermatitis (may persist or recur), lichen planus, psoriasis (emerging in some)
        
        #### Red flags requiring urgent referral
        
          **Meningococcal sepsis rash:** Non-blanching petechial/purpuric rash; fever, ill appearance; rapid progression (hours). Glass test (does not blanch under pressure). Emergency admission.
          **Severe or widespread cellulitis:** Rapidly spreading erythema, warmth, systemic signs (fever); may indicate Group A Streptococcus (GAS) or Staph aureus; requires IV antibiotics.
          **Severe or atypical chickenpox:** Extensive pustulation, haemorrhagic lesions, signs of secondary infection or complications; immunocompromised child → IV aciclovir
          **Suspected scabies in neonate or immunocompromised child:** Norwegian scabies (crusted) risk; requires systemic treatment & close contacts treated.
          Suspected herpes simplex in neonate ( Disseminated infection risk; requires IV aciclovir & urgent paediatric evaluation

      🔴Atopic dermatitis (eczema) in children▾
      
        **Atopic dermatitis (AD) is the most common chronic inflammatory skin condition in children.** Typically onset **Infants (0–2 years):** Erythematous, oozy patches on face, scalp, extensor surfaces; spares nappy area (moist, occluded environment protective). Often severe, sleep-disrupting due to pruritus.
          **Toddlers/preschoolers (2–5 years):** Flexural predominance (antecubital/popliteal fossae, neck, wrists, ankles); lichenification common; may spare face. Often exacerbated by irritants (rough clothing, fragrance, dust mites).
          **School-age (5+ years):** Usually milder & more localized to flexures; may spontaneously improve in adolescence (~70% resolve by age 12, but 30% persist or worsen)
        
        #### Diagnostic criteria & severity assessment
        
          **Diagnosis:** Pruritus + 3+ of: early onset (
          **Severity grading:** Mild (localized patches, minimal impact on sleep/daily activities), moderate (widespread but controlled with frequent emollients + occasional steroid), severe (extensive, sleep-disrupting, frequent systemic symptoms)
        
        #### First-line management
        
          **Emollients (moisturisers):** Foundation of all AD management. Use 2–4 times daily, especially immediately after bathing (within 3 minutes of drying lightly). Thick ointments (white soft paraffin, Unguentum M) superior to creams but less acceptable cosmetically; compromise: aqueous cream or sorbolene for frequent use.
          **Bathing:** Warm (not hot) water, soap-free wash (aqueous cream, Cetaphil, emollient-based wash); keep baths 5–10 min (prolonged soaking → increased water loss); pat dry gently (not vigorously)
          **Irritant avoidance:** Avoid wool, synthetic fabrics with rough weave; use cotton clothing. Fragrance-free products only; no bubble baths, perfumed soaps. Dust mite reduction: allergen-proof mattress covers, frequent washing of bedding (hot water).
          **Topical steroids (for active flares):** Hydrocortisone 1% for face/neck/flexures; Class III (e.g., triamcinolone 0.1%) for body in moderate-severe flares. Apply to active inflamed areas 1–2× daily × 1–2 weeks; cease once controlled. Do NOT use on face >2 weeks (atrophy risk).
          **Topical calcineurin inhibitors (tacrolimus, pimecrolimus):** Steroid-sparing option; useful for face/neck (avoid steroid atrophy). Tacrolimus 0.03% (children 2+ years) or 0.1% (older children); apply 2× daily until clear, then reduce frequency. Caution: transient burning/stinging common initially; black-box warning (theoretical malignancy risk; evidence lacking).

          Regular emollient use reduces flare frequency & steroid need
          Many parents under-use emollients, assuming child will "outgrow" AD with time. Emphasise that daily barrier repair prevents flares; reduces systemic steroid exposure; key to long-term control.

        #### When to refer to dermatology
        
          - Severe AD uncontrolled on optimal first-line therapy (frequent flares, severe sleep disruption, significant impact on quality of life)
          - Suspected secondary infection (pustulation, ooze, lymphadenopathy)
          - Diagnostic uncertainty or atypical presentation
          - Consideration of systemic therapy (dupilumab in severe childhood AD; NZ access variable)

      🐚Nappy rash (diaper dermatitis)▾
      
        **Nappy rash is the most common dermatitis in infants & toddlers.** Irritant contact dermatitis from prolonged moisture, friction, ammonia (urine), enzymes (stool). Usually mild & self-limiting with improved nappy hygiene; candida infection possible if prolonged/severe.

        #### Pathophysiology
        
          **Irritant dermatitis:** Occluded skin in nappy area → maceration; repeated wetting/drying → barrier disruption; prolonged urine/stool contact → irritation. Friction from nappy edges → erythema at borders.
          **Candida superinfection:** Warm, moist nappy environment favours Candida albicans; risk if antibiotic use (oral antibiotics → dysbiosis), if irritant rash prolonged/severe, or if baby has oral thrush.
        
        #### Clinical assessment
        
          **Irritant dermatitis:** Erythema, papules in nappy area (sparing skin folds); sharply demarcated; worst over pressure areas (buttocks, genitals); often painful rather than pruritic
          **Candida nappy rash:** Erythema with satellite pustules/macules extending beyond main rash area; often involves skin folds (groin, inguinal creases); shiny, beefy-red appearance; may have associated oral thrush (white plaques on tongue)
        
        #### Management
        
          **Nappy hygiene & barrier repair:** Change nappies frequently (every 2–3 hours, immediately after soiling); air dry skin between changes (leave nappy off when feasible); use fragrance-free wipes or cotton wool + water
          **Emollient barrier:** Apply zinc and castor oil cream (or equivalent; PHARMAC-funded products available) at each nappy change. Creates moisture barrier; gentle; suitable for sensitive skin.
          **For irritant dermatitis:** Increase nappy changes + barrier cream; usually resolves within 3–5 days. Avoid powder (increases irritation); avoid occlusive plastic pants (traps moisture).
          **For suspected candida:** Topical antifungal cream (miconazole or clotrimazole); apply 2–3× daily × 7–14 days. If oral thrush present, treat concurrent with miconazole or nystatin oral gel. Ensure barrier cream used alongside (candida cream alone insufficient).
          **Topical steroids not recommended:** May promote candida overgrowth; risk of atrophy with prolonged use in nappy area. Reserve for severe irritant dermatitis unresponsive to emollient + nappy care; use hydrocortisone 1% only, short course.

          Oral antibiotic + nappy rash = consider candida
          Antibiotics (amoxicillin, cephalosporins) kill normal flora; Candida overgrows. If baby on antibiotics develops nappy rash, suspect candida; add topical antifungal even if rash looks "irritant."

      🦠Infectious skin conditions: impetigo, ringworm, scabies, molluscum▾
      
        #### Impetigo (bacterial skin infection)
        **Non-bullous impetigo is the most common form.** Highly contagious; caused by Group A Streptococcus (GAS) ± Staph aureus (including MRSA in some regions). Requires antibiotic treatment & school exclusion.

          **Presentation:** Golden crusts on erythematous base; classically on face (around nose/mouth) or limbs. Often follows minor trauma (insect bite, scratch, abrasion). Non-bullous type more common than bullous.
          **Diagnosis:** Clinical; culture if severe, recurrent, or MRSA suspected
          **Treatment:** Topical antibiotics (mupirocin/Bactroban 2% ointment) 3× daily × 5–7 days for localized impetigo (
          **Infection control:** School exclusion until 24–48 hours post-antibiotic initiation (lesions must be covered); close contacts examined (asymptomatic carrier risk)
          **Complications:** Post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis (~1–5% if untreated; lower with treatment); acute rheumatic fever rare in NZ (antibiotics reduce risk).
        
        #### Fungal infections (tinea corporis, tinea capitis)
        **Ringworm is common in school-age children; animal exposure (cats, dogs) common source.** Name is misleading; caused by dermatophytes, not worms.

          **Tinea corporis (body):** Scaly, erythematous annular patches with raised borders; often multiple; mildly pruritic. Diagnosis: KOH prep (microscopy of scale) or clinical. Treatment: topical antifungal (terbinafine, azole creams) 1–2× daily × 2–4 weeks. PHARMAC-funded creams available.
          **Tinea capitis (scalp):** Scaly patches on scalp; may be kerion (boggy, pustular inflammatory response). Topical treatment alone insufficient (poor penetration to hair shaft). Systemic antifungal required: griseofulvin or terbinafine (oral suspension) × 4–8 weeks depending on agent & organism. School exclusion not needed if systemically treated.
          **Differential (pityriasis alba):** Hypopigmented scaly patches; non-infectious; common in darker-skinned children; self-limiting; emollient reassuring; no antifungal needed
        
        #### Scabies (Sarcoptes scabiei mite infestation)
        **Highly contagious; spread by skin-to-skin contact.** Pruritic tunnels/burrows caused by mite burrowing through stratum corneum. Systemic treatment required for all family members simultaneously.

          **Presentation:** Intense pruritus (worse at night); burrows (linear, whitish) on wrists, fingers, between toes, genitals, axillae. Papules, secondary excoriation common. Young children may have nodular scabies (inflamed nodules on genitals, axillae).
          **Diagnosis:** Clinical; mite seen on dermoscopy or KOH prep if available; often diagnosed presumptively if strong clinical suspicion + pruritic family members
          **Treatment:** Permethrin 5% cream (or benzyl benzoate 10–25%) applied whole-body (neck down, including genitals, under nails) × 1 application, repeat 1–2 weeks later. Apply after bathing to moist skin; leave on 8–14 hours. Alternatives: oral ivermectin (for Norwegian/crusted scabies or inability to apply topical). ALL close contacts treated simultaneously to prevent re-infestation.
          **School exclusion:** Until 24 hours post-treatment initiation
          **Pruritus duration:** Itch may persist 2–4 weeks post-successful treatment (immune reaction to dead mites); reassure parents. If worsening after 2 weeks, suspect re-infestation or treatment failure (re-examine, re-treat).
        
        #### Molluscum contagiosum (viral infection)
        **Common in preschool/school-age children; caused by poxvirus.** Self-limiting; spreads by direct contact or autoinoculation. Highly contagious but benign; often resolves spontaneously over 6–12 months without treatment.

          **Presentation:** Flesh-coloured, dome-shaped papules 2–5 mm; central dimple (umbilication); often clustered on trunk, arms, face; mildly pruritic or asymptomatic. Secondary eczema/"molluscum dermatitis" common if child scratches.
          **Diagnosis:** Clinical; if atypical, KOH or dermoscopy confirms
          **Management:** Reassurance + watchful waiting most appropriate (resolves spontaneously in >90% within 12 months). Treatment options if bothersome: cryotherapy (liquid nitrogen), curettage (with local anaesthetic), imiquimod (topical immune stimulant). Avoid trauma/scratching (secondary infection risk). School exclusion NOT required.
          **When to treat:** Large/numerous lesions causing distress, secondary infection, or concern about spread in close contacts (e.g., sibling with eczema at high infection risk). Discuss watchful waiting first.

      🦠Viral exanthems: hand-foot-mouth, chickenpox, measles▾
      
        #### Hand-foot-mouth disease (HFM)
        **Caused by enterovirus (Coxsackievirus A16, Enterovirus 71).** Highly contagious; outbreaks common in preschools. Self-limiting; usually mild; complications rare in immunocompetent children.

          **Presentation:** Fever 1–2 days, then characteristic vesicles on hands (palms), feet (soles), mouth (painful oral ulcers on tongue/soft palate); may also have rash on buttocks, genitals. Often painful (children refuse to eat/drink).
          **Duration:** 7–10 days; usually resolves without treatment
          **Management:** Supportive (fluids, analgesia—paracetamol/ibuprofen); exclude from preschool/school until fever resolves & lesions crusted (typically 5–7 days)
          **Complications:** Dehydration (mouth pain); rarely: viral meningitis, myocarditis (Enterovirus 71). Red flag: fever >3 days, drowsiness, severe lethargy → consider meningitis; LP if suspected.
        
        #### Varicella (chickenpox)
        **Caused by varicella-zoster virus (VZV).** Highly contagious (R₀ 10–12); rare in vaccinated populations (NZ childhood vaccination ~90% coverage).

          **Presentation:** Prodrome (fever, malaise) 1–2 days, then classic "crops" of vesicles in centripetal distribution (trunk > extremities, face). Vesicles break down to pustules → crusts over 7–10 days. Intensely pruritic.
          **Diagnosis:** Clinical; occasionally confirmed by Tzanck smear or PCR if atypical
          **Management:** Supportive care (paracetamol, cool baths with bicarbonate soda, calamine, antihistamines for itch); aciclovir NOT routinely recommended for immunocompetent children (minimal efficacy if started >24 hours post-rash). Reserved for: immunocompromised, severe varicella (extensive, haemorrhagic), secondary infection with systemic signs.
          **Exclusion:** Until all lesions crusted (typically 5–7 days post-onset)
          **Complications:** Secondary bacterial infection (GAS, Staph—if spreading erythema/systemic signs, treat with antibiotics), varicella pneumonitis (rare, usually in adolescents/adults), encephalitis (very rare)
          **Post-exposure prophylaxis:** Varicella-zoster immunoglobulin (VZIG) or aciclovir for immunocompromised contacts within 96 hours of exposure
        
        #### Measles (rubeola)
        **Rare in NZ due to MMR vaccination.** Highly contagious; serious complications possible (pneumonia, encephalitis). Notify Public Health if suspected.

          **Presentation:** Prodrome (high fever, cough, coryza—"3Cs") × 3–4 days, then Koplik spots (white spots on buccal mucosa), followed by maculopapular rash starting on face/hairline, spreading downward over trunk & limbs
          **Diagnosis:** Clinical; confirm with serology or viral PCR if outbreak suspected
          **Management:** Supportive; vitamin A supplementation (WHO guideline; reduces morbidity/mortality); exclude until 4 days post-rash onset

      🪴Warts and verrucae (viral infection)▾
      
        **Warts are benign, self-limiting infections caused by human papillomavirus (HPV).** Common in children; spread by skin-to-skin contact or autoinoculation. Most resolve spontaneously within 2–3 years without treatment.

        #### Types & presentation
        
          **Common warts (verruca vulgaris):** Firm, dome-shaped, flesh-coloured papules with hyperkeratotic surface; usually on hands, fingers, knees. May be single or multiple; slow growth over weeks–months.
          **Plantar warts (verruca pedis):** On sole of foot; often flattened due to pressure; may cause pain on weight-bearing; often mistaken for corn or callus
          **Flat warts (verruca plana):** Smooth, flat-topped, skin-coloured or slightly pigmented; often on face, neck, hands; less common
        
        #### Diagnosis & natural history
        
          **Diagnosis:** Clinical; if atypical, dermoscopy shows characteristic punctate vessels (black dots in dermoscopy)
          **Natural history:** ~30% resolve spontaneously within 1 year, ~70% within 3 years. Immune response gradually clears HPV; warts do not leave scars unless treated.
          **Contagion:** Spread by direct contact; autoinoculation common (scratching spreads virus)
        
        #### Management approach
        
          **Watchful waiting (preferred):** Most warts resolve spontaneously; treatment does not accelerate clearance & risks scarring. Recommend avoiding trauma/scratching; cover with plaster if in high-contact areas (school).
          **When to treat:** Large/multiple warts causing functional impairment (pain, difficulty grasping), psychological distress, or rapid growth. Treatment modalities: topical salicylic acid (15–17% solution or ointment, daily × weeks–months), imiquimod (topical immune stimulant), cryotherapy (liquid nitrogen freezing, may require multiple sessions), curettage/laser (reserved for resistant cases, risk of scarring).
          **Topical salicylic acid (PHARMAC-funded):** Apply daily after softening wart in warm water × 4–12 weeks. Irritation common; monitor for excessive erythema. Remove dead tissue weekly with pumice. Efficacy ~50% over 12 weeks with persistence.
          **Cryotherapy:** Rapid freezing with liquid nitrogen; painful; may require multiple sessions; efficacy ~60–70%; risk of blister/scarring if aggressive

          Reassure: warts are benign & self-resolving
          Childhood warts do not become cancer (HPV types associated with warts ≠ malignant types). Emphasise that watchful waiting is safe & often most effective; treatment is optional & has risks (pain, scarring, recurrence).

      💡Clinical pearls & pitfalls▾
      
        #### Pearls
        
          ✦**Emollients are the foundation of childhood eczema.** More important than steroids. Regular use prevents flares & reduces need for treatment. Parent education on generous, frequent application transformative.
          ✦**Most self-limited viral & parasitic rashes resolve without specific treatment.** Molluscum, warts, mild varicella, HFM: watchful waiting often best. Avoid unnecessary treatment that parents perceive as "fast-acting" but carries scarring/complication risk.
          ✦**Impetigo requires antibiotics & school exclusion.** Golden crusts = impetigo until proven otherwise. Topical mupirocin or systemic antibiotic; ensure family aware of contagion & exclusion period.
          ✦**Nappy rash + poor response to barrier cream = consider candida.** Treat both irritant dermatitis (nappy care, emollient) + fungal component (topical antifungal) if satellite lesions or skin fold involvement.
          ✦**Scabies = whole family treatment simultaneously.** Do not treat child alone. All close household contacts treated on same day, even if asymptomatic. Launder clothing/bedding in hot water.
          ✦**Hydrocortisone 1% is safe for face/intertriginous areas in children.** Higher potencies risk atrophy. Reserve potent steroids (Class III) for body, short courses only, & step-down as soon as control achieved.
          ✦**Oral antibiotic + new rash = drug reaction or candida.** Amoxicillin/cephalexin → amoxicillin rash (benign, self-resolving if continue antibiotic) or candida (Candida overgrowth post-antibiotic, requires antifungal treatment). Distinguish by timing & distribution.
        
        #### Pitfalls
        
          **Under-prescribing emollients in childhood eczema.** Parents often use small amounts; educate on "two-finger rule" (generous application). Eczema worsens without adequate barrier repair.
          **Using potent steroids on face or prolonged courses in children.** Risks atrophy, striae, telangiectasia. Hydrocortisone 1% or calcineurin inhibitor preferred for face; limit duration.
          **Treating molluscum/warts aggressively.** Most self-resolve; unnecessary treatment causes pain & scarring. Watchful waiting appropriate unless bothersome or spreading rapidly.
          **Missing diagnosis of impetigo because of atypical presentation.** Not all impetigo has golden crusts; pustules, blistering, or non-crusted lesions also impetigo. High index of suspicion if rapid spread or pustules on erythematous base.
          **Empirical candida treatment for all nappy rash.** Most nappy rash is irritant; unnecessary antifungal increases resistance. Reserve for satellite lesions, flexural involvement, or if baby on antibiotics.
          **Failing to exclude infectious skin conditions from school/preschool.** Impetigo, scabies, HFM, varicella require exclusion; molluscum & warts do not. Know guidelines; communicate to parents.
