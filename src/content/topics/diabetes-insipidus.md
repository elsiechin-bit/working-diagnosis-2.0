## Overview


**Terminology note:** "Diabetes insipidus" (DI) now includes three polyuria-polydipsia conditions with overlapping presentations. Updated nomenclature: AVP-D (arginine vasopressin deficiency = central DI) and AVP-R (arginine vasopressin resistance = nephrogenic DI); primary polydipsia is a distinct third entity.

        #### Three polyuria-polydipsia syndromes
        
          **AVP-D (Arginine Vasopressin Deficiency) — Central DI**

          Hypothalamic-pituitary failure to produce or release sufficient ADH (vasopressin). Result: kidneys cannot concentrate urine. Treatable with desmopressin replacement.
          **AVP-R (Arginine Vasopressin Resistance) — Nephrogenic DI**

          Kidneys' distal tubule & collecting duct are resistant to ADH/desmopressin action. Usually acquired (lithium most common). Limited response to desmopressin; symptomatic management focused on NSAIDs, thiazide diuretics, adequate hydration.
          **Primary Polydipsia**

          Excessive drinking (psychogenic or hypothalamic thirst dysregulation) overwhelms normal renal concentrating ability. Opposite of DI: normal ADH, normal kidneys, behavioural/psychiatric driver. Water deprivation leads to high urine osmolality (preserved renal function intact).
        
        #### Pathophysiology: AVP & kidney water reabsorption
        
          **Normal physiology:** Hypothalamus detects plasma osmolality & sets ADH release threshold (~280–295 mOsm/kg). ADH binds V2 receptors in distal tubule/collecting duct → aquaporin-2 (AQP2) channel insertion → water reabsorption
          **AVP-D:** Defective synthesis, storage, or release of ADH from posterior pituitary; V2 receptors & AQP2 channels normal but ADH unavailable
          **AVP-R:** V2 receptor mutation (X-linked, ~90% of genetic cases) or AQP2 defect; ADH present but kidneys cannot respond

      🔍Clinical presentation & initial assessment▾
      
        #### Classic presentation
        
          **Polyuria:** Large-volume dilute urine; typically >3–10 L/day in complete AVP-D, sometimes >20 L/day. Nocturia prominent (wakes multiple times to urinate)
          **Polydipsia:** Intense thirst; preference for cold/iced water; driven by hypernatraemia (high blood sodium)
          **Dehydration risk:** If access to water limited (unconscious, impaired, unable to drink), rapid hypernatraemia & hyperosmolality develops; can cause seizure, altered mental status, death
          **Sleep disruption:** Nocturia often severe; fatigue, quality of life impaired
          **Failure to thrive (infants):** Inadequate fluid intake or hypernatraemia; developmental delay if unrecognized
        
        #### Causes of AVP-D (Central DI)
        
          **Acquired (majority):** Head trauma, neurosurgery (pituitary/sellar procedures), intracranial neoplasm (pituitary adenoma, craniopharyngioma, germinoma), infection (meningitis, encephalitis), granulomatous disease (sarcoidosis, histiocytosis), vascular (aneurysm, infarction), medications (phenytoin, chemotherapy)
          **Genetic (rare):** Familial AVP-D (mutations in AVP gene or processing); present from birth or early childhood
          **Idiopathic:** ~50% of AVP-D cases; autoimmune-mediated destruction suspected
        
        #### Causes of AVP-R (Nephrogenic DI)
        
          **Acquired (most common):** Lithium (40–55% of lithium patients develop NDI; onset weeks to years), NSAIDs (chronic overuse), amphotericin B, demeclocycline, hypercalcemia, hypokalemia, post-obstructive diuresis, chronic kidney disease
          **Genetic:** X-linked V2R mutations (AVPR2 gene; affects males predominantly), autosomal recessive AQP2 mutations

      🧪Diagnostic approach: water deprivation test & interpretation▾
      
        **Water deprivation test is the gold standard** for distinguishing AVP-D, AVP-R, and primary polydipsia. 2024–2025 algorithms emphasize early plasma osmolality & AVP/copeptin measurement for faster diagnosis.

        #### Step 1: Initial labs (baseline, before deprivation)
        
          - Plasma osmolality, sodium, glucose, urea, creatinine
          - Urine osmolality, sodium, glucose, urine dipstick
          - Optional baseline AVP or copeptin (helpful if available; see interpretation below)
        
        #### Step 2: Water deprivation test protocol
        
          **Mild polyuria:** Begin overnight (water restriction from bedtime)
          **Severe polyuria (>10 L/day):** Perform during day for safety monitoring
          **Procedure:** Withhold all water; measure plasma osmolality & urine osmolality hourly; discontinue when:
            
              Urine osmolality plateaus (2 consecutive measurements vary 
              - Body weight drops >3%, OR
              - Plasma sodium rises >150 mmol/L (stop immediately; risk of seizure)

          **Once plateau reached:** Administer desmopressin (5 μg intranasal or 2 μg subcutaneous); measure urine osmolality 1–2 hours later

          ScenarioAfter WDTAfter desmopressinDiagnosis

        #### Copeptin as emerging diagnostic marker
        
          **Copeptin:** C-terminal peptide co-secreted with ADH; more stable than AVP; emerging marker for AVP-D diagnosis
          **High copeptin + high plasma osmolality:** Suggests AVP-R or primary polydipsia
          **Low copeptin + high plasma osmolality:** Consistent with AVP-D
          **Value:** Can help differentiate in ambiguous WDT results; may reduce need for repeat testing

          Safety during water deprivation test
          **Stop immediately if:** Body weight drops >3%, plasma sodium >150 mmol/L, seizure risk, severe symptoms. Patient must be observed. Severe dehydration can precipitate osmotic crisis, especially in AVP-R (kidneys cannot concentrate).

      💊Management & treatment▾
      
        #### AVP-D (Central DI) — Desmopressin replacement
        
          **First-line:** Desmopressin (synthetic ADH analogue)
          **Routes:** Intranasal (spray, metered spray, powder), oral tablet, subcutaneous/IM injection, sublingual melt
          **Intranasal dosing (most common):** 5–20 μg 1–3 times daily; titrate to individual response
          **Oral dosing:** 0.1–0.2 mg 3 times daily; less convenient, variable absorption
          **Onset & duration:** Intranasal 10–20 min onset, 8–12 h duration; plan doses around sleep & activities
          **Goal:** Adequate polyuria control without overtreatment (hyponatraemia risk)
        
        #### Monitoring desmopressin therapy
        
          **Baseline:** Plasma sodium, assess thirst, polyuria frequency, sleep quality
          **After starting/adjusting:** Monitor sodium at 1 week & 2–4 weeks; symptoms (thirst, nocturia, headache, lethargy)
          **Maintenance:** Periodic sodium checks (6–12 monthly or if symptoms change); adjust dose if patient gains/loses weight or develops medical changes
          **Warn patients:** Avoid excessive fluid intake (can cause hyponatraemia if dosed for thirst suppression). Free access to water is essential; never withhold water.
        
        #### AVP-R (Nephrogenic DI) — Symptomatic management
        **Desmopressin does not work in AVP-R**; focus is symptom control & preventing hypernatraemia.

          **First-line:** Adequate free water intake (ad libitum); patient education on maintaining hydration in hot weather
          **NSAIDs (e.g. indomethacin 50 mg TID):** Reduce prostaglandin-mediated urinary concentrating defect; 30–50% polyuria reduction; monitor renal function
          **Thiazide diuretics (e.g. hydrochlorothiazide 25 mg daily):** Paradoxical effect in NDI; mild volume depletion triggers proximal tubular reabsorption of sodium & water, reducing distal delivery; effective 20–30% polyuria reduction
          **Amiloride (potassium-sparing diuretic):** First-line for lithium-induced NDI; blocks lithium entry into collecting duct cells; 0.5–1 mg TID; monitor potassium & renal function
          **Combination:** Thiazide + amiloride often more effective than monotherapy
          **Stop offending medication:** If lithium-induced, gradual withdrawal (if clinically possible); partial recovery possible after stopping lithium (weeks to months)
        
        #### Adipsic AVP-D (rare special case)
        Damage to hypothalamic thirst centre; patient lacks thirst sensation. Extremely dangerous: dehydration & hypernatraemia can develop silently. Management: **mandatory scheduled fluid intake** (planned drinking 4–6× daily), desmopressin dosing, continuous education & monitoring, behavioural therapy to establish drinking habit.

      💡Clinical pearls & pitfalls▾
      
        #### Pearls
        
          ✦**Three syndromes, overlapping presentations.** AVP-D, AVP-R, and primary polydipsia can all present with polyuria + polydipsia. Water deprivation test is essential for differentiation.
          ✦**Desmopressin is diagnostic AND therapeutic for AVP-D.** Good response confirms AVP-D; no response suggests AVP-R or primary polydipsia. Use both the test and symptom response.
          ✦**Lithium-induced NDI is common.** 40–55% of lithium patients; may occur early or after years of therapy. Always ask about lithium; consider amiloride as protective agent in long-term lithium use.
          ✦**Hypernatraemia is the key danger.** If DI patient cannot access water (coma, confusion, post-op), rapid hypernatraemia develops. Seizure & death risk. Always ensure free water access with desmopressin therapy.
          ✦**Hyponatraemia from overdosed desmopressin is preventable.** Educate patient to avoid excessive fluid intake while on desmopressin. Warn of headache, lethargy, seizure (signs of hyponatraemia).
          ✦**Partial AVP-D & primary polydipsia overlap on WDT.** Urine osmolality 300–800 mOsm/kg is ambiguous. Measure basal AVP/copeptin, repeat testing, or therapeutic trial with desmopressin to clarify.
          ✦**Post-traumatic DI may be transient.** Head trauma frequently triggers AVP-D; some resolve within days–weeks. Monitor and reassess need for desmopressin periodically.
          ✦**Adipsic AVP-D is a medical emergency.** No thirst = silent dehydration & hypernatraemia. Needs mandatory timed fluid intake & close monitoring; high risk of complications.
        
        #### Pitfalls
        
          **Assuming all polyuria is DI.** Always differentiate: glucose, calcium, plasma osmolality, urine osmolality baseline before pursuing water deprivation test.
          **Performing water deprivation test without informed consent & safety precautions.** Test carries risk of severe hypernatraemia & osmotic crisis. Requires monitoring & ability to abort if safety thresholds breached.
          **Not identifying underlying AVP-D cause.** In newly diagnosed, obtain MRI of hypothalamic-pituitary region; assess for trauma, surgery, tumour, infection, granulomatous disease. Treatment may be directed at underlying cause.
          **Desmopressin overdosing without sodium monitoring.** SIADH-like hyponatraemia can develop if dosed too aggressively or if patient drinks excess water. Educate & monitor sodium.
          **Missing lithium-induced NDI during routine psychiatric follow-up.** Ask polyuria/nocturia systematically in lithium patients; consider prophylactic amiloride or switching mood stabilizer if NDI develops.
          **Giving desmopressin to AVP-R patient expecting efficacy.** Will not work; wastes time and risks hyponatraemia if fluid intake not controlled. Confirm AVP-D before starting desmopressin.
          **Overlooking primary polydipsia.** History of psychiatric illness, medication (antipsychotic, others), or compulsive drinking. Water deprivation test shows preserved concentrating ability. Desmopressin will worsen by allowing continued excessive intake with hyponatraemia risk.
