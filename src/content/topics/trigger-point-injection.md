#### What is a trigger point?
        A hyperirritable spot in skeletal muscle or fascia that, when palpated, produces local pain and characteristic referred pain patterns. Trigger points are distinct from tender points (diffuse tenderness without referral).

        #### Active vs latent trigger points
        
          **Active:** Causes spontaneous pain or pain on movement; patient seeks treatment
          **Latent:** Present but pain only on palpation; may become active after trauma or overuse

        #### Indications for trigger point injection
        
          - Chronic myofascial pain unresponsive to conservative measures (stretching, massage, physio)
          - Acute myofascial pain with functional impairment
          - Regional pain syndromes (neck, shoulder, lower back, limbs)
          - Tension-type headache with palpable trigger points in neck/trapezius
          - Fibromyalgia (localized trigger point treatment, not for diffuse symptoms)

        #### Contraindications
        
          - Infection over injection site or systemic infection
          Anticoagulation (relative; can inject if INR 
          - Allergy to local anaesthetic
          - Patient refusal
          - Inability to reliably identify trigger point (better with ultrasound guidance)

        #### Mechanism of action
        
          **Mechanical:** Needle insertion creates micro-trauma, disrupting muscle contracture and breaking pain cycle
          **Chemical:** Local anaesthetic blocks nociceptors; steroid reduces inflammation if used
          **Neurological:** Interrupts reflex arc; reduces substance P release

        #### Pre-injection assessment
        
          - Palpate muscle systematically to locate tender, taut bands
          - Identify trigger point (usually in the band); confirm referral pattern with patient
          - Assess muscle length and function; note ROM restrictions
          - Rule out other diagnoses (referred pain from joint, nerve root, viscera)

          Post-injection care
          Advise rest for 24-48 hours; ice or heat as tolerated. Gentle stretching and physio after 2-3 days accelerates recovery. Most patients benefit from 2-3 injections spaced 1-2 weeks apart, combined with home exercise programme.

      💪Upper trapezius and neck trigger points▾
      
        Most common trigger points in primary care; cause neck pain, headache and shoulder discomfort.

          Upper trapezius trigger points
          **Location:** Taut band in upper trapezius muscle, typically at superior angle of scapula or along upper trap fibres
          **Referred pain:** Ipsilateral neck, temporomandibular joint, temple, side of head
          **Associated conditions:** Tension-type headache, cervicogenic headache, neck stiffness
          
          **Palpation technique:**
          
            1
            Patient seated; examiner stands behind

            2
            Identify the taut band in upper trapezius by palpating from shoulder toward neck

            3
            Press firmly; trigger point elicits referred pain to head or jaw when palpated

          **Injection technique:**
          
            1
            Patient relaxed; neck slightly forward flexed

            2
            Clean skin with chlorhexidine

            3
            Palpate trigger point; mark with pen

            4
            Using 27G needle on 3 mL syringe, insert perpendicular to muscle fibres

            5
            Advance until muscle is felt (slight "pop" or resistance); needle should be in the taut band

            6
            Inject 0.5-1 mL of 1% lignocaine slowly; may elicit local twitch response (needle withdrawal reflex)

            7
            Withdraw; repeat 1-2 times at slightly different sites within same muscle band if multiple points

          **Total volume:** 1-3 mL per session across all injection sites
          **Expected response:** Immediate or within 1-2 minutes; local twitch response may indicate successful needle placement

          Levator scapulae trigger points
          **Location:** Superior medial border of scapula; deep to trapezius
          **Referred pain:** Angle of neck, shoulder joint, medial scapula
          **Injection:** Similar to upper trapezius but deeper penetration; aim for taut band at scapular angle

          Sternocleidomastoid (SCM) trigger points
          **Location:** Taut band within SCM muscle on anterior neck
          **Referred pain:** Face, jaw, temple, forehead (can mimic temporal headache)
          **Caution:** Vascular structures nearby (carotid artery); use careful palpation and small-gauge needle (27G)
          **Technique:** Patient supine; palpate SCM; inject perpendicular to muscle with 27G needle; 0.5 mL lignocaine

      💪Shoulder and arm trigger points▾

          Infraspinatus trigger points
          **Location:** Posterior shoulder, infraspinous fossa below spine of scapula
          **Referred pain:** Posterior shoulder, lateral arm, dorsal hand
          **Associated:** Rotator cuff pain, frozen shoulder, posterior shoulder impingement
          **Palpation:** Patient prone or side-lying; identify infraspinous fossa; palpate for taut bands
          **Injection:** 27G needle perpendicular; inject 0.5-1 mL into trigger point within muscle

          Supraspinatus trigger points
          **Location:** Supraspinous fossa (above spine of scapula)
          **Referred pain:** Lateral shoulder, lateral arm
          **Associated:** Subacromial impingement, rotator cuff tendinopathy
          **Technique:** Patient prone; identify supraspinous fossa; needle perpendicular to scapula; 0.5 mL per site

          Pectoralis major trigger points
          **Location:** Anterior chest, pectoralis major muscle
          **Referred pain:** Anterior chest, ipsilateral arm and hand (can mimic cardiac pain)
          **Caution:** Chest wall vascular structures; use 25-27G needle; careful depth to avoid pneumothorax
          **Technique:** Patient supine; palpate taut band in pectoral muscle; inject perpendicular; max 1 mL per site

          Biceps and forearm trigger points
          **Location:** Biceps brachii, brachioradialis, flexor carpi radialis
          **Referred pain:** Anterior arm, lateral forearm, thumb and index finger
          **Associated:** Tennis elbow (lateral epicondylitis), repetitive strain injury
          **Injection:** 27G needle; inject 0.5 mL per point; avoid radial nerve and artery (run medial to biceps)

      🦵Lower back and leg trigger points▾

          Quadratus lumborum (QL) trigger points
          **Location:** Deep in lower back, between pelvis and ribcage, lateral to spine
          **Referred pain:** Lower back, hip, lateral flank (often bilateral)
          **Associated:** Low back pain, pelvic tilt dysfunction, side-bending pain
          **Palpation:** Patient supine; palpate deep in flank below ribcage; trigger point tender to deep palpation
          **Injection:** 25G needle, 2-3 cm deep; inject 0.5-1 mL; may require ultrasound guidance for safety

          Erector spinae trigger points
          **Location:** Paraspinal muscles along lumbar spine
          **Referred pain:** Lower back, lateral hip, buttock
          **Associated:** Low back pain, lumbar strain, postural dysfunction
          **Injection:** 25G needle, perpendicular to spine; inject 0.5-1 mL into taut band; avoid deep penetration toward spinal canal

          Gluteus maximus trigger points
          **Location:** Buttock muscle; typically more superficial than other gluteal muscles
          **Referred pain:** Buttock, lower back, posterior thigh
          **Associated:** Buttock pain, prolonged sitting discomfort, hip dysfunction
          **Injection:** Patient prone; 25G needle, 1-2 cm deep; inject 0.5-1 mL per point

          Gluteus medius trigger points
          **Location:** Hip, lateral to gluteus maximus
          **Referred pain:** Lateral hip, lower back, lateral thigh
          **Associated:** Hip pain, Trendelenburg gait, greater trochanteric pain syndrome
          **Injection:** Patient side-lying; inject into taut band in gluteus medius; 0.5-1 mL

          Quadriceps trigger points
          **Location:** Vastus lateralis, vastus medialis, rectus femoris
          **Referred pain:** Anterior knee, lower leg, sometimes foot
          **Associated:** Patellofemoral pain, anterior knee pain, quadriceps strain
          **Injection:** Patient supine; 25G needle; inject 0.5-1 mL into visible or palpable taut band; avoid neurovascular structures

          Calf (gastrocnemius/soleus) trigger points
          **Location:** Superficial calf muscle, typically medial head of gastrocnemius
          **Referred pain:** Calf, heel, sole of foot (can mimic plantar fasciitis)
          **Associated:** Calf strain, achilles pain, heel pain
          **Injection:** Patient prone or seated; 27G needle; inject 0.5 mL into taut band; superficial injection, low risk

      ⚠️Complications and safety▾
      
        #### Common side effects (expected and temporary)
        
          **Local soreness:** 24-48 hours post-injection; manage with ice and NSAIDs
          **Local muscle stiffness:** Resolves within 48 hours
          **Transient increase in pain:** Some patients report 24-hour pain increase before improvement; reassure and manage symptomatically
          **Mild bruising or swelling:** Usually minor; apply ice

        #### Rare but serious complications
        
          **Infection:** Local cellulitis or abscess; use aseptic technique, counsel on red flags (fever, increasing pain/swelling)
          **Nerve injury:** Transient or persistent paresthesia; avoid repeated passes; consider ultrasound for deep muscles
          **Vascular injury:** Bleeding or hematoma; more common in anticoagulated patients or with large vessels nearby (neck, chest)
          **Pneumothorax:** Rare; from deep chest wall injections; counsel patient on chest pain, dyspnea warning signs
          **Allergic reaction:** To local anaesthetic; rare; have adrenaline available

        #### Sites requiring caution and ultrasound guidance
        
          **Neck (SCM, anterior neck):** Carotid artery, jugular vein, vagus nerve nearby; use 27G, shallow injection
          **Chest (pectoralis):** Pneumothorax risk; limit depth; use small volumes
          **Flank (QL, deep lateral muscles):** Kidney and other viscera; ultrasound recommended
          **Deep back muscles:** Spinal cord proximity; avoid midline; use ultrasound for deep injections

          When to use ultrasound
          Ultrasound guidance is strongly recommended for deep muscles (QL, deep back) and structures near vital organs (chest, neck). It increases accuracy, reduces complications, and improves patient confidence.

      📚Evidence and references▾
      
        #### Evidence for trigger point injection
        
          - Systematic reviews show benefit for chronic myofascial pain, particularly when combined with physical therapy
          - Effect size moderate to large; comparable to other interventions for regional pain
          - Dry needling (saline only) and local anaesthetic injection show similar efficacy in RCTs; steroid benefit unproven but commonly used

        #### Equipment needed
        
          - Sterile gloves, hand hygiene, skin disinfectant (chlorhexidine or iodine)
          - 25-27G needles (length 0.5-2 inches depending on depth)
          - 3-10 mL syringe
          - Local anaesthetic solution (1% lignocaine or 0.5% bupivacaine)
          - Optional: steroid (triamcinolone 10 mg) if planning combination injection
          - Ultrasound machine and probe (if available, especially for deep muscles)
