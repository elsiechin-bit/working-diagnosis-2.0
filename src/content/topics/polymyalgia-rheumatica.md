#### The classic picture
        
          **Age over 50** (usually well over) - PMR under 50 should make you doubt the diagnosis
          **Bilateral shoulder and/or pelvic-girdle pain** and aching, often with neck involvement
          **Morning stiffness lasting 45 minutes or more** - difficulty rolling over in bed, rising from a chair, raising arms to dress or wash hair
          **Subacute onset** over days to a few weeks; symptoms present at least 2 weeks
          **Constitutional symptoms** are common: fatigue, malaise, low-grade fever, weight loss, low mood
        
        #### Examination
        Painful, restricted active shoulder and hip movement, but **power is genuinely normal** - any apparent weakness is pain-limited, not true weakness. Peripheral features can occur: carpal tunnel syndrome, distal arthralgia, and occasionally pitting oedema of the hands (the RS3PE picture).

          True weakness is not PMR
          If there is genuine proximal weakness (not just pain) with a raised creatine kinase, think inflammatory myopathy, not PMR. PMR does not weaken muscle and the CK is normal.

      🩸Investigations & differential▾
      
        #### Investigations
        
          TestExpected / why
          
            ESR and CRPUsually raised (ESR often >40). CRP is more sensitive - a normal ESR does **not** exclude PMR. Take both before starting steroids.
            FBCNormochromic anaemia and thrombocytosis support an inflammatory process
            Creatine kinaseNormal in PMR - a raised CK points to myopathy or statin-related myalgia
            TSHHypothyroidism mimics the aches and stiffness
            RF / anti-CCPTo help separate from elderly-onset rheumatoid arthritis
            U&E, glucose/HbA1c, calcium, LFTsBaseline before steroids; screen for mimics and steroid risks
            Consider: protein electrophoresis, urinalysis, imagingWhere malignancy, myeloma or infection are plausible alternatives

        #### Differential diagnoses to exclude
        
          **Elderly-onset rheumatoid arthritis** (small-joint synovitis, positive serology)
          **Inflammatory myopathy** (true weakness, raised CK)
          **Hypothyroidism**; **statin myopathy**
          **Malignancy** (including myeloma) and **occult infection / endocarditis** - particularly if the response to steroids is poor
          **Osteoarthritis of shoulders/neck**, rotator cuff disease, fibromyalgia
        
        Classification criteria (2012 EULAR/ACR) combine age, bilateral shoulder involvement, morning stiffness, raised inflammatory markers, absence of RF/anti-CCP, and (optionally) ultrasound findings - but PMR remains a **clinical diagnosis** built on the picture, the markers, the exclusion of mimics, and the steroid response.

      ⚠️The giant cell arteritis overlap - do not miss it▾
      
        PMR and giant cell arteritis (GCA) are part of the same disease spectrum. Around 15-20% of people with PMR develop GCA, and roughly 40-50% of those with GCA have PMR features. GCA can threaten sight and must be treated as an emergency.

          Red-flag GCA symptoms - ask at every visit
          New temporal or unaccustomed headache, scalp tenderness (combing hair), **jaw claudication** (aching on chewing), and any **visual symptoms** - transient loss, blurring, double vision. Visual loss in GCA is often sudden, painless and irreversible.

        #### If GCA is suspected
        
          **Start high-dose glucocorticoid immediately** - do not wait for biopsy or specialist review. Typically prednisone 40-60 mg/day (higher, often IV methylprednisolone, if there are visual symptoms).
          Arrange **urgent same-day specialist referral** (rheumatology/ophthalmology) and confirmatory testing - temporal artery ultrasound (halo sign) or biopsy. Steroids do not erase biopsy findings for a week or more, so treatment must not be delayed.
          - The dose that controls pure PMR (around 15 mg) is far too low for GCA.

      💊Treatment & tapering▾
      
        #### Starting dose
        Prednisone **12.5-25 mg daily** (commonly 15 mg) for uncomplicated PMR. The response is typically **dramatic and rapid** - substantial relief within a few days. A clear response supports the diagnosis; failure to respond well within about a week should prompt review of the diagnosis rather than dose escalation.

        #### Tapering - slow and relapse-aware
        
          PhaseTypical approach
          
            Initial control15 mg daily until symptoms and markers settle (about 3-4 weeks)
            First reductionDown to 12.5 mg, then 10 mg over the following weeks
            Slow taperFrom 10 mg, reduce by about 1 mg every 4-8 weeks as tolerated
            DurationMost people need treatment for 1-2 years; some longer. Do not rush.

          Relapse is common
          Around half relapse during tapering. Treat a relapse by returning to the last effective dose, then taper more slowly - not by jumping straight to high doses. Warn patients never to stop steroids abruptly (risk of adrenal insufficiency and flare).

      🦴Steroid safety & monitoring▾
      
        Months of glucocorticoid carry predictable harms. Plan for them from day one.

            #### Bone protection
            Assess fracture risk; most older patients on prolonged steroids warrant a **bisphosphonate**
            Ensure adequate **calcium and vitamin D**
            Baseline and follow-up bone density where indicated

            #### Metabolic & vascular
            Monitor **blood pressure, glucose/HbA1c, weight**
            Watch for steroid-induced diabetes and hyperlipidaemia

            #### Other surveillance
            Mood and sleep, cataracts/glaucoma, skin and infection risk
            Consider gastroprotection if other GI risk factors

            #### Safety-netting
            Issue a **steroid card**; counsel on sick-day rules and not stopping abruptly
            Re-educate on GCA red-flag symptoms at each review

      🔄Relapsing or steroid-dependent disease▾
      
        #### Steroid-sparing options
        
          **Methotrexate** - the usual first steroid-sparing agent for frequent relapses, an inability to taper, or where steroid toxicity is a major concern.
          **IL-6 inhibition (tocilizumab; sarilumab):** established in GCA and supported by trial evidence in relapsing PMR; consider with specialist input where steroids cannot be reduced.
          **Refer to rheumatology** for atypical features, diagnostic doubt, frequent relapse, or difficulty tapering.

      💡Clinical pearls & pitfalls▾
      
        #### Pearls
        
          ✦**The steroid response is diagnostic.** A near-complete response to ~15 mg prednisone within days is expected. If it isn't dramatic, question the diagnosis.
          ✦**A normal ESR doesn't exclude PMR.** Always check CRP too - it is more sensitive.
          ✦**Ask about GCA at every visit.** It can emerge during PMR treatment - jaw claudication and visual symptoms are the ones that threaten sight.
          ✦**Check markers before the first dose.** Steroids normalise ESR/CRP within days and obscure the baseline.
          ✦**Bone-protect from the start.** Don't wait for the second year of steroids to think about fractures.
          ✦**Taper slowly.** Most relapses are driven by reducing too fast. Slow beats restarting high.
        
        #### Pitfalls
        
          **Treating "PMR" that needed 40-60 mg to control it.** If a low dose doesn't work, it is probably not PMR (or it is GCA) - escalating blindly masks the real diagnosis.
          **Missing malignancy, myeloma or infection** behind a steroid-poor "PMR" - the incomplete responder deserves a wider work-up.
          **Calling it PMR under 50** - rare enough that the label should be challenged.
          **Mistaking pain-limited movement for weakness** - true weakness plus a raised CK is myositis.
          **Stopping steroids abruptly** - risks adrenal insufficiency and flare.
          **Forgetting the patient owns the GCA safety-net** - they need to know which symptoms mean "seek care today".
