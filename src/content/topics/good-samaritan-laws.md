## Question 1: Do you have to stop?

          ### The two legal models
          "Good Samaritan law" describes two fundamentally different legal structures. The first - dominant in common law countries - is **liability protection**: you have no duty to help a stranger, but if you choose to, the law protects you from being sued. The second - dominant in civil law countries - is **duty to rescue**: you have a positive legal obligation to assist, and failing to do so is a criminal offence.

          These reflect different philosophies about citizenship, individual obligation, and the state's role. Neither is obviously superior. They produce different practical outcomes.

          ### New Zealand's position: no duty
          New Zealand follows the common law model. **You have no legal duty to rescue a stranger.** You may walk past a person in danger without legal consequence.

          The common law position, derived from English authority, is clear: there is no general duty to rescue. This is sometimes called "no duty to be a Good Samaritan" - which is an accurate but confusing name for a rule that actually means *you are not required to be one*.

          That said, the Medical Council of New Zealand's guidance notes that doctors have an **ethical obligation** to offer assistance in medical emergencies where reasonably practicable. [3] This creates an important tension: you have no legal duty but you have a professional ethical duty. They pull in different directions.

          ### The duty-to-rescue countries
          **France:** Article 223-6 of the Penal Code makes it a criminal offence to fail to assist a person in danger when you could do so. The offence carries up to five years' imprisonment and a €75,000 fine. [11]

          **Germany:** Paragraph 323c of the Criminal Code requires any person to render first aid at the scene of an accident or emergency to the extent that assistance is required and can be expected of them. Failure is a criminal offence. [12] The standard is calibrated to what is reasonable - a cardiologist is held to a different standard than a passer-by - but the obligation applies to all.

          **Other countries:** Spain, Italy, the Netherlands, and most of Scandinavia have analogous duty-to-rescue provisions.

          The philosophical difference is significant: in the duty-to-rescue world, community membership entails positive obligations to each other's welfare. In the common law world, the decision to help is treated as a matter of personal conscience - and then the law removes the liability risk to encourage what it does not require.

          ### The free-rider problem
          The duty-to-rescue model has one significant advantage: it removes the free-rider problem. In the common law world, you can rationally decline to help and suffer no legal consequence, while the person who does help assumes liability risk. In the duty-to-rescue world, you cannot.

          ## Question 2: Are you protected if you do?

          ### New Zealand: the gap in the floor
          New Zealand does not have a comprehensive Good Samaritan Act. Most clinicians assume such protection exists. It largely does not.

          The New Zealand Law Commission examined this question and has historically recommended legislative reform. [1] The recommendation has not been enacted. What exists instead is a patchwork:

            **Civil Defence and Emergency Management Act 2002:** Provides liability protection for people carrying out duties during declared emergencies. It does not cover a doctor who stops at a road accident on an ordinary Tuesday. [2]
            **Volunteers Employment Protection Act 1973:** Protects volunteer employment rights, not liability for negligent assistance.
            **Common law:** If you intervene, you assume a duty of care assessed against what is reasonable in the circumstances. Courts apply a more forgiving standard to emergency conditions than to planned clinical care. This is not statutory protection; it is a judicial approach that provides some comfort without certainty.

          The practical reality: negligence claims against Good Samaritans are rare in New Zealand, and courts have generally been sympathetic. But "rare" and "legally protected" are not the same thing. The absence of statutory clarity creates genuine uncertainty that likely deters some clinicians from intervening.

          ### Australia: the statutory model
          Australia has addressed this more directly, though as a federation the relevant laws are state and territory legislation.

          **New South Wales** (Civil Liability Act 2002, ss 56-58): A person is not liable in negligence for an act done in good faith when assisting someone apparently injured. [4] The protection is lost if the person was under the influence, committed gross negligence, or was acting in the course of their profession for reward.

          This last carve-out is notable: Good Samaritan provisions in several Australian states apply more clearly to unqualified bystanders than to healthcare professionals. For healthcare professionals, the position depends on whether they can be said to be acting outside any professional context - which is often unclear.

          **Queensland** (Civil Liability Act 2003, ss 26-27) and **Victoria** (Wrongs Act 1958, s 31B) are more straightforward for healthcare professionals, specifically including persons who provide assistance consistent with their professional training. Western Australia, South Australia, Tasmania, the ACT, and the Northern Territory all have variants. [5]

          Australia has moved further than New Zealand toward clear statutory protection.

          ### United Kingdom: indirect protection
          The UK lacks a comprehensive Good Samaritan statute, relying on two indirect mechanisms:

            **Compensation Act 2006, s 1:** Requires courts to consider whether requiring a particular standard of care would deter desirable activity for the benefit of society. [6]
            **Social Action, Responsibility and Heroism Act 2015 (SARAH Act):** Requires courts to have regard to whether the defendant was acting heroically, in an emergency, for the benefit of others. [7] Academic assessment of its practical effect has been underwhelming.

          The **General Medical Council** requires UK doctors to offer help in emergencies where reasonably expected. [8] This is a professional obligation backed by regulatory consequences, with minimal statutory liability protection attached - the same tension as New Zealand.

          For UK doctors, the practical answer is that professional indemnity organisations (MDU, MPS) generally provide coverage for Good Samaritan acts.

          ### United States: fifty different answers
          All fifty US states have Good Samaritan legislation, and no two are identical. At the federal level, the **Aviation Medical Assistance Act 1998** specifically protects medical professionals assisting during in-flight emergencies. [9]

          State laws vary on: whether they cover all bystanders or only healthcare professionals; whether the person must be present by chance (not on duty); whether they extend to AED use; and what standard they protect against (ordinary negligence vs gross negligence vs wilful misconduct).

          The most significant recent development is **drug overdose Good Samaritan laws**, now enacted in most states. [10] These laws provide immunity from prosecution for drug-related offences for people who call for help when someone is overdosing. Several states extended this to the overdosing person themselves. Studies found that enactment was associated with increases in emergency calls and naloxone deployment at overdose scenes - the laws appear to have directly saved lives by removing the disincentive to call.

          New Zealand does not yet have equivalent overdose legislation.

        ## Question 3: What should you actually do?

        ### Specific situations for New Zealand clinicians

        **Road accidents and public emergencies.** You have no statutory Good Samaritan protection, but courts assess your conduct against a reasonable emergency standard, not a planned clinical standard. If you choose to help: (1) Document what you did and why if possible. (2) Notify your professional indemnity insurer (MIPS or equivalent) of any incident. (3) Check your policy wording - most New Zealand medical indemnity policies do extend to Good Samaritan acts, but confirm this explicitly.

        **In-flight medical emergencies.** Airlines typically have policies requesting assistance from medical professionals on board. The legal position on liability varies by jurisdiction depending on aircraft registration and incident location. The practical position: you are expected to help, courts will assess you generously, and the airline's indemnity usually provides additional coverage. Help.

        **AED use.** Automated external defibrillators are designed for use by untrained bystanders. Several New Zealand districts have registered AED networks. There is no specific statute protecting AED users from liability, but the common law reasonable emergency standard applies, and the bar for negligent AED use in an out-of-hospital cardiac arrest is extremely high.

        **Opioid overdose.** New Zealand has expanded community naloxone access, and naloxone is available without prescription in some settings. There is no specific Good Samaritan overdose law equivalent to those in many US states. Whether this matters in practice is uncertain - NZ drug possession laws are enforced with more discretion than their American equivalents - but the absence of formal protection is a known gap.

        **The professional obligation dimension.** MCNZ guidance creates an ethical obligation to help in emergencies. This means that unlike a random bystander, a NZ doctor faces both an ethical duty to help and no clear statutory protection for doing so. The professional ethics and the legal framework pull in different directions. The practical answer - help, document, notify your indemnifier - is the same in any individual case, but the structural gap is worth naming.

        ### The case for reform

        Australia's state-level legislative approach provides a model. A straightforward New Zealand Good Samaritan Act along the lines of the NSW Civil Liability Act would:

          - Provide clear protection from civil liability for good-faith emergency assistance
          - Apply specifically to healthcare professionals assisting outside their employment context
          - Include an overdose Good Samaritan provision removing the prosecution disincentive at opioid overdose scenes
          - Align New Zealand with the standard of peer jurisdictions

        The Law Commission has examined this territory. The recommendation for reform has been noted and not acted on for several decades. [1] Meanwhile, New Zealand clinicians who stop at accidents, respond to in-flight emergencies, or assist at public events operate in a legal grey zone that their Australian colleagues do not.

        The argument for legislation is not primarily that clinicians are being sued - they largely are not. It is that uncertainty has a chilling effect on assistance that clear protection would remove. The person who drives past the accident because they are not sure they are covered is the policy failure the law has not yet fixed.

        **Until then: help, be reasonable, document, and know your indemnity policy.**
