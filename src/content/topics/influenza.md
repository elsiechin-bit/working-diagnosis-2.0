## Overview


**Influenza viruses are orthomyxoviruses with segmented negative-sense RNA genomes.** Three genera (A, B, C) cause human disease; Influenza A is most common & severe; B causes milder epidemics; C is rare & usually asymptomatic. The viral surface is covered with haemagglutinin (HA) and neuraminidase (NA) spike proteins—targets of immunity and vaccine.

        #### Genetic variation & evolution
        
          **Antigenic drift:** Point mutations in HA & NA genes accumulate gradually year-to-year; minor amino acid changes allow virus to evade prior immunity; requires new vaccine annually
          **Antigenic shift:** Reassortment of gene segments (usually HA or NA) between different influenza subtypes; produces novel pandemic strains with **pandemic potential** (e.g., 2009 H1N1 pandemic)
          **Nomenclature:** Type (A/B/C), subtype (HA-NA; e.g., H1N1, H3N2 for type A), strain origin, year; e.g., "A/Sydney/5/2024 (H3N2)"
        
        #### Influenza A subtypes circulating 2025–2026
        
          **H1N1 (pdm09 lineage):** From 2009 pandemic; still circulating; generally milder than H3N2
          **H3N2:** Dominant in recent seasons; more severe, higher hospitalisation rate; evolves rapidly (vaccine mismatch possible)
          **Influenza B:** Two lineages (Victoria, Yamagata); generally milder; lower mortality; vaccine includes both lineages
        
        #### NZ 2026 vaccine formulation
        **4-valent seasonal vaccine (expected March 2026):** H1N1 + H3N2 + Influenza B Victoria + Influenza B Yamagata. Specific strains determined by WHO recommendations February 2026 (WHO Global Influenza Surveillance & Response System—GISRS).

      🦠Epidemiology & transmission▾
      
        #### Seasonal pattern (Southern Hemisphere)
        
          **Winter peak:** June–August (May–September extended season)
          **Epidemic onset:** Declared when surveillance thresholds exceeded (typically late May in NZ)
          **Duration:** 8–12 weeks; attack rate 5–15% population per season
          **Pandemic potential:** Every 10–40 years novel subtypes emerge; 1918 (H1N1), 1957 (H2N2), 1968 (H3N2), 2009 (H1N1pdm09)
        
        #### Transmission & contagiousness
        
          **Route:** Respiratory droplets; airborne in enclosed spaces; fomite contact (hands, surfaces)
          **Incubation:** 1–4 days (median 2 days)
          **Shedding:** Most contagious 24 hours before symptom onset through 5–7 days post-onset (longer in children, immunocompromised)
          **R value:** Approximately 1.3–1.5 per infected person; pandemic strains higher (~2 for 1918 pandemic)
        
        #### Risk factors for severe disease
        
          High-risk groups for influenza complications
          **Increased hospitalisation & mortality:** Age >65 years, chronic respiratory disease (asthma, COPD), chronic heart disease, diabetes, chronic kidney disease, chronic liver disease, neurological/neurodevelopmental conditions, immunocompromised (HIV, on immunosuppressants), obesity (BMI >30), **Māori & Pasifika ancestry** (independent risk factor), pregnancy, recent childbirth, long-term care facility resident.

      💉Immunity & vaccination▾
      
        #### Natural immunity from infection
        
          **Duration:** 1–2 years (wanes gradually); subtype-specific (prior H1N1 infection does not protect against H3N2)
          **Mechanism:** Neutralising antibodies to HA & NA; T-cell memory; heterologous immunity (partial cross-protection to drifted variants)
          **Reinfection risk:** Possible every season due to drift; prior infection reduces severity but not always transmission
        
        #### Vaccination strategies
        
          **Inactivated intramuscular vaccine (IIV):** Standard; split virion or whole virion; 60–90% effective against exact circulating strain, 40–60% against drifted variant
          **Live attenuated nasal vaccine:** Not available NZ currently; limited use due to poor efficacy in some populations
          **Recombinant HA vaccine (RIV):** Emerging; higher potency; useful in egg allergy
          **High-dose vaccine (age >65):** Double-dose IIV or quadrivalent recombinant; improves immunogenicity in older adults
        
        #### NZ vaccination programme 2026
        
          **Funded vaccines (free at DHBs/GPs):**
            
              - Age ≥65 years
              - Chronic disease: asthma, COPD, diabetes, CVD, chronic kidney disease, chronic liver disease, neurological/neurodevelopmental conditions
              - Immunocompromised (HIV, on immunosuppressants, recent transplant)
              - Pregnant women (any trimester)
              - Postpartum women (within 3 months of delivery)
              - Long-term care facility residents
              - Māori & Pasifika (all ages, equity basis)
              - Healthcare workers (occupational risk)

          **Timing:** Administered April–June; uptake target >70% in high-risk groups
          **Efficacy expectation:** Reduces hospitalisation ~70%; reduces mortality ~40–50%
        
        #### Vaccine efficacy variation
        
          **Strain match:** If vaccine strain matches circulating strain, efficacy 80–90%; if drifted, 40–60%
          **Age:** Declining efficacy >65 years (40–50%); improved with high-dose vaccine
          **Prior immunity:** Individuals with history of prior influenza or vaccination may have lower vaccine response (original antigenic sin)
          **2025 NZ prediction:** H3N2 dominance expected; vaccine efficacy likely 50–60% depending on drift match

      🔍Clinical presentation & diagnosis▾
      
        #### Uncomplicated influenza ("flu")
        
          **Onset:** Abrupt (hours, not days); systemic symptoms often precede respiratory
          **Cardinal symptoms:** Fever >38.5°C, myalgia (legs, back), malaise, headache, sore throat, cough (often dry initially)
          **Duration:** Fever 3–5 days; myalgia/malaise 1–2 weeks; cough 2–3 weeks
          **Exam findings:** Fever, pharyngeal erythema (usually no exudate; distinguishes from strep), crackles/wheezes if lower respiratory involved
          **Laboratory:** Leukopenia typical (WBC 
        
        #### Complicated influenza
        
          **Viral pneumonia:** Progressive dyspnoea, hypoxia, bilateral infiltrates; highest mortality; may need ICU support
          **Secondary bacterial pneumonia:** Fever returns after 5–7 day improvement; productive cough (purulent sputum); pathogens: Streptococcus pneumoniae, Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA risk if recent antiviral exposure)
          **Myocarditis/pericarditis:** Chest pain, dyspnoea, arrhythmia, signs of heart failure; troponin elevated, ECG changes; can be fulminant
          **CNS involvement:** Encephalitis, Guillain-Barré syndrome (rare; ~1 per million infected)
          **Exacerbation of chronic disease:** Asthma, COPD, diabetes control, heart failure
        
        #### Diagnostic testing
        
          **Clinical diagnosis:** During season, fever + myalgia + cough + acute onset = presumed influenza; >80% PPV during epidemics
          **PCR (nasopharyngeal swab):** Gold standard; rapid (2–4 hours) or standard (24 hours); highest sensitivity first 3–5 days
          **Rapid antigen test:** Lateral flow; 
          **When to test:**
            
              - Suspected influenza in high-risk patients (to guide antivirals within 48-hour window)
              - Hospitalised patients
              - Pregnant women
              - Immunocompromised
              - Outbreaks, surveillance, epidemiological investigations
              - NOT routine in low-risk outpatients with mild illness (clinical diagnosis sufficient)

      💊Antiviral therapy & clinical management▾
      
        #### Neuraminidase inhibitors (antivirals)
        **Class:** NA inhibitors prevent viral release from infected cells, reducing viral load & symptom duration.

          **Oseltamivir (Tamiflu):**
            
              - Oral 75 mg BD × 5 days (age-adjusted dosing for children)
              - Most commonly used NZ
              - Absorption not affected by food; take with food if GI upset

          **Zanamivir (Relenza):**
            
              - Inhaled 5 mg (2 inhalations) BD × 5 days
              - Avoids drug interactions; caution in asthma/COPD (bronchospasm risk)

          **Peramivir:** IV (single dose); reserved for hospitalised patients unable to take oral/inhaled
        
        #### Efficacy of antivirals
        
          **Symptom reduction:** ~24 hours shorter fever duration; 1–2 day reduction in overall illness; most benefit in first 48 hours
          **Complication reduction:** ~30% reduction in lower respiratory complications (pneumonia) in high-risk groups when given early
          **Hospitalisation reduction:** ~50% in high-risk patients if started within 48 hours
          **Window of opportunity:** **MUST be given within 48 hours of symptom onset for benefit**; minimal to no effect if started >72 hours
        
        #### When to prescribe antivirals
        
          Indications for oseltamivir/zanamivir
          **Prescribe if:** Suspected/confirmed influenza in high-risk group **within 48 hours of symptom onset**. High-risk = age >65, chronic disease, Māori/Pasifika, pregnant, obesity, immunocompromised. Also prescribe hospitalised patients, secondary bacterial pneumonia, myocarditis/severe complications.

          **Do NOT prescribe:** Low-risk patient >72 hours from symptom onset; uncomplicated mild illness in healthy person (minimal benefit)
          **Side effects:** Oseltamivir: nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea (take with food); rarely neuropsychiatric symptoms (alert patient to report). Zanamivir: bronchospasm (use cautiously in asthma/COPD)
        
        #### Supportive management
        
          **Paracetamol/ibuprofen:** Treat fever for comfort; does not prevent complications
          **Hydration:** Oral fluids; IV if unable to maintain intake or severe illness
          **Rest & isolation:** Return to work/school when afebrile without antipyretics (typically 5–7 days); immunocompromised longer (up to 10–14 days shedding)
          **Antibiotics:** NO routine use; reserve for confirmed secondary bacterial pneumonia (fever recurrence after 5–7 days improvement, productive sputum, infiltrates on CXR)

      🚨Complications & severe disease▾
      
        #### Primary viral pneumonia
        
          **Presentation:** Progressive dyspnoea, hypoxia, rapid clinical deterioration (often within 24–48 hours)
          **Findings:** Bilateral infiltrates on CXR (ground-glass opacities, consolidation); SpO₂ 
          **Pathophysiology:** Viral direct lung damage, inflammatory cytokine storm; high-risk high-dose antiviral (oseltamivir) & supportive care (oxygen, mechanical ventilation, ECMO if severe)
          **Mortality:** 5–50% depending on age & underlying disease; pregnant women & immunocompromised highest risk
        
        #### Secondary bacterial pneumonia
        
          **Timing:** Fever returns or persists after initial improvement (day 5–10)
          **Pathogens:** S. pneumoniae, S. aureus (MRSA if recent antivirals), H. influenzae
          **Management:** Antibiotics (amoxicillin + clavulanate or cefotaxime if severe/MRSA risk); respiratory support; repeat CXR to confirm pneumonia
        
        #### Myocarditis & heart failure
        
          **Presentation:** Chest pain, dyspnoea, palpitations, syncope during acute illness; troponin elevated, ECG abnormalities (ST changes, arrhythmia)
          **Fulminant course possible:** Cardiogenic shock, arrhythmias, sudden death; ICU management, inotropes, mechanical support if severe
        
        #### Neurological complications
        
          **Encephalitis:** Altered consciousness, seizures; CSF pleocytosis (viral pattern); high mortality
          **Guillain-Barré syndrome:** Post-influenza (1–3 weeks after symptom onset); ascending paralysis, respiratory failure; requires ICU monitoring & plasmapheresis/IVIG

      🌍NZ influenza landscape & surveillance▾
      
        #### Epidemiological surveillance (ESR, NZPHL)
        
          **Weekly surveillance:** ESR publishes influenza surveillance reports (laboratory-confirmed cases, strain identification, hospitalisation rates)
          **Epidemic declaration:** Typically late May when case numbers exceed threshold (varies by DHB)
          **Sentinel surveillance:** GPs, EDs report suspected influenza; subset tested for strain characterisation (WHO GISRS feedback)
          **Hospital surveillance:** Tracked hospitalisations, ICU admissions, deaths; enables rapid response identification of severe strains
        
        #### 2025 NZ influenza season (expected winter June–August)
        
          **Predicted strains:** H3N2 + H1N1 + Influenza B (both lineages)
          **Vaccine roll-out:** April–May 2026 (prior to season); funded groups via DHBs/GP clinics
          **Antiviral supply:** Oseltamivir (Tamiflu) stockpile anticipated; ensure availability in primary care & ED by May
          **Māori & Pasifika focus:** Enhanced outreach, culturally appropriate messaging; vaccination & early antiviral access prioritised (higher complication risk)
        
        #### Primary care pandemic preparedness
        
          **Stockpile:** Oseltamivir, zanamivir, paracetamol, oxygen, personal protective equipment (masks, N95s for high-risk contacts)
          **Triage protocols:** Respiratory isolation; drive-through clinics if surge; telephone triage for low-risk
          **Staffing:** Plan for staff illness (up to 30% absence in severe season)
          **Communication:** Clear messaging to public on symptoms, testing, isolation, high-risk groups; avoid misinformation

      💡Clinical pearls & pitfalls▾
      
        #### Pearls
        
          ✦**Influenza antivirals work only in the first 48 hours.** If >48 hours from symptom onset, no benefit from oseltamivir. Do not prescribe late.
          ✦**High-risk groups include Māori & Pasifika.** Independent risk factor; equity-based access to vaccine & antivirals critical.
          ✦**Vaccine efficacy varies 40–90% by strain match.** Never tell patients "the flu shot doesn't work." It reduces hospitalisation & death by 40–50% in high-risk.
          ✦**Myocarditis is uncommon but serious.** Chest pain + troponin elevation in acute influenza = ICU admission, not outpatient management.
          ✦**Secondary bacterial pneumonia emerges after initial improvement.** Returning fever on day 5–7 = consider antibiotics; repeat CXR to confirm.
          ✦**Antigenic drift means vaccine changes every year.** No lifelong immunity; annual vaccination critical in high-risk groups.
          ✦**Influenza is NOT "just a cold."** Abrupt systemic illness, myalgia, severe malaise distinguish it from rhinovirus/other mild URIs.
          ✦**Isolation matters for transmission.** High-risk contacts (elderly, pregnant, immunocompromised) warrant close monitoring & consideration of prophylactic antivirals if exposed.
        
        #### Pitfalls
        
          **Prescribing antivirals >48 hours from symptom onset.** No evidence of benefit; contributes to resistance without improving outcomes. Do not do this.
          **Testing only for reassurance (low-risk patients).** Clinical diagnosis during season is sufficient; multiplex results misleading if prolonged shedding. Save testing for high-risk considering antivirals.
          **Over-relying on vaccine efficacy.** Explain to patients that 50–60% efficacy means significant protection but not guaranteed; still reduces hospitalisation/death substantially.
          **Missing secondary bacterial pneumonia.** Do not assume clinical improvement on antiviral means no complication. Fever recurrence = reevaluate, CXR, consider antibiotics.
          **Routine antibiotics for uncomplicated influenza.** No benefit; increases resistance. Reserve for confirmed secondary bacterial pneumonia.
          **Not considering high-risk when assessing severity.** Older patient or Māori/Pasifika with flu symptoms = aggressive antiviral + close monitoring, not reassurance.
          **Delaying vacc campaign messaging.** Winter vaccination uptake depends on early promotion (March–April). Late campaign = poor uptake.
