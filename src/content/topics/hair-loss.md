#### Hair growth cycle
        
          **Anagen (growth):** 2-7 years; 85-90% of scalp hairs in this phase
          **Catagen (transition):** 2-3 weeks; hair follicle shrinks
          **Telogen (resting):** 2-4 months; 10-15% of scalp hairs; hair can shed during this phase

        #### Hair loss classification
        
          **Scarring alopecia:** Follicle destroyed; permanent; requires dermatology
          **Non-scarring alopecia:** Follicle preserved; potentially reversible if cause addressed

        #### Normal hair shedding
        
          - 50-100 hairs per day normal; varies by hair density and length
          - Seasonal variation (more shedding autumn/winter)
          - Hair on pillow, in shower, in brush normal; concerns when visible thinning occurs

      👨‍🦲Androgenetic alopecia (male and female pattern hair loss)▾
      
        #### Androgenetic alopecia in men
        
          **Prevalence:** 50% by age 50; 80% by age 80
          **Genetics:** Polygenic; family history strong predictor
          **Pathophysiology:** Androgens (DHT) cause miniaturisation of follicles in genetically predisposed individuals
          **Presentation:** Receding hairline, crown thinning, or diffuse thinning; gradual onset
          **Progression:** Variable; some arrest early, others progress rapidly

        #### Male pattern baldness classification (Norwood scale)
        
          - Type I: Minimal recession
          - Type II: Temporal recession
          - Type III: Vertex recession; earliest stage for intervention to be effective
          - Types IV-VII: Progressive thinning and baldness

        #### Androgenetic alopecia in women
        
          **Prevalence:** 20-30% by age 50; increases with age
          **Presentation:** Diffuse thinning at crown and parting; widened part line; maintained frontal hairline (unlike men)
          **Triggers:** Menopause common; also stress, iron deficiency, thyroid disease
          **Ludwig scale:** Grades I (mild), II (moderate), III (severe) based on density loss

        #### Red flags and when to investigate further
        
          - Sudden onset or rapid progression (suggests telogen effluvium or alopecia areata, not androgenetic)
          - Patchy hair loss (alopecia areata, tinea capitis)
          - Scarring or scaling (scarring alopecia; requires dermatology)
          - In women: signs of hyperandrogenism (irregular periods, acne, hirsutism) — check PCOS

      🌊Telogen effluvium▾
      
        #### Overview
        Temporary increase in shedding (up to 300 hairs/day); triggered by stress, illness, or medication. Most common reversible hair loss cause.

        #### Common triggers
        
          **Acute:** High fever, surgery, acute illness, emotional stress, trauma
          **Chronic:** Chronic stress, depression, anxiety, sleep deprivation
          **Nutritional:** Iron deficiency, protein malnutrition, vitamin B12/folate deficiency, zinc deficiency
          **Medical:** Thyroid disease (hypo or hyperthyroidism), postpartum period, autoimmune disease
          **Medications:** Retinoids, anticoagulants, beta-blockers, ACE inhibitors, NSAIDs, some anticonvulsants

        #### Clinical presentation
        
          - Diffuse shedding noticed on pillow, in shower, in brush
          - Onset 2-3 months after trigger (hair growth cycle delay)
          - Patient hair pull test may show >3 telogen hairs per 60 hairs

        #### Management
        
          - Identify and address trigger (stress management, treat thyroid disease, correct nutritional deficiencies)
          Iron studies if not recently checked; ferritin 
          - TSH; check B12, folate
          - Stop or reduce culprit medication if possible (in consultation with prescriber)
          - Reassurance: usually self-resolves within 3-6 months of trigger removal
          - Topical minoxidil may speed recovery (optional)

        #### Prognosis
        
          - Acute telogen effluvium: resolves 3-6 months after trigger
          - Chronic telogen effluvium: may persist 6-12+ months; can overlap with androgenetic alopecia

      🔵Alopecia areata▾
      
        #### Overview
        Autoimmune condition causing sudden patchy hair loss; can occur at any age; 1-2% lifetime prevalence.

        #### Clinical presentation
        
          **Alopecia areata:** Well-demarcated round/oval bald patches; smooth scalp (non-scarring); can be single or multiple
          **Alopecia totalis:** Complete scalp hair loss
          **Alopecia universalis:** Loss of all body hair (rare; 1-2% of AA cases)
          **Exclamation mark hairs:** Pathognomonic sign — short hairs that taper toward scalp, seen at margin of active patches

        #### Associated conditions
        
          - Thyroid disease (10-20% of patients)
          - Vitiligo (5-10%)
          - Celiac disease
          - Down syndrome
          - Atopy and allergic diseases

        #### Prognosis
        
          - 50% regrow hair within 1 year without treatment
          - Worse prognosis: early onset, extensive involvement, alopecia totalis, associated autoimmune disease, family history
          - Remission can occur spontaneously but recurrence common (30-50% within 5 years)

        #### Management
        
          **Mild (limited patches):** Intralesional corticosteroid injection (triamcinolone 10 mg/mL) every 4-6 weeks; topical corticosteroids (clobetasol propionate 0.05%)
          **Moderate to severe:** Refer to dermatology; consider systemic corticosteroids, JAK inhibitors (ruxolitinib now approved), topical sensitizers
          **Psychological support:** Significant psychological impact; counseling and support groups helpful
          **Screen for associated conditions:** TSH, tissue transglutaminase (celiac)

      ❓Other causes and investigations▾
      
        #### Other non-scarring alopecia
        
          **Tinea capitis:** Fungal infection; scaling, erythema, sometimes alopecia; 10-KOH preparation or fungal culture
          **Trichotillomania:** Hair-pulling disorder; irregular alopecia, broken hairs, self-induced
          **Anagen effluvium:** Damage to growing hair (chemo, radiation); sudden shedding of hair in anagen phase

        #### Investigations in hair loss
        
          **Hair pull test:** >3 telogen hairs per 60 hairs suggests active shedding
          **Dermoscopy:** Non-invasive; useful in clinic to assess hair shaft, follicles, scaling
          **Scalp biopsy:** Indicated if scarring suspected or diagnosis unclear; dermatologist performs
          **Full blood count:** Iron, ferritin, B12, folate
          **TSH:** Screen for thyroid disease
          **Hormonal assessment (women):** If signs of hyperandrogenism (PCOS) — testosterone, DHEA-S, LH/FSH

        #### When to refer to dermatology
        
          - Scarring alopecia (lichen planopilaris, discoid lupus, central centrifugal cicatricial alopecia)
          - Alopecia areata with >50% involvement or alopecia totalis/universalis
          - Diagnostic uncertainty
          - Failure to respond to standard therapy (finasteride, minoxidil)
          - Scalp inflammation, scaling, or signs of scarring
          - Hair loss with signs of systemic disease
