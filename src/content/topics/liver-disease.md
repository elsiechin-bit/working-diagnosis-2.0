#### Patterns — what the elevations tell you

              PatternDominant enzymesMain causes

                Hepatocellular
                ALT, AST elevated (≫ ALP)
                Viral hepatitis, MASLD, ALD, DILI, autoimmune hepatitis, ischaemia

                Cholestatic
                ALP, GGT elevated (≫ ALT)
                Gallstones, PSC, PBC, drugs, pancreatic mass, cholangiocarcinoma

                Mixed
                ALT, ALP, GGT all elevated
                DILI, infiltrative disease (sarcoid, lymphoma), sepsis

                Isolated GGT
                GGT only (ALT, ALP normal)
                Alcohol use, fatty liver, enzyme induction (phenytoin, rifampicin)

          #### ALT/AST ratio (De Ritis ratio)
          
            **ALT > AST:** typical of MASLD and viral hepatitis (hepatocyte injury).
            **AST:ALT > 2:1** strongly suggests **alcohol-related liver disease**.
            **Very high ALT (>10–20× ULN):** acute hepatitis (viral, ischaemic, toxic), biliary obstruction.

          #### Synthetic function — the markers that matter
          
            **INR / prothrombin time:** most sensitive marker of acute hepatic failure. Prolonged INR in liver disease = poor synthetic function.
            **Albumin:** low in chronic liver disease; also low in malnutrition, nephrotic syndrome, sepsis.
            **Bilirubin:** conjugated (direct) elevated in hepatocellular or cholestatic disease; unconjugated in haemolysis or Gilbert's syndrome.

            Isolated raised bilirubin
            **Gilbert's syndrome** — benign, hereditary, unconjugated hyperbilirubinaemia that worsens with fasting, illness or stress. ALT, ALP and GGT are normal. No treatment needed; diagnose clinically and reassure.

          #### How to approach abnormal LFTs
          
            - Confirm the pattern (hepatocellular vs cholestatic vs isolated GGT).
            Take a thorough history: **alcohol**, medications (including herbals/supplements), metabolic risk factors, viral risk factors.
            If cholestatic: **ultrasound is the first investigation** — excludes biliary obstruction.
            - If hepatocellular: add viral serology (HBsAg, HBcAb, HCV Ab), autoimmune screen (ANA, ASMA, IgG), iron studies, ferritin, caeruloplasmin if <40.
            - Mildly elevated LFTs (<3× ULN) that resolve spontaneously without clear cause can be monitored; persistent >6 weeks warrants investigation.

            Do not ignore the ALP
            Isolated ALP elevation (with normal GGT) is more likely bone origin — check GGT to differentiate. ALP + raised GGT = hepatic cholestasis until proven otherwise. Arrange ultrasound.

        🫀MASLD — metabolic-associated steatotic liver disease▾

            Nomenclature update 2023
            NAFLD (non-alcoholic fatty liver disease) is now renamed **MASLD** (metabolic-associated steatotic liver disease). The inflammatory subtype NASH is now **MASH** (metabolic-associated steatohepatitis). The old term NAFLD implied exclusion of alcohol; MASLD focuses on metabolic drivers.

          #### Diagnosis
          
            - Hepatic steatosis on imaging (ultrasound: echogenic liver) or biopsy.
            - Plus at least one of: overweight/obesity, type 2 diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidaemia, raised waist circumference.
            - No excessive alcohol use (<140 g/week women, <210 g/week men).

          #### Risk stratification in primary care
          The key question is: does this patient have clinically significant fibrosis (F2+)? Use:

              #### FIB-4 score
              **= Age × AST / (platelets × √ALT)**
              **<1.3:** low risk — reassure, lifestyle
              **1.3–2.67:** indeterminate — consider elastography or specialist referral
              **>2.67:** high risk — refer hepatology

              #### Liver stiffness (elastography)
              FibroScan or MR elastography.
              **<8 kPa:** likely F0–F1
              **8–12 kPa:** F2–F3 (significant fibrosis)
              **>12 kPa:** F4 (probable cirrhosis) — refer

          FIB-4 is free, calculated from existing bloods, and validated in MASLD. It should be calculated at first diagnosis and repeated annually in patients with ongoing risk factors.

          #### Management
          
            **Weight loss:** 5–10% body weight loss improves steatosis; >10% may reverse fibrosis and resolve MASH. The most effective intervention.
            **Exercise:** aerobic and resistance exercise both reduce liver fat independent of weight loss. Target 150–300 min moderate intensity per week.
            **Alcohol:** advise abstinence or minimal use — alcohol accelerates fibrosis in MASLD.
            **Control metabolic risk:** optimise blood pressure, lipids, blood glucose. Statins are safe in MASLD — do not withhold due to fear of liver toxicity.
            **GLP-1 receptor agonists** (semaglutide) have shown significant MASH resolution in trials and are increasingly used where available.
            **Resmetirom** (thyroid hormone receptor beta agonist) — first drug FDA-approved specifically for MASH with fibrosis (2024); not yet available in NZ.
            **Avoid hepatotoxic agents:** review all medications, supplements and herbals.

          #### Monitoring
          
            - Annual LFTs and FIB-4 in established MASLD.
            - Reassess FIB-4 at ≥6-monthly intervals if borderline or risk factors are changing.
            - Cirrhosis: 6-monthly liver ultrasound + AFP for HCC surveillance; gastroscopy for varices at diagnosis.

        🍺Alcohol-related liver disease (ALD)▾
        
          #### Spectrum
          ALD ranges from simple steatosis (fatty liver — reversible with abstinence) through **alcoholic hepatitis** to **alcohol-related cirrhosis**. The degree of injury does not correlate perfectly with intake — genetic susceptibility plays a role.

          #### LFT pattern
          
            **AST:ALT ratio >2:1** (usually >2:1, rarely >10:1) — characteristic of ALD.
            - GGT is highly sensitive for alcohol use (enzyme induction even without liver disease).
            - MCV may be elevated (macrocytosis from folate deficiency and direct alcohol toxicity).

          #### Alcoholic hepatitis
          
            - Presents with: jaundice + recent heavy alcohol use + fever + right upper quadrant pain + coagulopathy.
            Discriminant function (Maddrey's score) ≥32 or MELD >20 = severe alcoholic hepatitis — consider **prednisolone** 40 mg/day for 28 days if no contraindications (active infection, GI bleed, hepatorenal syndrome).
            - Lille score at day 7: if >0.45, prednisolone is unlikely to help — stop.
            - All patients: nutritional supplementation, thiamine, folate, abstinence support.

            Acute alcoholic hepatitis — do not miss
            Bilirubin >80 µmol/L + AST:ALT >2:1 + recent heavy drinking + no other cause = alcoholic hepatitis. Severity is high and 28-day mortality can reach 30–50% in severe disease. Urgent hepatology input is needed.

          #### Management of ALD
          
            **Abstinence** is the single most effective intervention at every stage — refer for alcohol support services, consider pharmacotherapy (naltrexone, acamprosate, disulfiram).
            - Nutritional support: high-protein diet, thiamine 100 mg/day, multivitamin.
            - Fatty liver is fully reversible with abstinence — fibrosis can regress.
            - Even in cirrhosis, abstinence significantly improves outcomes and reduces the risk of decompensation.

        🔴Cirrhosis — recognition and management▾
        
          #### Clinical features

              #### Portal hypertension
              Ascites, splenomegaly, caput medusae
              Varices (oesophageal, gastric)
              Thrombocytopenia (hypersplenism)

              #### Hepatocellular failure
              Jaundice, coagulopathy (raised INR)
              Hypoalbuminaemia, oedema
              Hepatic encephalopathy, muscle wasting

              #### Cutaneous signs
              Spider naevi (>5, upper body)
              Palmar erythema, Dupuytren's
              Leukonychia, clubbing, parotid enlargement

              #### Biochemistry
              Low albumin, raised INR
              Raised bilirubin, thrombocytopenia
              Low sodium (late); raised creatinine (HRS)

          #### Decompensation — the four major events

               Ascites
              Diuretics (spironolactone ± furosemide), low-sodium diet. Large-volume: therapeutic paracentesis + albumin (8 g/L drained). Refractory: TIPS, transplant evaluation.

               Variceal haemorrhage
              Acute: terlipressin/octreotide + antibiotics + urgent endoscopy (banding). Secondary prophylaxis: non-selective beta-blocker (propranolol/carvedilol) + variceal banding.

               Hepatic encephalopathy
              Identify and treat precipitant (infection, bleed, constipation, electrolyte imbalance, sedatives). Lactulose first-line; rifaximin for recurrent episodes.

               Spontaneous bacterial peritonitis (SBP)
              Suspect if ascitic neutrophils >250/mm³. Empirical ceftriaxone; IV albumin 1.5 g/kg day 1, 1 g/kg day 3 reduces mortality. Secondary prophylaxis: norfloxacin.

          #### Routine care in compensated cirrhosis
          
            **HCC surveillance:** 6-monthly liver ultrasound ± AFP. AFP alone is insufficient.
            **Varices:** gastroscopy at diagnosis; repeat interval based on findings.
            **Medications to avoid:** NSAIDs, aminoglycosides, metformin (if eGFR <30), sedatives, opioids (reduce dose).
            **Vaccinations:** Hep A, Hep B, pneumococcus, influenza.
            - Consider transplant evaluation for MELD ≥15 or first decompensation.

          #### MELD score
          The Model for End-Stage Liver Disease (MELD) uses bilirubin, INR and creatinine to estimate prognosis. Calculate with an online tool. **MELD ≥15:** consider hepatology referral for transplant assessment. **MELD >20:** high short-term mortality risk.

        🦠Viral hepatitis — Hep B and Hep C▾
        
          #### Hepatitis B (HBV)
          
            MarkerInterpretation
            
              HBsAg +veInfected (acute or chronic). If >6 months = chronic.
              HBsAb +veImmune — vaccinated or resolved infection.
              HBcAb IgMAcute infection.
              HBcAb IgGPast infection (may be resolved or occult).
              HBeAg +veHigh replication, highly infectious.
              HBV DNA (viral load)Key for treatment decisions and monitoring.

          #### When to treat HBV
          
            HBV DNA >2000 IU/mL *and* raised ALT or fibrosis ≥F2.
            - Cirrhosis with detectable HBV DNA — treat regardless of ALT.
            - Immunosuppression planned: start antiviral prophylaxis (tenofovir or entecavir) before immunosuppression.
          
          First-line: **tenofovir alafenamide (TAF)** or **entecavir** — highly potent, low resistance rates. Long-term, often lifelong therapy. Refer to hepatology for initiation.

            Reactivation risk
            HBV reactivation can occur with immunosuppressive therapy (steroids >20 mg >4 weeks, chemotherapy, biologics, rituximab). Screen all patients for HBsAg and HBcAb before starting. Prophylaxis prevents potentially fatal reactivation.

          #### Hepatitis C (HCV)
          
            - Screen: HCV antibody (positive = past or present exposure). Confirm with HCV RNA (quantitative PCR) — if negative, infection has cleared.
            - All patients with chronic HCV (RNA positive) should be treated, regardless of fibrosis stage.
            **Direct-acting antivirals (DAAs):** sofosbuvir/velpatasvir (12 weeks) or glecaprevir/pibrentasvir (8 weeks). >95% sustained virological response (SVR = cure).
            - In NZ: DAAs are funded for all genotypes. Prescribing is accessible through hepatologists, gastroenterologists and some trained GPs/NPs.
            - After SVR: HCC risk reduces but remains elevated if cirrhosis was present — continue 6-monthly ultrasound surveillance.

            Screen broadly for Hep C
            In NZ, universal screening is recommended for all adults born 1945–1985 and anyone with a history of injecting drug use, blood transfusion before 1992, tattooing in unregulated settings, or incarceration. Most chronic HCV is asymptomatic — it will not be found unless you screen.

        💊Drug-induced liver injury (DILI)▾
        
          DILI is a diagnosis of exclusion — consider it in any abnormal LFT when another cause is not found. It can be **hepatocellular, cholestatic or mixed**. Onset is typically within 3 months of starting a drug; some drugs cause delayed injury (months to years).

          #### Management
          
            - Stop the offending agent (if identifiable and safe to do so).
            - Most cases resolve within weeks to months of stopping.
            - Cholestatic DILI may persist for 3–6 months.
            - Refer urgently if: jaundice + coagulopathy (Hy's Law criteria = risk of fatal outcome), encephalopathy, or ALT >10× ULN with rising bilirubin.

            Statins and liver disease
            Statins are safe in MASLD and mild-to-moderate chronic liver disease — the cardiovascular benefit outweighs risk. Routine LFT monitoring during statin therapy is not recommended unless symptomatic. Do not withhold statins because of mildly elevated baseline LFTs (<3× ULN).

        📋Other conditions to consider▾
        
          #### Autoimmune hepatitis (AIH)
          
            - Often middle-aged women; can present as acute hepatitis or as chronic disease found incidentally.
            - Pattern: hepatocellular, raised IgG, positive ANA or ASMA (anti-smooth muscle antibody).
            - Diagnosis requires liver biopsy. Treatment: prednisolone + azathioprine. Refer to hepatology.

          #### Primary biliary cholangitis (PBC)
          
            - Predominantly middle-aged women; fatigue, pruritus, or incidental cholestatic LFTs.
            - AMA (anti-mitochondrial antibody) positive in 95%. Raised IgM.
            - Treatment: ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA) — slows progression. Refer.

          #### Primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC)
          
            - Predominantly men; strongly associated with IBD (especially ulcerative colitis).
            - Cholestatic LFTs + dilated/beaded bile ducts on MRCP. High risk of cholangiocarcinoma.
            - No effective medical treatment; refer for ERCP/MRCP and specialist management.

          #### Haemochromatosis
          
            - HFE gene mutation (most common C282Y homozygote). Check iron studies: raised transferrin saturation (>45%) + ferritin.
            - Confirm with HFE genotyping. Treatment: venesection to reduce iron stores.
            - Screen first-degree relatives.

          #### Wilson's disease
          
            - Autosomal recessive copper accumulation. Present before age 40 — consider in young patients with liver disease, neuropsychiatric symptoms, or haemolysis.
            - Low caeruloplasmin is the screening test. Refer for confirmatory testing (24-hr urinary copper, slit-lamp for Kayser-Fleischer rings).

        🚨When to refer — red flags▾
        
          #### Urgent / same-day
          
            ⚡**Acute liver failure:** jaundice + encephalopathy + coagulopathy — hospital immediately
            ⚡**Variceal haemorrhage:** haematemesis or melaena in known or suspected cirrhosis — emergency
            ⚡**Suspected SBP:** cirrhotic patient with fever, abdominal pain, worsening encephalopathy or ascites — hospital
            ⚡**ALT >10× ULN** with jaundice or coagulopathy (Hy's Law) — same-day referral

          #### Routine referral to hepatology
          
            - FIB-4 >2.67 or liver stiffness >8 kPa — significant fibrosis likely
            - Established cirrhosis — for surveillance and management planning
            - Suspected AIH, PBC, PSC, haemochromatosis, Wilson's
            - Chronic HBV meeting treatment criteria
            - Chronic HCV (for DAA initiation if not prescribing in primary care)
            - LFTs persistently >3× ULN without clear cause after 3–6 months investigation
            - MELD ≥15 or first hepatic decompensation

        💡Pearls & pitfalls▾
        
          #### Pearls
          
            - Calculate FIB-4 in all patients with MASLD — it takes 30 seconds and guides referral and intensity of follow-up.
            - AST:ALT >2:1 is a strong clue for alcohol-related liver disease — ask again about intake.
            - Statins are safe in MASLD and mildly elevated LFTs. The cardiovascular benefit is too important to withhold.
            - Screen for Hep C in all patients born 1945–1985 and anyone with injecting drug use history — most are asymptomatic.
            - Always ask about herbal medicines and supplements — they are a common and often overlooked cause of DILI.
            - Isolated raised GGT with normal ALP and ALT = look at alcohol intake and enzyme-inducing drugs.
          
          #### Pitfalls
          
            - Normal LFTs do not exclude cirrhosis — late cirrhosis may have normal or low ALT as hepatocyte mass is lost.
            - Withholding NSAIDs in cirrhosis: avoid them — they precipitate renal failure and worsen fluid retention.
            - Missing HBV reactivation before immunosuppression — screen every patient starting biological therapy, chemotherapy or high-dose corticosteroids.
            - Diagnosing MASLD without calculating a fibrosis score — the distinction between low-risk and high-risk disease completely changes management.
            - Delaying Hep C treatment because of comorbidities — DAAs are safe, well-tolerated, and cure is available to everyone.
