## Overview


**Breast cancer is the most common malignancy in NZ women,** affecting ~1 in 9 over a lifetime. Approximately 3500 new cases diagnosed annually; ~850 deaths/year (mortality declining due to earlier detection & improved treatments).

        #### Age & risk
        
          **Peak incidence:** Age 65–74 years; median age at diagnosis ~64 years. However, younger women (age 35–49) comprise ~15% of diagnoses & often present with more aggressive disease (higher grade, triple-negative phenotype).
          **Age-standardised incidence:** NZ has one of highest rates globally (~90 per 100,000 women); rates relatively stable past decade.
          **Hereditary breast cancer (5–10% of cases):** BRCA1/BRCA2 mutations confer ~70% lifetime breast cancer risk; PALB2 ~35% risk; TP53 (Li-Fraumeni) very high risk. Genetic testing critical for risk stratification & management.
        
        #### Equity considerations
        
          **Māori & Pacific women:** Lower screening uptake (~60–65% vs 73% European); later-stage diagnosis at presentation; higher mortality rates. Barriers: healthcare access, transport, cultural disconnect from screening services, health literacy.
          **BreastScreen Aotearoa inequity focus:** Te Aho o te Kahu Cancer Control Agency now prioritising equity; culturally responsive approaches, Māori & Pacific provider partnerships, transport assistance, engagement in design of services.
          **Transgender & gender-diverse women:** BreastScreen Aotearoa offers screening to transgender women aged 45–69 (eligibility based on current gender); screening protocols & communication adapted for trans-specific needs.

      🔬BreastScreen Aotearoa: Screening programme overview▾
      
        **BreastScreen Aotearoa is the free national mammography screening programme,** aiming to identify breast cancer at earlier stage when treatment outcomes best. Launched 1998; now screening ~270,000 women annually; ~1300 cancers detected/year (~45% of all NZ breast cancer diagnoses via screening).

        #### Eligibility & age range
        
          **Core eligibility (currently):** Age 45–69 years; asymptomatic (no breast symptoms); no mammogram in past 12 months; NZ health services eligible (resident/citizen).
          **Age expansion (ongoing):** Upper age range gradually expanding to 74 by end 2029. Women 70–74 increasingly eligible; discussion recommended re benefit-harm (lower absolute benefit, higher comorbidity burden in very elderly).
          **Below age 45:** Not routinely eligible for free screening; however, private screening available (cost variable, ~$150–300). Breast Cancer Foundation NZ recommends age 40+ consider yearly mammograms if elevated risk (family history, dense breasts, genetic predisposition).
          **Previously treated breast cancer:** Eligible to re-enter programme if meet criteria; surveillance mammography typically 1–2 yearly depending on treatment history & risk.
        
        #### Screening interval & modality
        
          **Frequency:** Biennial (every 2 years) mammography.
          **Technology:** 2D digital mammography standard; 3D tomosynthesis (digital breast tomosynthesis, DBT) increasingly available (improves detection in dense breast tissue; reduces false-positive rate). DBT not yet universally funded via BreastScreen Aotearoa; private clinics increasingly offer.
          **Quality standards:** Rigorous quality assurance; mammograms double-reported (read by ≥2 radiologists); consistent standards across regions; peer review processes.
        
        #### Screening outcomes & effectiveness
        
          **Detection rate:** Mammography detects 80–90% of breast cancers in screening populations (sensitivity 80–90%; specificity ~95%). Sensitivity lower in dense breast tissue.
          **Mortality reduction:** Regular mammography reduces breast cancer mortality by >30% (some estimates 33%–35%) in screened populations; greatest benefit age 50–69.
          **Stage shift:** ~45% of BSA-detected cancers are stage I; ~30% stage II. Symptomatic (non-screened) diagnoses: ~25% stage I; ~40% stage II or higher.
          **Overdiagnosis:** ~10% of screened cancers identified would not become life-threatening; breast cancer has variable natural history; some cancers indolent. Represents 1 overdiagnosis per ~8–10 cancers detected (acceptable harm-benefit ratio per most guidelines, though controversial).
        
        #### Recall & assessment pathway
        
          **Abnormal screening mammogram (~5–8% of screening mammograms):** Woman recalled for diagnostic assessment (free); includes additional mammographic views, ultrasound (often), clinical examination, possible biopsy.
          **Benign findings (~80–85% of recalls):** Routine screening resumes; interval return in 2 years (or sooner if indicated).
          **Cancer diagnosis (~10–15% of recalls):** Referral to multidisciplinary team (MDT) for treatment planning; staging (imaging, bloods); discussion of surgical, radiation, systemic therapy options.
          **High-risk lesions (DCIS, ADH, etc.):** Management per guidelines; often surgical excision +/– radiation; close surveillance.
        
        #### Access & enrolment
        
          **Enrolment:** Women age 45–74 can enrol via BreastScreen Aotearoa phoneline (0800 270 200), online (Te Puna system), or via GP referral. Automatic invitations sent after first mammogram (2-yearly); women can decline/defer.
          **Mobile screening units:** Travel to regional areas; schedule available via NSU website. Important access for rural/provincial women.
          **Barriers & support:** Transport assistance available via some PHOs; culturally appropriate support (Māori, Pacific, refugee communities); translated materials (multiple languages).

      ⚠️Risk assessment & stratification▾
      
        **Breast cancer risk is heterogeneous;** age alone insufficient for risk stratification. Risk assessment combines demographic, hormonal, reproductive, family history, and genetic factors.

        #### Risk factors (non-genetic)
        
          **Age:** Risk increases exponentially with age; ~1 in 30 at age 40; 1 in 9 by age 60–69; 1 in 6 by age 80.
          **Reproductive history:** Nulliparity (never pregnancies) ↑ risk (~30% higher). Late first pregnancy (>30 years) ↑ risk (~20% vs age 20–24). Short lactation (
          **Hormone replacement therapy (HRT):** Estrogen-progestin HRT ↑ risk ~20–30% during use; risk declines post-cessation (returns to baseline within ~5 years). Estrogen-only HRT minimal increased risk.
          **Oral contraceptives:** Slight ↑ risk (~20%) during use; returns to baseline post-cessation. Benefit of contraception often outweighs small cancer risk.
          **Breast density:** High breast density (~4x increased risk vs fatty breasts); ~50% of women age 40–50 have dense breasts; densities decline with age, parity, HRT use.
          **Alcohol:** Each drink/day ~10% increased risk; risk dose-dependent.
          **Obesity (post-menopausal):** ~20–30% increased risk (estrone production in adipose); pre-menopausal effect unclear.
          **Physical inactivity:** ↑ risk; regular exercise ↓ risk ~20–30%.
          **Personal history of breast cancer:** ~20% lifetime risk of contralateral breast cancer; higher if LCIS, DCIS, or younger age at first diagnosis.
          **Prior benign breast disease:** Complex fibroadenomas, papillomas, atypical hyperplasia (ADH, ALH, LCIS) → elevated risk (category depends on histology); high-risk lesions warrant closer surveillance.
        
        #### Genetic risk factors
        
          **BRCA1 & BRCA2 mutations:** Account for ~5–10% of breast cancers. BRCA1 confers ~72% lifetime breast cancer risk; BRCA2 ~69%. Also confer increased ovarian cancer risk (BRCA1 ~44%, BRCA2 ~17%), melanoma, pancreatic cancer (particularly BRCA2). Autosomal dominant inheritance; 50% transmission to offspring.
          **PALB2 mutations:** ~2% of hereditary breast cancer; ~35% lifetime breast cancer risk. Also associated with pancreatic cancer.
          **TP53 mutations (Li-Fraumeni syndrome):** Rare but very high risk (~90% lifetime breast cancer, often young onset). Multiple cancer predispositions (sarcoma, adrenal, leukemia, brain).
          **Other moderate-risk genes:** PTEN (Cowden syndrome), CHEK2, ATM, and others account for smaller proportion; ongoing discovery.
        
        #### Risk assessment tools
        
          **Tyrer-Cuzick v8 (T-C8):** Web-based tool incorporating age, family history (BRCA1/2 prediction algorithm embedded), reproductive factors, HRT use, breast density. Calculates 10-year & lifetime breast cancer risk. Widely used internationally; increasingly adopted in NZ for risk stratification & screening recommendation.
          **Gail model:** Simpler tool; calculates 5-year & lifetime risk using age, race, reproductive history, number of prior biopsies, family history. Less precise than T-C8; particularly underestimates high-risk women.
          **NZ-specific risk assessment:** Genetic Health Service NZ (GHSNZ) uses validated tools (BOADICEA, others) for genetic risk prediction in families with breast cancer clustering. Three-generation family history required for assessment.

      🧬Genetic testing & Genetic Health Service NZ▾
      
        **Genetic testing for hereditary breast cancer is offered through Genetic Health Service NZ (GHSNZ),** publicly funded for eligible individuals. Testing includes BRCA1, BRCA2, PALB2, TP53 (and additional genes in some cases).

        #### Testing criteria & referral
        
          **GHSNZ referral criteria (broadly):** Breast cancer diagnosed age 
          **Genetic counselling:** Pre-test counselling essential (discuss inheritance, implications for family, psychological impact, privacy). Counselling provided by genetic counsellors (often prior to testing decision).
          **Testing pathway:** If cancer patient available, testing typically begins with affected individual (higher yield). If no living affected family, testing may start in unaffected at-risk family members (lower sensitivity).
          **Timeline:** Referral to result ~3–6 months (can be longer if extended multigene panels or family studies required).
        
        #### Mutation frequency & implications
        
          **BRCA1/BRCA2 carrier frequency:** ~1 in 300–500 general population (higher in Ashkenazi Jewish, Icelandic, other founder populations). Among NZ breast cancer patients, ~9% carry pathogenic variants in BRCA1/2.
          **Heterozygous carriers (BRCA1/2):** Breast cancer risk ~70% lifetime; ovarian cancer ~40–45% (BRCA1) or ~17–20% (BRCA2). Male carriers: ~5–10% breast cancer risk, elevated prostate cancer (BRCA2).
          **Homozygous carriers:** Extremely rare; congenital abnormalities, severe immunodeficiency (typically lethal in childhood).
          **Variants of uncertain significance (VUS):** ~10–15% of genetic tests reveal VUS (functional consequence unknown). Interpretation evolving as database grows; re-classification possible.
        
        #### Post-test management (BRCA1/2 positive carriers)
        
          **Surveillance:** Mammography + MRI annually (starting age 30–35 or earlier depending on family history; MRI offers higher sensitivity in younger, denser-breasted women).
          **Chemoprevention:** Tamoxifen (reduces risk ~50%) for premenopausal; aromatase inhibitors for postmenopausal. Discussed with oncology; decision on tolerability vs risk reduction.
          **Preventive surgery:** Risk-reducing mastectomy (~99% risk reduction, though not 100%; also prevents ovarian surveillance burden). Risk-reducing salpingo-oophorectomy (RRSO; at menopause or age 40–45 if premenopausal); reduces breast cancer risk ~30% + ovarian cancer ~99%. Fertility implications; hormone replacement post-surgery needed.
          **Family screening:** Cascade genetic testing offered to at-risk family members (siblings, offspring, parents). Improved outcomes if family members identified & offered surveillance/prevention prior to diagnosis.
        
        #### NZ-specific genetic service considerations
        
          **Regional variation:** GHSNZ provides centralised service; waitlists variable by region. Private genetic testing available (cost ~$1500–2500 for single-gene, more for multigene panels); coverage via private insurance variable.
          **Equity in genetic services:** Underrepresentation of Māori in genetic databases; risk assessment algorithms & testing recommendations developed on predominantly European cohorts (may misclassify risk in other populations). Ongoing work to improve equity; culturally appropriate counselling essential.

      🩺Diagnostic pathway & symptomatic breast disease▾
      
        **~55% of NZ breast cancers present symptomatically** (lump, pain, discharge, skin changes). Prompt evaluation essential for earlier-stage diagnosis.

        #### Presenting symptoms & red flags
        
          **Palpable lump:** Most common presenting symptom (~70–80% of presentations). Urgent assessment warranted (cancer risk ~10–15% if new lump in women >50 years; lower in younger women but still significant).
          **Nipple discharge:** Spontaneous, unilateral, bloody/blood-stained → high suspicion for malignancy. Bilateral, expressed only with pressure, or clear/green → benign (ectasia, fibrocystic).
          **Skin changes:** Dimpling, peau d'orange (pitting edema), erythema, ulceration → advanced local disease or inflammatory breast cancer (rare, ~3%; aggressive).
          **Nipple changes:** Inversion (recent onset, unilateral) → concern for underlying mass/retraction.
          **Pain:** Breast pain alone (mastalgia) rarely indicates malignancy (~1–2% risk), but bilateral persistent pain + lump warrants imaging. Localised pain at site of lump → higher concern.
        
        #### Diagnostic assessment
        
          **Clinical examination:** Systematic assessment bilateral breast, axilla, supraclavicular nodes. Documentation of size, consistency, fixation, skin involvement, nipple changes.
          **Imaging (triple assessment):** Clinical exam + imaging + (±) tissue diagnosis optimises diagnostic accuracy (~95–99%).
          **Mammography:** First-line imaging age >35–40 years (sensitivity ~85–90%; specificity ~90%). Diagnostic mammography (multiple views) for symptomatic women; additional ultrasound if mammogram inconclusive or woman has dense tissue.
          **Ultrasound:** Adjunct to mammography; excellent for evaluating palpable lumps, distinguishing solid from cystic. No radiation. First-line imaging in young women (
          **MRI:** High sensitivity (~95%) but lower specificity; useful for staging in diagnosed cancer, evaluating dense breast tissue, screening high-risk individuals (BRCA carriers). Not first-line diagnostic tool due to cost, availability, false-positive rate.
          **Tissue diagnosis:** Fine-needle aspiration cytology (FNAC), core needle biopsy (CNB; preferred), or excision biopsy for inconclusive imaging or high suspicion despite benign imaging. Biopsy gold standard; definitively establishes diagnosis.
        
        #### Referral pathway (GP to specialist)
        
          **Urgent referral (2-week rule):** Palpable lump, nipple discharge (spontaneous, unilateral), skin/nipple changes, or suspicious imaging. Referral to breast surgeon or dedicated breast clinic for assessment, imaging, biopsy if needed.
          **Non-urgent referral:** Diffuse mastalgia without lump, benign-appearing imaging; may be managed in primary care or routine outpatient clinic.
          **NZ access:** Public referral via GP (2-week urgent pathway common); private breast surgeons also available (cost-dependent). Rural areas may have longer wait times; telemedicine consultations emerging.

      💊Management overview & treatment principles▾
      
        **Breast cancer treatment is multidisciplinary,** individualised to stage, subtype (HR status, HER2, triple-negative), molecular features (genomic signatures), patient age/comorbidities, and patient preference. Overview below; detailed treatment protocols beyond scope of primary care but familiarity essential for counselling & shared decision-making.

        #### Staging & prognostic factors
        
          **TNM staging:** Tumour size (T), regional lymph node involvement (N), distant metastasis (M) determine stage (I–IV). Stage I–III locoregional disease; stage IV metastatic (incurable, palliative intent).
          **Hormone receptor (HR) status:** Estrogen receptor (ER) & progesterone receptor (PR) positive (ER/PR+) ~70% of cancers; generally lower grade, better prognosis, benefit from hormonal therapy.
          **HER2 status:** HER2+ ~15–20% cancers; often more aggressive; benefit from HER2-targeted therapy (trastuzumab, others).
          **Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC):** ER/PR/HER2− ~15% cancers; aggressive biology; chemotherapy primary systemic option (no targeted endocrine/HER2 therapy); prognosis intermediate-to-poor despite treatment. BRCA1 mutations enriched in TNBC (~20% TNBC carry BRCA1). PARP inhibitors (olaparib, talazoparib) emerging for BRCA-mutant TNBC (improving outcomes).
          **Grade:** Histological grade (Nottingham/Bloom-Richardson) predicts aggressiveness; grade 3 → chemotherapy more likely recommended.
          **Genomic signatures:** Multigene assays (Oncotype DX, MammaPrint, others; limited NZ availability, mostly private) provide recurrence risk stratification in ER/PR+ early disease; help guide adjuvant chemotherapy decision. Some used in HER2+ disease post-trastuzumab.
        
        #### Surgery
        
          **Breast-conserving therapy (BCT):** Lumpectomy/wide local excision + radiation (if ER/PR+ can de-escalate radiation in selected cases). Preferred for stage I–II if feasible (patient preference, adequate margin, no contraindication to radiation). Equivalent survival vs mastectomy; superior cosmesis.
          **Mastectomy:** Indicated for stage III, inflammatory, diffuse microcalcifications, prior radiation, patient preference, or inadequate margin on BCT. Axillary dissection (full) vs sentinel node biopsy (SNB) depending on nodal status (SNB for node-negative disease; dissection if positive nodes or macrometastasis detected).
          **Reconstructive surgery:** Offered post-mastectomy (implant-based, autologous, or hybrid); timing (immediate vs delayed) individualised. Important for psychosocial outcomes.
        
        #### Radiation therapy
        
          **Adjuvant radiation post-BCT:** Standard (~50 Gy to breast, boost to tumour bed); reduces locoregional recurrence ~50% & breast cancer mortality ~15%. Omitted only if very low-risk (age >70, grade 1, ER+, 
          **Post-mastectomy radiation:** Indicated if high-risk features (≥4 positive nodes, T3/T4, close/positive margins, lymphovascular invasion). Improves locoregional control & overall survival.
          **Palliative radiation:** Bone metastases, brain mets, chest wall recurrence → pain relief, symptom control, occasional prolonged survival if oligometastatic.
        
        #### Systemic therapy
        
          **Endocrine therapy (ER/PR+ disease):** Tamoxifen (premenopausal), aromatase inhibitors (postmenopausal), ±ovarian suppression (in premenopausal). Standard 5–10 years adjuvant (reduces recurrence ~40–50%, mortality ~30%). Side-effects: hot flushes, VTE (tamoxifen), osteoporosis, sexual dysfunction.
          **Chemotherapy:** Indicated in high-grade, node-positive, HER2+, TNBC, or high-risk ER/PR+ (intermediate-high genomic risk score). Neoadjuvant (pre-surgery to downstage) or adjuvant (post-surgery). Standard regimens: AC-T (doxorubicin/cyclophosphamide → paclitaxel) or other combinations. ~25% absolute reduction in recurrence risk (variable by subtype).
          **HER2-targeted therapy (HER2+ disease):** Trastuzumab (Herceptin) standard, ±pertuzumab (Perjeta). Combined with chemotherapy neoadjuvant/adjuvant; ±endocrine therapy if ER/PR+ co-expression. Improves DFS & OS ~30–40%. Cardiotoxicity concern (LVEF monitoring required).
          **CDK4/6 inhibitors (ER/PR+ advanced disease):** Palbociclib, ribociclib, abemaciclib; combined with endocrine therapy or fulvestrant in metastatic ER+ HER2− disease. Improve progression-free survival; used in metastatic setting primarily (emerging in adjuvant high-risk).
          **PARP inhibitors (BRCA-mutant or HRD+ tumours):** Olaparib, talazoparib; showing benefit in BRCA1/2-mutant TNBC & ER+ metastatic disease. Emerging in adjuvant setting for high-risk carriers.
          **Immunotherapy:** PD-L1 inhibitors (atezolizumab, durvalumab) increasingly used in triple-negative neoadjuvant/metastatic disease (improves pCR & OS in select patients). Still evolving; limited NZ public funding (private cost significant).
        
        #### Metastatic disease (stage IV)
        
          **Palliative intent:** Median OS ~2–3 years (range 6 months → 10+ years depending on subtype, burden, ER/PR status). Goals: symptom control, quality of life, delay progression. Sequential systemic therapy (not combination for toxicity unless specific indications).
          **Oligometastatic disease:** If ≤3 metastases & controlled primary, aggressive treatment (stereotactic body RT, surgery) may improve outcome (emerging approach; still evolving evidence).

      📋GP role in breast cancer management & follow-up▾
      
        #### Primary prevention & risk reduction counselling
        
          **Modifiable risk factors:** Counsel on alcohol limitation, physical activity (150 min/week moderate activity), weight management (especially postmenopausal obesity), HRT minimisation (use lowest effective dose, shortest duration). Evidence for 20–30% risk reduction with lifestyle changes.
          **Chemoprevention:** Discuss tamoxifen (premenopausal) or aromatase inhibitors (postmenopausal) in high-risk women (≥1.7% 5-year risk per some guidelines; cost-benefit individualised). ~50% risk reduction; side-effects (VTE, endometrial, hot flushes) require informed discussion.
        
        #### Screening counselling & BreastScreen Aotearoa enrolment
        
          **Age 45–49:** Discuss benefits (30% mortality reduction in screened populations) vs harms (false-positives, overdiagnosis ~10%, anxiety). Some guidelines now support routine screening age 45–49; others individualised discussion. NZ trend toward expanding age downward (BSA now including 45+).
          **Age 50–69:** Recommend enrolment in BreastScreen Aotearoa (highest benefit-harm ratio). If not enrolled, encourage (call 0800 270 200 or online).
          **Age 70–74:** Expanding eligibility; discuss with patient (lower absolute benefit; higher comorbidity burden; decision individualised). Breast Cancer Foundation NZ decision aid available.
          **Above age 75:** Consider individual health status, life expectancy, prior screening history; routine screening not recommended.
        
        #### Management of abnormal imaging/recalls
        
          **Support & reassurance:** Recall anxiety high; reassure that ~80–85% recalls benign. Prepare for likely assessment pathway (additional imaging, possible biopsy). Offer support (counselling, information).
          **Follow-up coordination:** Ensure woman attends recall appointment; clarify results with radiology/breast surgeon; communicate findings back to patient once confirmed.
        
        #### Post-treatment follow-up (early breast cancer survivors)
        
          **Surveillance imaging:** Typically managed by specialist (breast surgeon, oncology). GP role: facilitate access, ensure adherence to surveillance schedule, be aware of typical schedules (varies by stage, treatment; usually clinical exam + imaging every 3–6 months for first 2 years, then annually).
          **Late side-effects monitoring:** Cardiotoxicity (HER2-targeted, anthracyclines; ECG/ECHO baseline, periodic); radiation-induced secondary malignancy; lymphoedema; cognitive impairment ("chemo brain"); sexual dysfunction; bone health (if on AI).
          **Comorbidity management:** CVD risk (especially HER2/anthracycline exposure; lipid management, BP control); osteoporosis screening/treatment (aromatase inhibitor users, premenopausal with ovarian suppression); mental health (depression/anxiety post-diagnosis common; access psychology/counselling if needed).
          **Contralateral breast surveillance:** If prior BC, ~1–2% annual risk of contralateral BC; continue screening (BreastScreen Aotearoa if eligible, or private if high-risk genetic features). Discuss risk-reducing options (contralateral mastectomy, surveillance, chemoprevention) if BRCA+ or very high risk.
          **Genetic counselling (if not done at diagnosis):** Refer for risk assessment & cascade testing in family if appropriate (especially if young age at diagnosis, TNBC, family history).
        
        #### Palliative & supportive care
        
          **Advanced disease:** GP often primary contact for symptom management (pain, nausea, fatigue, dyspnea); collaboration with palliative care & oncology. Advance care planning discussion (goals of care, resuscitation status, end-of-life wishes).
          **Psychological support:** Refer to psycho-oncology, support groups (Breast Cancer Foundation NZ offers); access counselling if depression/anxiety present.
          **Financial support:** Welfare entitlements, transport assistance, employment protections; GP aware of resources available locally.
