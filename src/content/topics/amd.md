## Overview


**AMD is the leading cause of irreversible vision loss in the elderly in developed nations.** It affects the macula, responsible for central (reading, face recognition) vision. Characterized by age-related degeneration of retinal pigment epithelium (RPE), photoreceptors, and underlying choroid, driven by genetic & environmental factors (age, smoking, hypertension, diet).

        #### Dry AMD (atrophic; 85% of cases)
        **Pathophysiology:** Accumulation of lipid-rich deposits (drusen) beneath RPE → progressive RPE & photoreceptor death → geographic atrophy (GA). Driven by complement dysregulation, oxidative stress, inflammation.

          **Early/intermediate:** Small drusen, subtle RPE changes; slowly progressive; most remain asymptomatic or stable
          **Advanced (geographic atrophy):** Focal or confluent RPE loss with photoreceptor degeneration; sharp demarcation; central or peripheral; progressive vision loss (reading difficulty, navigation impaired)
          **Natural history:** 10–15% of intermediate AMD progress to advanced AMD within 10 years without treatment
        
        #### Wet AMD (exudative, neovascular; 15% of cases)
        **Pathophysiology:** Abnormal choroidal neovascularization (CNV) in response to RPE damage → vessel leakage of blood & fluid beneath neurosensory retina → rapid vision loss, scarring.

          **Presentation:** Metamorphopsia (wavy lines), scotoma (dark spot), sudden vision decline; ophthalmoscopy shows hard exudate, retinal thickening, blood
          **Risk:** ~50% conversion of wet AMD in one eye to fellow eye within 5 years; screening of asymptomatic eye critical
          **Prognosis without treatment:** Progressive vision loss; legal blindness common within 2 years
        
        #### Risk factors
        
          **Non-modifiable:** Age (incidence rises sharply >75), family history (genetic component ~60%), white race (Caucasian >3× risk vs Black/Asian populations)
          **Modifiable:** Smoking (4× risk; dose-dependent), hypertension, dyslipidaemia, diabetes, BMI, light exposure, low antioxidant intake (vitamins C/E, lutein, zeaxanthin, zinc)

      🔍Clinical diagnosis & grading▾
      
        #### Symptoms & presentation
        
          **Dry AMD:** Often asymptomatic in early stages; metamorphopsia, dim/blurry central vision, difficulty reading or with low lighting (intermediate–advanced); scotoma if GA affects fovea
          **Wet AMD:** Acute/subacute metamorphopsia (wavy lines), scotoma, blurred vision; visual decline over days–weeks
          **Amsler grid:** Simple home screening tool; patient reports wavy lines or blank areas = urgent ophthalmology referral
        
        #### Clinical examination
        
          **Best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA)** – establish baseline; critical for monitoring
          **Dilated fundoscopy:** Drusen (yellow deposits), RPE changes, GA (sharp-demarcated pale areas), exudates, blood, fluid
          **Optical coherence tomography (OCT):** Gold standard; visualises drusen, RPE-Bruch's membrane interface, GA, subretinal fluid, choroidal neovascularization, central foveal thickness
          **Fluorescein angiography (FA):** Identifies CNV in wet AMD; shows classic vs occult patterns
          **Fundus autofluorescence (FAF):** Highlights drusen, GA margins, RPE dysfunction

      💊Dry AMD management: 2024–2025 breakthrough therapies▾
      
        **First therapies approved for dry AMD (August 2023–2025).** Complement C3 inhibition & photobiomodulation represent paradigm shift from "no treatment" era.

        #### Complement inhibitors (FDA-approved 2023–2024)
        
          **Pegcetacoplan (Fovista, Apellis):** C3 complement inhibitor; intravitreal injection every 2 weeks. DRAMATIC trial: slowed GA progression 34% over 2 years vs placebo. First-line for advanced dry AMD with GA.
          **Avacincaptad pegol (Izervay, Ivera):** Alternative complement C5 inhibitor; intravitreal monthly. FILLY trial: slowed GA progression 27% vs placebo.
          **Mechanism:** Complement dysregulation drives drusen formation & RPE/photoreceptor death; blocking C3/C5 reduces inflammation & slows atrophy
          **Safety profile:** Generally well-tolerated; injection site reactions, transient vision changes; long-term data emerging
        
        #### Photobiomodulation (LED therapy)
        
          **Valeda Light Delivery System (FDA-approved 2024):** Non-invasive; red & near-infrared light therapy to boost retinal mitochondrial function
          **Treatment:** In-office session once weekly × 37 weeks
          **Efficacy:** Modest; slows GA progression ~20% vs sham; best suited for early–intermediate AMD
          **Accessibility:** Non-invasive; patient-friendly; emerging option for earlier intervention
        
        #### Emerging & experimental (pipeline 2025–2026)
        
          **Gene therapy:** ABBV-RGX-314, 4D-150 (AAV-delivered anti-VEGF or multi-target) – one-time intravitreal injection; Phase 2 data promising; aim to reduce injection burden
          **WNT pathway agonists, mitochondrial therapeutics (elamipretide):** Phase 2 trials underway for GA protection
        
        #### Supportive care
        
          **Nutritional supplementation:** AREDS2 formula (vitamins C, E, zinc, lutein, zeaxanthin) slows progression of intermediate AMD; no benefit in early AMD
          **Smoking cessation:** Most modifiable risk factor; halves progression risk
          **Diet:** Mediterranean-style (antioxidants, omega-3) associated with slower progression
          **Laser:** Thermal, photodynamic lasers NO LONGER recommended for dry AMD alone (no proven efficacy; risk of damage)

      💉Wet AMD: anti-VEGF therapy▾
      
        **Anti-VEGF intravitreal injections are gold standard & proven to stabilise or improve vision in wet AMD.** Revolutionised treatment since 2006.

        #### Anti-VEGF agents (FDA-approved)
        
          Agent (brand)MechanismDosingPros/cons
          
            **Ranibizumab (Lucentis)**Humanised anti-VEGF-A antibody fragment0.5 mg IVT monthly or PRN (as-needed)Excellent evidence; rapid systemic clearance; more frequent injections
            **Bevacizumab (Avastin, off-label)**Full-length anti-VEGF-A antibody1.25 mg IVT monthlyOff-label for AMD; cheaper; systemic VEGF suppression; increased systemic exposure
            **Aflibercept (Eylea)**Recombinant VEGF receptor decoy2 mg IVT every 2 months (after 3 monthly)Longer durability; fewer injections; superior systemic PK; excellent efficacy

        #### Treatment protocols
        
          **Loading phase:** 3 consecutive monthly injections, then maintenance interval (every 4–12 weeks depending on agent & response)
          **As-needed (PRN):** Treat only if fluid/exudation recurs on imaging; reduces injection burden but requires frequent monitoring
          **Treat-and-extend:** Extend interval between injections if dry on OCT; individualised to patient response
          **Goal:** Stabilise or improve BCVA; eliminate intraretinal/subretinal fluid; prevent scar formation
        
        #### Outcomes & durability
        
          **Efficacy:** ~70% stabilise vision; 30% improve BCVA with intensive treatment. 10–15% continue to decline despite treatment (treatment resistance)
          **Cumulative improvement:** Early intensive treatment (monthly ×3) followed by maintenance is superior to PRN from start
          **Long-term:** Treatment is typically lifelong; cessation leads to CNV reactivation & vision loss
        
        #### Safety & systemic effects
        
          **Local ocular:** Endophthalmitis (0.02–0.1% per injection), retinal tear/detachment, cataract, corneal abrasion (rare)
          **Systemic:** All three agents reach systemic circulation; aflibercept & bevacizumab have greater systemic VEGF suppression than ranibizumab; clinical significance unclear but counsel on theoretical risks
          **Monitoring:** Intraocular pressure, anterior chamber (endothelitis signs), OCT response; patient education on red-flag symptoms (pain, floaters, flashing lights)

      🔍NZ clinical pathway & primary care role▾
      
        **Primary care: screening, symptom recognition, urgent referral; not diagnostic/therapeutic delivery.** Most AMD management (diagnosis, monitoring, injections) is secondary/tertiary ophthalmology.

        #### Primary care responsibilities
        
          **Screening (adults >55):** Routine dilated fundus exam during check-ups; identify drusen, RPE changes, ask about vision changes
          **Risk factor modification:** Smoking cessation counselling (highest-impact modifiable risk); BP/lipid control
          **Patient education:** Amsler grid home screening; report metamorphopsia immediately
          **Recognition of acute wet AMD:** Sudden blurred vision + metamorphopsia = same-day ophthalmology referral
          **AREDS2 supplementation:** Advise intermediate AMD patients on formal antioxidant supplementation
        
        #### Referral triggers to ophthalmology
        
          **Urgent (same-day):** Acute vision loss with metamorphopsia (suspected wet AMD)
          **Routine (1–2 weeks):** Drusen identified on screening; history of AMD in fellow eye; family history + vision decline
          **Surveillance:** Established dry AMD typically managed by optometrist/ophthalmologist; co-management with GP for general health (smoking, cardiovascular risk)
        
        #### NZ-specific notes
        
          **Anti-VEGF therapy:** Publicly funded through DHB hospital ophthalmology for wet AMD; strict criteria (vision acuity, CNV type). Some patients access private services (cost $1000–2000 NZD per injection).
          **Complement inhibitor access:** Emerging 2024–2025; check current PHARMAC/DHB funding status; likely private or restricted access initially
          **Low-vision services:** Rehabilitative support through community care, local blindness services; often underutilised; GP referral encouraged for quality-of-life optimisation

      💡Clinical pearls & pitfalls▾
      
        #### Pearls
        
          ✦**Wet AMD is an ophthalmology emergency.** Acute vision loss + metamorphopsia = same-day referral. Treatment window is days; delays lead to irreversible scarring & permanent vision loss.
          ✦**Complement inhibitors now offer hope for dry AMD.** First approved therapies slow GA progression 27–34%. Refer intermediate/advanced dry AMD to ophthalmology for consideration.
          ✦**Smoking is the highest-modifiable risk factor.** Smokers have 4× risk; cessation halves progression rate. Strong counselling yields meaningful vision preservation.
          ✦**Anti-VEGF is long-term commitment.** Wet AMD requires lifelong treatment; cessation = CNV reactivation. Manage expectations upfront; NOT a cure, but vision-preserving.
          ✦**AREDS2 reduces progression in intermediate AMD.** Formal antioxidant supplementation (C, E, zinc, lutein, zeaxanthin) slow GA growth ~25–30%; worth counselling intermediate cases.
          ✦**50% of wet AMD will affect fellow eye within 5 years.** Screen asymptomatic eye regularly; educate on Amsler grid; prophylactic treatment debatable but close surveillance mandatory.
          ✦**Family history is powerful risk factor (~60% genetic).** Relatives of AMD patients warrant early screening & aggressive modifiable risk factor reduction.
        
        #### Pitfalls
        
          **Delay in wet AMD diagnosis/referral.** Vision window is narrow; treatment resistance increases with delay. Do not assume "needs an appointment"; send same-day to ED or on-call ophthalmology.
          **Assuming all central vision loss is AMD.** Differential: macular holes, central serous chorioretinopathy, retinitis pigmentosa, myopic CNV. Refer for imaging if uncertain.
          **Not counselling smokers adequately.** Smoking cessation is the single most impactful intervention; GPs are positioned to reinforce it repeatedly.
          **Missing drusen on screening.** Requires dilated exam under adequate lighting; if unsure, refer to optometrist or ophthalmology rather than labelling as "normal".
          **Not discussing AREDS2 in intermediate AMD.** Evidence supports supplementation; simple intervention, well-tolerated, reduces progression ~25%.
          **Overlooking fellow-eye wet AMD risk.** Patients with wet AMD in one eye need education on monitoring other eye; Amsler grid, regular check-ups, low threshold for reporting symptoms.
