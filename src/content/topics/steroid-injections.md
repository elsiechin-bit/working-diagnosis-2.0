#### Indications for steroid injection
        
          - Osteoarthritis (OA) with inflammatory component and pain not controlled by conservative measures
          - Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) — bridge therapy while awaiting DMARD effect
          - Soft tissue rheumatism: bursitis, tendinopathy, tenosynovitis
          - Joint effusions (therapeutic + diagnostic aspiration)
          - Crystalline arthropathies (gout, pseudogout) — to abort acute attacks

        #### Absolute contraindications
        
          **Suspected septic joint:** Aspiration and culture MUST precede injection; intra-articular steroid in infected joint worsens outcome
          **Overlying skin infection or cellulitis**
          **Systemic infection or bacteremia** (delay injection 2 weeks post-treatment)

        #### Relative contraindications
        
          Anticoagulation or bleeding diathesis (proceed with caution; can inject if INR 
          - Severe immunosuppression (increased infection risk)
          - Uncontrolled diabetes (higher infection risk; counsel patient)
          - Allergy to steroid or local anaesthetic

        #### Consent and documentation
        
          Explain procedure: needle puncture, risk of infection (rare 
          - Warn: transient increase in pain in 10-15% of patients; manage with analgesics and ice
          - Document: joint aspirated/injected, volume, appearance of fluid (if aspirated), steroid dose, response
          - Provide written post-injection care instructions (rest, ice, analgesia, review if no improvement in 3 weeks)

        #### Local anaesthetic
        
          **1% lignocaine (lidocaine):** Onset 10-15 min; duration 1-2 hours; volume 2-5 mL depending on joint
          **0.5% bupivacaine:** Slower onset (20 min); longer duration (4-6 hours); useful if patient needs immediate pain relief
          - Can mix steroid and local anaesthetic in same syringe (check compatibility) or inject sequentially

          Post-injection precautions
          Advise rest for 24-48 hours post-injection. Patients can resume normal activity after this period. "Flare" (temporary increase in pain) occurs in 10-15%; manage with analgesia, ice and reassurance.

      🦵Knee injection▾
      
        Most common joint injected in primary care. Straightforward anatomy; high success rate.

          Medial infrapatellar approach (most common)
          **Anatomy:** Entry through infrapatellar tendon at its medial edge; needle advanced posteriorly to enter joint space
          **Landmark:** Identify patellar tendon; inject at its medial border, just below the patella
          
          **Technique:**
          
            1
            Patient supine or seated with knee slightly flexed (15-20°)

            2
            Palpate patellar tendon; mark medial edge with pen

            3
            Clean skin with chlorhexidine or iodine; allow to dry

            4
            Infiltrate skin and subcutaneous tissue with 1% lignocaine

            5
            Using 25G needle on 10 mL syringe, insert at 45° angle medial to patellar tendon, just below patella

            6
            Advance needle posteriorly; feel for "pop" as joint capsule is penetrated

            7
            Aspirate to confirm needle position (non-bloody fluid = correct); inject steroid

            8
            Withdraw needle; apply dressing

          **Steroid dose:** Methylprednisolone 20-40 mg or triamcinolone 20-40 mg
          **Success rate:** ~90% on first attempt with palpation; higher with ultrasound guidance

          Lateral parapatellar approach (alternative)
          **Anatomy:** Entry lateral to patellar tendon; technically easier if medial approach fails
          **Technique:** Same as medial approach, but insert needle lateral to patellar tendon at same depth

        #### Tips for success
        
          - Flex knee slightly to relax joint capsule
          - Feel for the "pop" as needle penetrates joint space
          - Aspiration before injection reduces risk of intra-articular haematoma
          - If difficult to feel pop, ask patient to flex and extend knee — movement helps locate joint space
          - Ultrasound guidance greatly increases success rate and reduces complications

      💪Shoulder injection (glenohumeral joint)▾
      
        Second most common; more technically difficult than knee. Anatomical landmarks are key.

          Anterior approach (most common in primary care)
          **Anatomy:** Entry through anterior joint space between humeral head and glenoid fossa
          **Landmark:** Coracoid process (bony prominence below and medial to acromion)
          
          **Technique:**
          
            1
            Patient seated or supine with arm relaxed at side, internally rotated (hand on abdomen)

            2
            Palpate coracoid process (prominent bony point just medial to shoulder joint)

            3
            Mark point 1-2 cm lateral and slightly inferior to coracoid tip

            4
            Clean skin; infiltrate with 1% lignocaine

            5
            Using 25G needle, insert at point marked, directed posteriorly and slightly medially at 45-60° angle

            6
            Advance 2-3 cm until resistance felt (joint capsule); withdraw slightly and inject

            7
            Inject steroid slowly

          **Steroid dose:** Methylprednisolone 20-40 mg or triamcinolone 20-40 mg
          **Success rate:** ~70% with palpation; 90%+ with ultrasound

        #### Alternative: Posterior approach
        
          **Anatomy:** Entry posterior to shoulder joint (from back)
          **Landmark:** 2-3 cm below posterolateral aspect of acromion
          **Advantage:** Less traumatic to rotator cuff; lower risk of neurovascular injury
          **Needle direction:** Directed anteriorly and slightly medially toward anterior joint space

        #### Pitfalls to avoid
        
          - Injecting into bursa instead of joint (subacromial bursa is common error)
          - Going too deep and hitting neurovascular structures
          - Poor patient positioning (arm must be relaxed and internally rotated)
          - Ultrasound strongly recommended for shoulder injections in primary care

      🎯Other joints: hip, ankle, wrist, elbow▾

          Hip joint injection
          **Access:** Difficult in primary care; ultrasound or fluoroscopy usually required
          **Indication:** Osteoarthritis, labral pathology, capsular inflammation
          **Approach:** Anterior (between sartorius and rectus femoris) or anterolateral
          **Landmark:** Inguinal ligament; needle advanced toward femoral head
          **Steroid dose:** 20-40 mg methylprednisolone or triamcinolone
          **Recommendation:** Refer to radiology/rheumatology for ultrasound or fluoroscopy-guided injection

          Ankle joint injection (talocrural)
          **Access:** Relatively straightforward; palpable landmarks
          **Indication:** Osteoarthritis, chronic ankle pain, post-fracture stiffness
          **Approach:** Anterior (between tibia and fibula, above talus)
          **Landmark:** Anterior ankle crease; needle directed posteriorly between tibia and fibula
          **Steroid dose:** 10-20 mg methylprednisolone or triamcinolone
          **Success rate:** ~80% with palpation

          Wrist injection (radiocarpal)
          **Access:** Small joint; palpation technique requires practice
          **Indication:** Osteoarthritis, rheumatoid arthritis, post-Colles' fracture arthritis
          **Approach:** Dorsal (between radius and ulna)
          **Landmark:** Dorsal wrist crease; needle between radius and carpals
          **Steroid dose:** 5-10 mg methylprednisolone or triamcinolone
          **Tip:** Ultrasound guidance recommended for small joints

          Elbow injection (ulnohumeral)
          **Access:** Lateral or posterior approaches; straightforward anatomy
          **Indication:** Osteoarthritis, rheumatoid arthritis, post-tennis elbow pain
          **Approach:** Lateral (between lateral epicondyle and radial head)
          **Landmark:** Lateral epicondyle and radial head; needle directed medially into joint space
          **Steroid dose:** 10-20 mg methylprednisolone or triamcinolone
          **Success rate:** ~85% with palpation

      💉Soft tissue and bursal injections▾
      
        Often simpler than intra-articular injections; lower risk; same steroid dose effectiveness.

          Subacromial bursa injection (shoulder)
          **Indication:** Subacromial bursitis, rotator cuff tendinopathy, impingement syndrome
          **Landmark:** Posterolateral edge of acromion; needle inserted 2-3 cm below in direct line with bursa
          **Steroid dose:** 20-40 mg methylprednisolone or triamcinolone + 2-3 mL 1% lignocaine
          **Technique:** Insert at 90° angle to skin, directed toward bursa (not into joint); inject when resistance felt

          Trochanteric bursa injection (hip)
          **Indication:** Greater trochanteric bursitis, lateral hip pain, Trendelenburg gait
          **Landmark:** Palpate greater trochanter on lateral hip; inject at point of maximal tenderness
          **Steroid dose:** 20-40 mg methylprednisolone + 3-5 mL 1% lignocaine
          **Technique:** Perpendicular to skin; may feel "click" as needle enters bursa

          Prepatellar bursa injection (knee)
          **Indication:** Prepatellar bursitis (housemaid's knee), chronic inflammation
          **Landmark:** Swollen, tender area anterior to patella
          **Steroid dose:** 10-20 mg methylprednisolone + 2-3 mL 1% lignocaine
          **Technique:** Superficial injection; be careful not to penetrate into joint space (stay anterior to patella)

          Olecranon bursa injection (elbow)
          **Indication:** Olecranon bursitis (student's elbow), chronic swelling
          **Landmark:** Swollen area over tip of elbow (olecranon process)
          **Steroid dose:** 10 mg methylprednisolone + 1-2 mL 1% lignocaine
          **Technique:** Direct injection into bursa; avoid underlying bone

          Achilles tendon sheath injection
          **Indication:** Achilles tenosynovitis, chronic tendon pain
          **Landmark:** Medial or lateral aspect of Achilles tendon at mid-calf level
          **Steroid dose:** 10 mg methylprednisolone or hydrocortisone + 2-3 mL 1% lignocaine
          **Caution:** Do NOT inject directly into tendon (risk of rupture); inject into surrounding sheath only

          de Quervain's tenosynovitis (thumb)
          **Indication:** Thumb pain along radial aspect of wrist (repetitive strain, new mothers)
          **Landmark:** Pain at radial styloid; palpate tendons (APL and EPB) at wrist base
          **Steroid dose:** 5-10 mg methylprednisolone + 1-2 mL 1% lignocaine
          **Technique:** Inject into tendon sheath, not tendon itself; often 1-2 injections needed

      ⚠️Complications and management▾
      
        #### Common complications
        
          **Post-injection flare (10-15%):** Temporary increase in pain 24-48 hours post-injection. Manage with ice, rest, analgesia. Reassure patient — usually resolves in 48 hours.
          **Transient local swelling:** Normal inflammatory response to procedure; peaks 24-48 hours; resolves over days
          **Local bruising or haematoma:** Usually minor; apply ice; avoid anticoagulants if possible pre-injection
          **Temporary increase in sweating or flushing:** Rare; usually resolves within hours

        #### Rare but serious complications
        
          Septic arthritis ( Fever, severe pain, inability to bear weight within 24-48 hours. URGENT: aspiration and culture required. Contact rheumatology or orthopedics.
          **Allergic reaction to steroid or local anaesthetic:** Rash, anaphylaxis. Have adrenaline available.
          **Nerve injury:** Rare; permanent sensation loss or motor weakness. More common with hip and shoulder injections.
          **Artery/vessel puncture:** Rare; presents with rapid swelling or extensive bruising. Monitor closely.
          **Steroid-induced osteonecrosis:** Extremely rare in primary care (more common with repeated injections at weight-bearing joints); counsel regarding limit of 2-3 injections per joint per year

        #### Red flags — when to refer urgently
        
          - Fever, severe joint pain, and swelling >48 hours post-injection (suspect septic arthritis)
          - Persistent neurological symptoms (numbness, weakness) post-injection
          - Severe allergic reaction
          - Inability to move joint despite steroid injection and rehabilitation

        #### Equipment needed
        
          - Sterile gloves, gauze, dressing pack
          - Chlorhexidine or iodine-based skin disinfectant
          - 25G needle (1.5" standard), 10 mL syringe
          - 1% lignocaine or 0.5% bupivacaine (5-10 mL)
          - Steroid preparation (methylprednisolone, triamcinolone)
          - Sterile container for aspirate if culture planned
