#### Major depressive disorder (DSM-5)
          ≥5 symptoms for ≥2 weeks, causing significant functional impairment:

            - Depressed mood (most days, most of the day).
            - Loss of interest or pleasure (anhedonia).
            - Significant appetite or weight change (>5% body weight).
            - Sleep disturbance (insomnia or hypersomnia).
            - Psychomotor agitation or retardation (observable, not just subjective).
            - Fatigue or loss of energy.
            - Worthlessness or excessive guilt.
            - Diminished ability to concentrate or make decisions.
            - Recurrent thoughts of death, suicidal ideation, or suicide attempt.
          
          #### Severity stratification
          
            **Mild:** 5–6 symptoms; minimal functional impairment.
            **Moderate:** 6–7 symptoms; moderate functional impairment.
            **Severe:** Most symptoms; significant functional impairment; may include psychotic features.
          
          #### Subtypes and specifiers
          
            **With psychotic features:** Delusions or hallucinations (usually mood-congruent).
            **With catatonia:** Motor abnormalities; responds to lorazepam.
            **With peripartum onset:** During pregnancy or within 4 weeks postpartum.
            **With seasonal pattern:** Recurrent depressions in winter (seasonal affective disorder, SAD).
            **With anxious distress:** Prominent anxiety symptoms.

        🧬Aetiology and pathophysiology▾
        
          #### Biological factors
          
            **Monoamine hypothesis:** Deficiency of serotonin, noradrenaline, and dopamine; basis for SSRI and other antidepressants.
            **HPA axis:** Hyperactivity of hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis; elevated cortisol in severe depression.
            **Neuroinflammation:** Elevated cytokines (IL-6, TNF-alpha); may contribute to treatment-resistant depression.
            **Genetic:** Heritability ~40%; no single gene; multiple susceptibility loci.
          
          #### Psychological factors
          
            - Negative thinking patterns and cognitive distortions (Beck's cognitive model).
            - Behavioural inactivity and loss of reinforcement.
            - Rumination and worry cycles.
          
          #### Environmental and social factors
          
            - Trauma, childhood abuse, or major life stressors.
            - Social isolation, relationship conflict, loss.
            - Chronic medical illness, pain, disability.
            - Substance use, especially alcohol and opioids.

        💊Pharmacological management▾
        
          #### SSRIs (selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors) - first-line
          
            **Examples:** Sertraline, citalopram, escitalopram, paroxetine, fluoxetine.
            **Efficacy:** ~60–70% response rate; take 4–6 weeks for effect (sometimes up to 12 weeks).
            **Side effects:** Sexual dysfunction (20–40%), GI upset, hyponatraemia (SIADH, especially elderly), initial anxiety, insomnia or sedation.
            **Advantages:** Safe in overdose, minimal drug interactions (except citalopram at high doses and 3A4 substrates).
          
          #### SNRIs (serotonin-noradrenaline reuptake inhibitors)
          
            **Examples:** Venlafaxine, desvenlafaxine, duloxetine.
            **Efficacy:** Similar to SSRIs; may be better for pain syndromes.
            **Side effects:** Similar to SSRIs, plus dose-dependent hypertension (especially venlafaxine).
            **Caution:** Abrupt discontinuation can cause withdrawal (dizziness, "brain zaps").
          
          #### Tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs)
          
            **Examples:** Amitriptyline, nortriptyline, clomipramine.
            **Efficacy:** Similar to SSRIs but older and less well-tolerated.
            **Side effects:** Anticholinergic (dry mouth, constipation, urinary retention), sedation, weight gain, cardiac conduction effects.
            **Use:** Second-line or for specific indications (chronic pain, insomnia).
          
          #### Atypical antidepressants
          
            **Mirtazapine:** Sedating; useful if insomnia; weight gain common.
            **Bupropion:** Activating; good for apathy and fatigue; lower sexual dysfunction; avoid in seizure disorder.
            **Trazodone:** Sedating; low-dose used for insomnia; priapism risk (rare).
          
          #### Augmentation strategies for partial response
          
            - Add another antidepressant (e.g., bupropion to SSRI).
            - Add atypical antipsychotic (aripiprazole, quetiapine) for augmentation.
            - Thyroid hormone (T3) augmentation in some refractory cases.
            - Lithium augmentation (requires monitoring).

        🗣️Psychosocial interventions▾
        
          #### Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT)
          
            - Targets negative thoughts, unhelpful behaviours, and thought-feeling-behaviour loops.
            - ~12–16 sessions; efficacy similar to or better than medication in mild-moderate depression; often combined with medication in severe depression.
          
          #### Interpersonal therapy (IPT)
          
            - Focuses on role transitions, grief, interpersonal disputes, interpersonal deficits.
            - Time-limited (12–16 weeks); effective, especially for depression linked to life events or relationships.
          
          #### Psychodynamic therapy
          
            - Longer-term; explores unconscious patterns and past relationships.
            - Evidence supportive but less robust than CBT or IPT for acute depression.
          
          #### Behavioural activation
          
            - Structured scheduling of pleasurable and valued activities to break inactivity and avoidance.
            - Simple, effective, especially early in treatment.
          
          #### Lifestyle interventions
          
            - Exercise: 20–30 min aerobic activity 3–5x/week; efficacy similar to antidepressants for mild-moderate depression.
            - Sleep hygiene.
            - Social engagement and support.

        ⚠️Suicide risk and safety planning▾
        
          #### Risk factors
          
            - Recent suicidal ideation or previous attempt.
            - Male gender (3:1 higher completion rate despite higher ideation in women).
            - Older age, especially males >65.
            - Access to means (firearms, medications).
            - Substance use, especially alcohol and opioids.
            - Recent loss or severe life stressor.
            - Social isolation.
          
          #### Assessment and safety planning
          
            - Ask directly about suicidal thoughts; does not increase risk.
            - Assess intent, plan, and access to means.
            - Create a safety plan: identify warning signs, internal coping strategies, people and social settings to distract, people and professionals to ask for help, ways to make environment safer.
            - Remove access to lethal means if possible.
            - Consider hospitalization if imminent risk.

        ❓Common questions▾
        
          #### How long does it take for antidepressants to work?
          Usually 2–4 weeks to notice an effect; 8–12 weeks for full benefit. If no improvement by 4–6 weeks at adequate dose, consider changing medication or adding to it.

          #### Will I be on antidepressants forever?
          Not necessarily. After 1–2 years of stability, a trial off medication can be considered with close monitoring. ~50% remain well; 50% relapse. Risk of relapse is higher with multiple prior episodes. Some people need long-term maintenance.

          #### Can antidepressants cause dependence?
          Not addiction, but physical dependence can occur with abrupt discontinuation (withdrawal syndrome). Taper slowly over 4–8 weeks to minimize this.

          #### Is therapy or medication better?
          For mild-moderate depression: either alone is effective. For moderate-severe: combination of medication and therapy is superior. For psychotic depression: medication (often with antipsychotic) is essential.

      📚Key references▾

          #### Key guidelines & evidence
          
            - DSM-5 Major Depressive Disorder diagnostic criteria.
            - NICE Depression Management Guidelines.
            - STAR*D trial — sequential SSRI therapy algorithm in treatment-resistant depression.
            - SSRIs, SNRIs, tricyclics, mirtazapine all effective; choice depends on side effect profile and comorbidity.
            - Psychotherapy (CBT, IPT) equivalent to antidepressants for mild-moderate depression.
            - Combination therapy superior to monotherapy in moderate-severe depression.
            - ECT and ketamine for severe, treatment-resistant depression.
