#### DSM-5 substance use disorder
        Diagnosed when a maladaptive pattern of substance use leads to clinically significant impairment or distress. Eleven criteria grouped into four domains: impaired control, social impairment, risky use, and pharmacological criteria (tolerance and withdrawal).

        Severity: **Mild** (2-3 criteria), **Moderate** (4-5 criteria), **Severe** (≥6 criteria).

        #### Key distinctions
        
          **Use vs Abuse vs Dependence:** The DSM-5 unified these into a single "substance use disorder" diagnosis with severity specifiers. "Dependence" is now reserved for physiological dependence (tolerance and withdrawal), which is only one of 11 diagnostic criteria.
          **Substance-induced intoxication vs withdrawal:** Separate diagnoses describing acute neurobiological effects of the drug, not the disorder itself.
          **Addiction:** Colloquial term; clinically refers to a severe SUD with compulsive drug-seeking despite harm.

        #### The addiction triad
        
          **Tolerance:** Progressively larger doses needed to achieve same effect.
          **Withdrawal:** Characteristic syndrome upon cessation or dose reduction.
          **Craving:** Intense desire for the drug, triggered by contextual cues or emotional states.

        #### Common substances and their clinical profiles
        **Opioids (heroin, oxycodone, codeine):** Euphoria, analgesia, respiratory depression. Withdrawal is severe but not life-threatening; overdose is potentially fatal.

        **Alcohol:** CNS depressant; withdrawal can be life-threatening (seizures, delirium tremens). Long-term use causes peripheral neuropathy, liver disease, cardiomyopathy.

        **Stimulants (cocaine, methamphetamine, MDMA):** Euphoria, hyperactivity, hyperalertness. Withdrawal is psychologically severe but not medically dangerous. Long-term use causes cardiac and neuropsychiatric damage.

        **Benzodiazepines:** Anxiolysis, sedation, muscle relaxation. Withdrawal can be severe and life-threatening at high doses; tapering is essential.

        **Cannabis:** Mild withdrawal syndrome; mostly psychological. Increasing potency (THC concentration) raises risk of dependence and psychotic symptoms.

      🔍Assessment in primary care▾
      
        #### Screening
        **ASSIST (Alcohol, Smoking and Substance Involvement Screening Test):** Practical, brief 8-item tool for all substances. Scores ≥27 indicate need for intervention; ≥4 indicates hazardous use.

        **AUDIT-C:** Three-item alcohol screening; score ≥4 in men, ≥3 in women indicates hazardous use.

        #### Key assessment questions
        
          - What substance(s), how much, how often, and for how long?
          - Route of administration (oral, IV, inhalation)?
          - Attempts to cut down or control use?
          - Continued use despite knowing problems it causes?
          - Tolerance or withdrawal symptoms?
          - Impact on relationships, work, health, legal status?
          - Comorbid mental health conditions (depression, anxiety, trauma)?

        #### Physical examination findings
        
          **Opioids:** Track marks, abscesses, constipation, miosis (pinpoint pupils).
          **Stimulants:** Agitation, tachycardia, nasal septum perforation (cocaine), severe dental disease ("meth mouth").
          **Alcohol:** Hepatomegaly, spider angiomata, peripheral neuropathy, Wernicke-Korsakoff signs.
          **Benzodiazepines:** Sedation, ataxia, slurred speech; long-term use may show no outward signs.

        #### Investigations
        **Urine drug screening:** Rapid but unreliable; many false positives and negatives. Useful for baseline but not diagnostic on its own.

        **LFTs, FBC, glucose:** Assess for complications (cirrhosis, nutritional deficiency, diabetes).

        **Cardiac assessment:** ECG in stimulant users (QT prolongation, arrhythmias); echocardiogram if cardiomyopathy suspected.

        **Hepatitis screening (B, C):** Essential in IV drug users; assess vaccination status for Hep B.

      💊Pharmacological treatment of opioid use disorder▾
      
        #### Overview
        Medication-assisted treatment (MAT) combines opioid agonist or partial agonist therapy with psychosocial support. Evidence strongly supports MAT for opioid use disorder; non-medication approaches alone have poor outcomes.

        #### Methadone
        **Mechanism:** Full mu-opioid agonist. Crosses blood-brain barrier slowly; long half-life (24-48 hours) allows once-daily dosing.

        **Dosing:** Induction 20-30 mg daily; increase by 5-10 mg every 3-7 days to maintenance 60-120 mg daily (some require up to 200+ mg). Must be dispensed daily at a clinic or pharmacy initially.

        **Advantages:** Most potent; completely prevents withdrawal; can be given once daily. Gold standard for heavily dependent users.

        **Disadvantages:** Requires daily clinic attendance initially; overdose risk if diverted; QT prolongation at high doses; slow induction (risk of early dropout).

        **Monitoring:** Baseline ECG (check QTc); repeat if dose >100 mg daily or if arrhythmias suspected. UDS every 1-3 months.

        **NZ context:** Methadone is controlled; prescribing requires licensing and clinic-based dispensing.

        #### Buprenorphine
        **Mechanism:** Partial mu-opioid agonist with high receptor affinity. Lower abuse potential than methadone; overdose risk lower (respiratory depression plateau at high doses).

        **Dosing:** Induction 2-4 mg sublingual daily; increase to maintenance 8-16 mg daily (range 4-32 mg). Can be dosed every other day or three times weekly.

        **Advantages:** Can be prescribed in primary care (in some jurisdictions); lower overdose risk; less sedating; slower onset of withdrawal; can use twice or thrice-weekly dosing.

        **Disadvantages:** Partial agonism means ceiling effect (insufficient for some heavily dependent users); precipitation of acute withdrawal if given to recently opioid-intoxicated patient; sublingual absorption variable.

        **Combination formulation (buprenorphine/naloxone):** Naloxone added to deter IV use (naloxone poorly absorbed sublingually but would precipitate withdrawal if dissolved and injected). Most formulations use this combination.

        **Monitoring:** No routine ECG needed. UDS every 1-3 months.

        **NZ context:** Buprenorphine is increasingly subsidised in primary care; can be prescribed by trained GPs.

        #### Naltrexone
        **Mechanism:** Full mu-opioid antagonist. Blocks euphoric effects of opioids; less relapse prevention than agonist therapy.

        **Dosing:** Oral 50 mg daily, or extended-release (Vivitrol) 380 mg IM monthly.

        **Advantages:** No abuse potential; fewer drug interactions; can be prescribed in primary care.

        **Disadvantages:** Higher relapse rates than methadone or buprenorphine; poor adherence; must be opioid-free for 7-10 days before starting (risk of acute withdrawal); hepatotoxicity risk at higher doses; less effective for heavy users.

        **Use case:** Best for highly motivated, employed, opioid-dependent patients (not heroin addiction typically). Useful bridge post-detoxification.

        **Monitoring:** Baseline LFTs; repeat every 6-12 months or if symptomatic.

      🍺Pharmacological treatment of alcohol use disorder▾
      
        #### Pharmacological options
        Medications are adjuncts to psychosocial intervention; none is curative alone.

      ⚡Pharmacological treatment of stimulant and benzodiazepine use disorders▾
      
        #### Stimulant use disorder (cocaine, methamphetamine, MDMA)
        No FDA-approved medications; treatment is primarily psychosocial. Pharmacological options target withdrawal symptoms or comorbidities.

        #### For acute withdrawal (depression, anhedonia, fatigue)
        
          **Antidepressants (SSRIs or bupropion):** Help with depression and craving; onset slow (weeks). Bupropion may help more than SSRIs for stimulant withdrawal.
          **Psychostimulants (methylphenidate, amphetamine):** Controversial; can reduce craving by providing controlled dopamine; high abuse risk; only in supervised, structured settings.
          **Modafinil:** Wakefulness promoter; may reduce cocaine craving; limited evidence.

        #### For comorbid conditions
        
          **ADHD + stimulant use:** Methylphenidate or amphetamine may reduce stimulant-seeking if appropriately dosed and monitored; risky and requires close supervision.
          **Depression:** Antidepressants (SSRIs, bupropion).
          **Anxiety:** SSRIs; avoid benzodiazepines (cross-addiction risk).

        #### Benzodiazepine use disorder
        **Critical:** Abrupt cessation is medically dangerous (seizures, status epilepticus, autonomic hyperactivity). Slow tapering is mandatory.

        #### Tapering approach
        
          **Step 1:** Convert all benzodiazepines to diazepam equivalent (longer half-life; easier tapering). See equivalent dose table.
          **Step 2:** Reduce by 5-10% every 1-2 weeks. Slower tapering (10-25% reduction every 4 weeks) may be less distressing.
          **Step 3:** Once at low dose (≤2 mg diazepam equivalent), reduce more slowly or hold to allow stabilisation.
          **Duration:** Can take 6-12 months or longer for high-dose users. Rushing increases seizure risk and relapse.

        #### Adjunctive medications during tapering
        
          **Anticonvulsants (pregabalin, gabapentin):** May ease withdrawal symptoms and reduce seizure risk during tapering.
          **SSRIs:** Help with anxiety and comorbid depression.
          **Melatonin or short-acting sedatives (Z-drugs):** Not recommended (cross-dependence risk); consider short-term non-benzodiazepine options (trazodone) if insomnia severe.

      ⚠Practical prescribing and harm reduction▾
      
        #### Shared decision-making
        People with substance use disorders often have low health literacy and past negative experiences with clinicians. Collaborative, non-judgmental approach is essential. Discuss benefits, risks, and alternatives with the patient.

        #### Baseline investigations before starting MAT
        
          - LFTs, FBC, U&Es
          - Hepatitis B and C serology; HIV test (with consent)
          - Baseline urine drug screen
          - ECG if methadone (especially if dose >100 mg daily)
          - Pregnancy test in women of childbearing age

        #### Monitoring during treatment
        
          **Adherence:** Regular clinic or pharmacy visits; pill counts if oral; observed therapy for higher-risk patients.
          **Urine drug screens:** Aim for monthly initially, then every 3 months if stable. Screen for polydrug use (benzodiazepines, stimulants, alcohol).
          **Clinical review:** Monthly for first 3 months, then 3-monthly if stable. Assess compliance, side effects, mental health, social functioning.
          **Dose adjustments:** Based on symptoms (inadequate relief of cravings or withdrawal suggests need for increase; oversedation suggests need for decrease).

        #### Pregnancy and substance use disorders
        **Methadone and buprenorphine are safe in pregnancy;** dose requirements often increase in third trimester. Untreated opioid use disorder carries high risk of relapse, overdose, and complications (preterm labour, intrauterine death). Newborn withdrawal (neonatal abstinence syndrome) is manageable with supportive care and morphine if needed.

        **Naltrexone:** Less data; generally avoided unless patient highly motivated and excellent adherence expected.

        #### Overdose prevention and naloxone
        **All patients on opioid medications and those at risk of opioid overdose should have naloxone (Narcan) on hand.**

        **Naloxone dosing:** 0.4-2 mg IM/IV/IN; can repeat every 2-3 minutes if no response. Intranasal formulation (4 mg) increasingly available; non-invasive and faster uptake.

        **Counsel patients:** Risk of overdose is highest immediately after detoxification (reduced tolerance) or when combining opioids with benzodiazepines, alcohol, or other CNS depressants.

        #### Interactions with opioid medications
        
          **Benzodiazepines + opioids:** Profound respiratory depression; avoid or use with extreme caution.
          **Methadone + strong CYP3A4 inhibitors (ketoconazole, ritonavir):** Increased methadone levels; monitor for overdose.
          **Buprenorphine + benzodiazepines:** Less severe than with full opioid agonists but still risky; avoid if possible.
          **Naltrexone + opioid painkillers:** Naltrexone blocks analgesic effect; patient unable to use opioids for pain. Consider alternative analgesics.

        #### Relapse management
        Relapse is common and does not indicate treatment failure. Increase frequency of appointments, consider urine screening, and assess for underlying triggers (depression, social stress, comorbid substance use). Dose adjustments may be needed; some patients benefit from supervised dosing.

        #### Discharge and long-term management
        MAT can be lifelong. Some patients stabilise and eventually reduce and cease therapy; others do better on maintenance. There is no predetermined timeline. Abrupt cessation is risky; tapered discontinuation should only occur in consultation with patient and addiction specialist.

      🔗NZ-specific context and resources▾
      
        #### New Zealand approach to substance use disorders
        NZ has moved toward primary care integration of opioid treatment. Buprenorphine is now subsidised in general practice under certain conditions; GPs with appropriate training can prescribe and manage patients. Methadone remains more restricted, typically managed by specialist addiction services or dedicated methadone clinics.

        **Māori and Pasifika:** Significantly overrepresented in substance use disorder populations; culturally informed care and engagement of whānau is essential.

        #### Funding and access
        
          **Buprenorphine:** Subsidised in primary care (requires Special Authority in some cases).
          **Methadone:** Usually funded through DHBs or addiction services.
          **Naltrexone:** Subsidised for alcohol use disorder in primary care.
          **Psychosocial support:** Access varies; Alcohol and Drug Services (typically DHB-based) provide specialist support.

        #### Key contacts and guidelines
        
          **Ministry of Health:** Substance Addiction Framework and NZ guidelines for addiction treatment.
          **NZPSA (NZ Pain Society/Addiction Society):** Professional guidelines and training.
          **Alcohol and Drug Helpline:** 0800 787 797 (confidential support and referral).
          **Local DHB Addiction Services:** Specialist assessment and management.

        #### Stigma and communication
        Language matters. Use **"substance use disorder"** not **"addiction,"** **"addict,"** or **"substance abuse."** Avoid moral judgments. Frame treatment as a medical condition requiring ongoing management, similar to diabetes or hypertension.
