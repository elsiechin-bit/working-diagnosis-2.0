## Overview


**Hypogonadism is subnormal serum testosterone with clinical symptoms.** Prevalence 6–12% of men; rises sharply >70 years. Diagnosis requires both biochemical (low T) AND clinical evidence (sexual, metabolic, mood symptoms); treatment indicated only when both present.

        #### Testosterone physiology
        
          **Production:** Testes (90%; regulated by LH/GnRH axis) + adrenals (10%); ~6–7 mg/day secretion in healthy adult male
          **Metabolism:** ~98% bound (SHBG, albumin); ~2% bioavailable free T; hepatic clearance; t½ ~70 minutes (why steady-state dosing needed)
          **Actions:** Sexual (libido, erectile function, orgasm), musculoskeletal (muscle mass, strength, BMD), metabolic (lipid profile, glucose homeostasis), CNS (mood, cognition, confidence), haematopoietic (RBC production)
          **Reference ranges (healthy adult male):** Total T 10–30 nmol/L; free T 225–900 pmol/L (highly lab-dependent; use local reference)
        
        #### Pathophysiology of hypogonadism
        
          **Primary (testicular):** Testes fail to produce T despite normal/elevated LH; causes: Klinefelter syndrome, cryptorchidism, orchitis, trauma, chemotherapy, hemochromatosis, alcohol
          **Secondary (central):** Pituitary/hypothalamic dysfunction → low LH/FSH; causes: pituitary tumour (prolactinoma), hypothyroidism, opioid use, obesity, aging, CNS disease
          **Combined:** Some conditions (chronic illness, obesity) affect both axes
        
        #### Clinical presentation
        
          **Sexual:** Low libido, ED, infertility, azoospermia (if exogenous T given)
          **Musculoskeletal:** Decreased muscle mass/strength, osteoporosis (low BMD), fatigue
          **Metabolic:** Weight gain, dyslipidaemia, insulin resistance, metabolic syndrome
          **Mood/cognitive:** Depression, anxiety, irritability, poor concentration, low confidence
          **Physical:** Gynaecomastia (elevated oestrogen relative to T), fine body hair, reduced facial/body hair, small testes

      🔍Diagnosis & investigation▾
      
        #### Clinical suspicion & screening
        
          **Symptom checklist:** Low libido, ED, reduced facial/body hair, gynaecomastia, reduced muscle mass, fatigue, depression, infertility. Multiple symptoms warrant biochemical testing.
          **Risk groups (higher prevalence):** Age >70, type 2 diabetes, obesity (BMI >30), chronic opioid use, metabolic syndrome, depression, prior chemotherapy/radiotherapy, pituitary disease
          **One-off complaint (e.g., isolated ED) does NOT automatically indicate TRT;** many men with normal T have ED (vascular, psychogenic, medication-induced)
        
        #### Biochemical testing
        
          **Timing:** Early morning (08:00–10:00) blood draw; T peaks 6–8 AM; afternoon samples not reliable
          **Total testosterone:** First-line screening; 
          **Borderline T (8–10 nmol/L):** Recheck on separate morning; measure free T (bioavailable or calculated)
          **Free testosterone:** 65 years
          **LH/FSH:** Differentiate primary vs secondary; LH high = primary; LH low = secondary
          **Prolactin:** If secondary (low LH), check prolactin (elevated suggests prolactinoma)
          **MRI pituitary:** If prolactin elevated or LH markedly suppressed; rule out adenoma before TRT
        
        #### Baseline investigations before starting TRT
        
          **All men:** PSA (baseline; if >3 μg/L, urology review before TRT; >4 μg/L contraindication until cleared), DRE (prostate exam), haematocrit (baseline for later comparison), lipids (cholesterol, LDL, HDL, triglycerides), BP, weight/BMI
          **If >40 years or symptomatic:** Consider ECG, fasting glucose/HbA1c
          **If concerned for testicular failure:** Testicular ultrasound (assess volume, exclude mass)

      💊Testosterone formulations & dosing▾
      
        #### Intramuscular injections
        
          **Testosterone enanthate (Sustanon, generics):** Ester of T; IM injection 250 mg Q2–3 weeks
          **Pharmacokinetics:** Peaks 24–48 hours; declines over 2–3 weeks → fluctuating levels; some men experience mood swings, libido/energy peaks & troughs between injections
          **Advantages:** Cheap, simple, once-every-few-weeks, proven efficacy, good for men unable to apply gels daily
          **Disadvantages:** Fluctuating T levels, injection site bruising/pain, monthly clinic appointments (in some settings), suppresses testicular function faster than gels, azoospermia in younger men
          **Dosing:** 250 mg Q2–3 weeks; adjust frequency based on response (some men prefer weekly at lower dose for stability)
        
        #### Transdermal gels (testogel & others)
        
          **Testogel (testosterone 1% gel; Besins, Bioniche, generics):** Apply daily to clean, dry skin (abdomen, shoulders, arms, front of thighs)
          **Pharmacokinetics:** Absorbed through skin; reaches steady-state by day 5–7; physiologic diurnal rhythm maintained (peaks morning, lower evening); t½ 30–40 min after absorption
          **Dosing:** Start 50 mg daily (one sachet or 5 g gel packet); increase to 100 mg daily if needed (based on T level & symptoms). Typical range 50–100 mg/day.
          **Advantages:** Physiologic levels, maintains diurnal rhythm, non-invasive, daily application allows flexibility, fewer mood/energy swings, lower risk of azoospermia than injections
          **Disadvantages:** Daily application (adherence), potential skin irritation/rash, transfer risk to partner/children (cover application site after drying), cost (often private in NZ)
          **Application technique:** Squeeze entire sachet into palm, apply evenly to clean skin, allow 5 min drying before dressing; do not wash off for ≥6 hours; rotate application sites
          **Transfer risk:** Warn patient to allow drying before partner contact; minimal risk if dried; women & children can absorb T if skin-to-skin contact before drying (feminisation risk in prepubertal boys)
        
        #### Transdermal patches
        
          **Testosterone patch (Andropatch, generics):** 2–6 mg/day; applied to skin once or twice daily
          **Advantages:** Physiologic levels, diurnal rhythm maintained, non-invasive, good adherence if patient remembers
          **Disadvantages:** Skin irritation/rash (common), adhesion problems, less convenient than gels, cost
          **Less commonly used in primary care than gels or injections.**
        
        #### Oral testosterone (undecanoate)
        
          **Andriol (testosterone undecanoate capsules, taken with fatty meal):** 120–280 mg/day in divided doses
          **Advantages:** Oral, non-invasive, familiar to patients, no transfer risk
          **Disadvantages:** Poor bioavailability (10–20%), requires multiple daily doses, must be taken with food, hepatic metabolism (though undecanoate bypasses first-pass via lymphatic absorption), efficacy lower than injectables or transdermal, expensive in NZ
          **Limited role in modern practice;** mostly reserved for men with needle phobia or inability to apply gels (e.g., very poor skin) or noncompliance.
        
        #### Long-acting injectables (emerging)
        
          **Testosterone undecanoate (Nebido):** IM injection 1000 mg at 0, 6 weeks, then Q10–14 weeks
          **Advantages:** Ultra-long duration; minimal frequent dosing; physiologic levels maintained
          **Disadvantages:** Very expensive, not widely available in NZ, requires specialist clinic administration

      🧬Testogel: Evidence & NZ context▾
      
        #### Efficacy of transdermal testosterone (testogel)
        
          **Sexual function:** Meta-analyses show 60–80% of hypogonadal men report improved libido, erectile function, sexual satisfaction after 3–6 months testogel. Efficacy depends on baseline T, psychogenic factors, partner dynamics.
          **Mood & energy:** Improves depression, fatigue, confidence; effect size moderate; takes 4–12 weeks for maximal benefit
          **Muscle & strength:** Modest increase in lean muscle mass (~2–3 kg over 6–12 months), improved strength; less dramatic than anabolic steroids at supraphysiologic doses
          **Bone mineral density:** Prevents further decline; may improve slightly over 1–2 years; protective against fracture in hypogonadal men
          **Metabolic:** Modest improvement in insulin sensitivity, waist circumference, lipid profile (↑ HDL, ↓ LDL); weight loss from concurrent lifestyle change amplifies effect
        
        #### Safety profile of testogel
        
          **Cardiovascular:** Observational data mixed; some studies suggest T replacement ↑ CVD risk in men with prior MI (controversial). TRT Study (RCT, 2018) in older men (>65, testosterone 
          **Prostate:** TRT does NOT increase prostate cancer risk in men with PSA 4 μg/L or prostate cancer history.
          **Haematocrit:** T stimulates RBC production; ~20% of men develop mild polycythaemia (Hct >50%); usually asymptomatic. Risk of thrombosis if Hct >55%; rare, but monitor 6-monthly.
          **Liver:** Testogel (transdermal) minimal hepatic metabolism; safe. Oral T undecanoate bypasses first-pass, also safe. Injectable enanthate minimal hepatic effect.
          **Gynaecomastia:** Aromatization of T to oestrogen may worsen gynaecomastia; rare contraindication if severe/painful
          **Fertility:** Exogenous T suppresses FSH/LH → azoospermia in younger men (especially injectables); reversible on cessation. Counsel men wanting children; consider alternatives (hCG + hMG, or defer TRT until family complete).
        
        #### NZ access & funding
        
          **Testosterone enanthate (injectables):** PHARMAC-funded; availability through GP or specialist clinic
          **Testogel (transdermal gel):** Not PHARMAC-funded; private cost ~NZ$60–150/month (depending on dose, pharmacy); branded (Besins) more expensive; generic versions cheaper
          **Patches, oral:** Not commonly funded; private access
          **GP role:** Diagnose hypogonadism, initiate injectable T if appropriate; testogel available for men preferring transdermal or if needle-phobic. Some GPs will fund testogel from practice budget if clinical case strong.
          **Specialist access:** Endocrinologist referral useful if diagnosis unclear, secondary hypogonadism with pituitary concerns, or poor response to standard therapy

      ⚠️Contraindications, monitoring & safety▾
      
        #### Absolute contraindications to TRT
        
          **Prostate cancer (active):** T may promote tumour growth; wait until remission (PSA undetectable, post-treatment stability)
          **Severe untreated sleep apnoea:** T worsens apnoea (↑ muscle tone pharyngeal); treat OSA first
          **Uncontrolled severe polycythaemia (Hct >55%):** Risk of thrombosis; defer until Hct 
          Acute MI ( Unstable; defer until stabilised
        
        #### Relative contraindications (caution, individualised decision)
        
          **PSA >4 μg/L or rising::** Urology assessment required before TRT; rule out prostate cancer first
          **Prior MI/stroke:** Benefit-risk must be discussed; many GPs/cardiologists now initiate cautiously in symptomatic men with prior event if PSA 
          **Severe untreated depression/suicidality:** Mood improvement with T usually positive, but screen & treat mental health first
        
        #### Monitoring during TRT
        
          **Week 0 (baseline):** PSA, DRE, haematocrit, BP, weight, lipids, mood screen (PHQ-9)
          **Week 3–6 (on testogel) or week 6 (on injections):** Repeat testosterone level (target 15–25 nmol/L, i.e., mid-high normal); adjust dose if needed
          **3-monthly, first year:** PSA (watch for rapid rise >0.75 μg/L/year suggests pathology), haematocrit (polycythaemia), BP, clinical response (libido, mood, energy, muscle), side effects (acne, mood changes)
          **6-monthly, years 2+:** PSA, haematocrit, lipids (TRT may lower HDL slightly), mood, symptoms
          **Annually:** Full reassessment; consider discontinuation if no clinical benefit, or if adverse effects emerge
        
        #### Side effects & management
        
          **Acne/oily skin:** ~10–15%; usually mild; topical retinoids, benzoyl peroxide; rarely warrants cessation
          **Testogel-specific skin irritation:** ~5–10%; erythema, pruritus at application site; rotate sites, reduce frequency (apply every other day instead of daily), or switch formulation
          **Gynaecomastia:** ~5–10%; mild, painless often tolerated; if severe/painful, consider aromatase inhibitor (anastrozole 1 mg BD) or switch to lower dose
          **Polycythaemia (Hct >50%):** ~15–20%; usually asymptomatic. If Hct >55% or symptomatic (headache, dyspnoea, visual disturbance), consider phlebotomy or dose reduction
          **Mood changes:** Rare; some men report mild irritability or agitation (dose-dependent); usually resolves with dose adjustment or cessation
          **Infertility/azoospermia:** Reversible on cessation (sperm recovery 3–12 months); counsel men wanting children before starting

      🎯Indications & clinical decision-making▾
      
        #### Clear indications for TRT
        
          **Symptomatic primary hypogonadism:** Low T + clinical symptoms (libido, ED, fatigue, mood); testicular failure documented (elevated LH, low T). Start TRT if no contraindications.
          **Symptomatic secondary hypogonadism (pituitary/hypothalamic):** Low T + low LH/FSH + symptoms; rule out pituitary tumour first (MRI if indicated). Consider TRT if no tumour.
          **Hypogonadism post-chemotherapy/radiotherapy:** Symptomatic men often benefit; consider TRT after washout period if T remains low
        
        #### Controversial/individualized indications
        
          **Aging-related decline ("andropause"):** T naturally declines ~1% per year >30 years; mild decline (T 8–10 nmol/L) with minimal symptoms may NOT require TRT. If symptoms clear & T low-normal, trial TRT 3–6 months, reassess.
          **ED with normal/borderline T:** PDE5i preferred first-line (see ED explainer). TRT only if low T documented; combined therapy (T + PDE5i) sometimes synergistic if hypogonadal.
          **Obesity & metabolic syndrome:** Weight loss is first-line; TRT may help but not substitute for lifestyle change. Consider if T low after weight loss sustained attempt.
          **Depression with low-normal T:** Antidepressant first-line; TRT may be adjunctive if T low-normal & depression improves with T trial
        
        #### Not indications for TRT (avoid)
        
          **Normal testosterone + any symptom:** ED, fatigue, mood symptoms with T >10 nmol/L warrant investigation of other causes (PDE5i for ED, antidepressant for depression, sleep assessment for fatigue)
          **Performance-enhancing (healthy athletes):** TRT for competitive advantage is doping; harm outweighs benefit; not ethical/legal
          **Isolated hair loss/baldness:** Not indication; T may worsen androgenetic alopecia

      🇳🇿NZ prescribing pathway & counselling▾
      
        #### GP prescribing checklist
        
          **Symptom screen:** Sexual (libido, ED), mood (depression, irritability), energy, muscle/strength
          **Biochemistry:** Confirm low T (morning sample 65
          **Baseline exam & investigations:** BP, weight, DRE, PSA (absolute baseline required), haematocrit, lipids
          **Cardiovascular risk:** Ask about prior MI, stroke, angina; consider ECG if >65 or significant CVD risk factors
          **Contraindications check:** Active prostate cancer, severe untreated OSA, recent MI, Hct >55%
          **Fertility counselling:** If man 
          **Initiate therapy:** Injectable testosterone enanthate 250 mg IM Q2–3 weeks (PHARMAC-funded) or testogel 50 mg daily (private, if preferred)
          **Safety planning:** Recheck T in 3–6 weeks, adjust dose if needed; PSA & Hct at 3 months, then 6-monthly
        
        #### Patient counselling (essential topics)
        
          **What TRT does:** "Replaces missing testosterone; typically improves energy, mood, libido, sexual function over 4–12 weeks. Not a cure for ED alone (may need sildenafil too). Not a quick fix; requires patience & ongoing monitoring."
          **Formulation choice:** Injections (cheap, simple, Q2–3 weeks) vs gel (daily, physiologic, costs extra). Discuss preference & adherence.
          **Side effects to watch:** Acne, mood changes, water retention, testogel rash; report within 2–4 weeks if bothersome.
          **Monitoring:** "Blood tests at 3 months, then 6-monthly for PSA & blood count (check for blood thickening). Annual full review. Can't predict who will benefit — trial 3–6 months, then decide if continuing."
          **Cardiovascular:** If prior MI/stroke, "TRT is cautious; discuss with your cardiologist." No sex for 4–6 weeks post-MI; TRT doesn't change that.
          **Fertility:** "If you want children in next 1–2 years, TRT will reduce sperm count; reversible after stopping. Discuss before starting."
          **Duration:** "Usually long-term (years); cessation leads to symptoms returning. Annual reassess whether continuing helps."
        
        #### When to refer to specialist (endocrinologist/urologist)
        
          - Secondary hypogonadism with suspected pituitary disease (low LH/FSH, elevated prolactin)
          - PSA >3 μg/L (urology to rule out cancer)
          - No response to TRT after 6 months (recheck T level; consider compliance, formulation change, or referral)
          - Severe side effects (acne, mood changes, polycythaemia not controlled)
          - Infertility with hypogonadism (consider hCG/hMG instead of TRT)

      💡Clinical pearls & pitfalls▾
      
        #### Pearls
        
          ✦**Hypogonadism is underdiagnosed in primary care.** Many men suffer low libido, ED, depression, fatigue without ever being tested. Low threshold for T screening in >50 years or with risk factors.
          ✦**Biochemistry + symptoms = diagnosis; not T level alone.** A man with T 7 nmol/L & no symptoms doesn't need TRT. A man with T 9 nmol/L & severe ED/fatigue does. Treat the patient, not the number.
          ✦**Testogel offers physiologic dosing.** Maintains diurnal rhythm; fewer mood swings than injectables. Higher cost (private) but preferred by many men; consider if budget allows.
          ✦**TRT Study showed CVD signal in older men.** Caution in >65 years with prior MI; benefit-risk discussion essential. Younger men with no CVD history appear safer.
          ✦**PSA monitoring is non-negotiable.** TRT does not cause prostate cancer, but rapid PSA rise (>0.75 μg/L/year) needs investigation. Baseline PSA before starting is mandatory.
          ✦**Polycythaemia is common but manageable.** ~20% of men develop Hct >50%. Usually asymptomatic; monitor 6-monthly. Phlebotomy or dose reduction if Hct >55%.
          ✦**TRT improves symptoms, not function recovery in severe organic ED.** Sexual function still requires intact neurovascular system. PDE5i or other mechanical therapy often needed alongside T replacement.
        
        #### Pitfalls
        
          **Treating ED with T without checking T level.** Most ED is not hypogonadal. PDE5i first-line; TRT only if low T documented.
          **Not repeating morning T if initial sample borderline.** Single borderline T not diagnostic; recheck on different morning.
          **Starting TRT in high-PSA men without urology clearance.** PSA >3 μg/L needs workup first (DRE, consider PSA density, biopsy if indicated). PSA >4 contraindication until cleared.
          **Ignoring cardiovascular history.** Men >65 or with prior MI/stroke need careful benefit-risk discussion; some GPs over-cautious, others under-cautious.
          **No monitoring after initiation.** Recheck T at 3–6 weeks to confirm adequate dosing; PSA 3-monthly first year. Lazy monitoring = missed polycythaemia, PSA rise, or inadequate dosing.
          **Expecting TRT to restore fertility.** Exogenous T suppresses sperm; informs men AFTER starting. If fertility critical, discuss hCG/hMG (preserve FSH) instead.
          **Not addressing lifestyle alongside TRT.** T + weight loss, exercise, smoking cessation synergize. TRT alone without behaviour change = modest benefit.
