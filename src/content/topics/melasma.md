## Overview


Melasma is a **chronic, acquired symmetrical hyperpigmentation disorder primarily affecting photoexposed areas of the face.** Classically known as "chloasma" or the "pregnancy mask," it presents as brown-to-gray-brown macules and patches, most commonly on cheeks, forehead, nose bridge, upper lip, and chin.

        #### Multifactorial pathogenesis
        **Ultraviolet & visible light:** UV-A and visible light (300–1200 nm) trigger melanocyte activity and reactive oxygen species. **Hormonal factors:** estrogen and progesterone upregulate melanin synthesis; pregnancy, oral contraceptives, and hormone replacement therapy are major triggers. **Genetic predisposition:** strong family history; more prevalent in darker phototypes (Fitzpatrick III–VI) and populations with high baseline melanin. **Vascular & dermal contribution:** dermal vessels and fibroblasts contribute to pigmentation; intralesional mast cells and stem cells play a role in persistence. **Inflammation:** chronic low-level inflammation perpetuates melanogenesis.

        #### Classification by depth
        
          **Epidermal (40%):** melanin in the epidermis; responds best to topical depigmenting agents; brown, sharply demarcated.
          **Dermal (10%):** melanin in dermal macrophages; deeper, slower to resolve; may require combination therapy or lasers.
          **Mixed (50%):** both epidermal and dermal involvement; most common; requires layered approach.

          2024–2025 Consensus: melasma is chronic & relapsing
          Complete clearance is unrealistic; goal is significant improvement (~50–75%) with strict maintenance. Cessation of therapy typically leads to recurrence within weeks to months, especially with sun exposure.

      🔍Assessment & workup▾
      
        #### History
        
          **Age of onset & triggers:** pregnancy, OCP initiation, HRT, sun exposure, recent move to sunnier climate
          **Medications:** OCPs, HRT, NSAIDs (topical idiopathic photodermatitis), photosensitising agents
          **Sun exposure & photoprotection habits:** outdoor work, beach exposure, inconsistent sunscreen use
          **Prior treatments & response:** what worked, what didn't, adherence
          **Quality of life impact:** melasma causes significant psychosocial distress; assess mood, confidence, social withdrawal (MelaQoL or DLQI questionnaire)
          **Ethnicity & family history:** strong predictor of severity and recurrence risk
        
        #### Examination
        
          - Bilateral, symmetric distribution on face (cheeks, forehead, nose, upper lip, chin most common)
          - Wood's lamp assessment (if available) can help estimate epidermal vs dermal load: epidermal appears darker under UV; dermal less pronounced
          - Assess for confounding dermatitis, post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation, or other hyperpigmentation disorders
        
        #### Investigations
        
          **Baseline photography** with standardised lighting; objective tracking with MASI (Melasma Area and Severity Index) or photographic comparison
          **Thyroid & metabolic screening:** hypothyroidism can worsen; check TSH, iron studies if fatigue
          **Hormonal assessment:** if irregular periods or signs of PCOS; rule out hormonal causes
          - No routine genetic testing needed; diagnosis is clinical

      ☀️Photoprotection: the irreplaceable foundation▾

          Most melasma treatment failure is inadequate photoprotection
          Without strict sun protection, all other interventions will fail. Photoprotection is not optional; it is the prerequisite for any topical or procedural therapy.

        #### Practical photoprotection protocol
        
          **Avoid direct sun 10 a.m.–3 p.m.** when UV index is highest
          **SPF 50+ broad-spectrum sunscreen, reapply every 3 hours.** Water-resistant formulations preferred. Apply generous amount (5–7 ml per face)
          **Iron oxide-based & pigmented sunscreens preferred:** visible light (300–700 nm) can trigger melanin synthesis; tinted mineral sunscreens provide additional visible light protection
          **Daily protective clothing:** wide-brimmed hats, UV-protective clothing (UPF 50+)
          **Year-round protection:** Aotearoa's southern hemisphere UV index remains high even in winter
          **Avoid unnecessary heat & inflammation:** physical irritation, harsh skincare, and friction can worsen melasma via post-inflammatory pigmentation
        
        **Counsel realistically:** even imperfect adherence to photoprotection significantly improves outcomes and reduces recurrence. Perfect compliance is unrealistic; the goal is sustained 80%+ adherence.

      💊Topical depigmenting agents▾
      
        #### Gold standard: triple combination (hydroquinone + tretinoin + corticosteroid)
        The triple combination of hydroquinone, tretinoin, and a fluorinated corticosteroid has demonstrated superior efficacy in treating melasma compared to individual agents alone. **Kligman formula:** 4% hydroquinone + 0.05% tretinoin + 0.01% fluocinolone acetonide. **NZ availability:** Branded as "Kligman solution"; available as compounded or proprietary formulations. Often prescribed off-label and unfunded; patients typically pay privately (~$30–60/tube).

          **Efficacy:** ~50–89% improvement in MASI scores over 12 weeks with regular use.
          **Onset:** 4–12 weeks for noticeable improvement; maximum effect at 12–24 weeks.
          **Application:** thin layer once daily at night; avoid contact with eyes, lips. Apply only to affected areas to minimise irritation.
          **Irritation common:** erythema, scaling, mild burning, especially in weeks 1–4. Advise moisturiser & patient counselling on temporary worsening.
          **Maintenance:** after improvement, reduce frequency (e.g., twice weekly or switch to 2% hydroquinone monotherapy) to maintain and reduce irritation.
          **Contraindications:** avoid in pregnancy & breastfeeding (tretinoin teratogenic; hydroquinone has absorbed systemically and cautionary data); avoid in patients with sulite sensitivity (metabisulfite preservative).
          **Long-term safety:** ochronosis (blue-black pigmentation from chronic hydroquinone use) is rare (~0.3% with prolonged use); mitigated by maintenance rather than continuous application.
        
        #### Single agents & alternatives
        
          AgentMechanism & efficacyNZ availability

            **Tretinoin 0.05–0.1%**Upregulates cell turnover, improves epidermal penetration of depigmentants. Irritating; usually in combination.Prescription; private or health insurance.
            **Azelaic acid 15–20%**Tyrosinase inhibitor; alternative if hydroquinone contraindicated or causes irritation. Comparable efficacy to hydroquinone in some trials.Available; generally well-tolerated.

        #### Combination topical therapy approach
        2025 consensus: **combination therapy consistently outperforms monotherapy.** Strategies include:

          **Triple combo (1st line):** hydroquinone + tretinoin + corticosteroid
          **Dual therapy (if irritation or availability):** hydroquinone + azelaic acid, or tretinoin + kojic acid
          **Add supporting agents:** vitamin C, hyaluronic acid, niacinamide for barrier support and tolerability
          **Maintenance after improvement:** reduce to 2% hydroquinone 2–3×/week or alternate with azelaic acid to sustain and reduce adverse effects

      💊Oral & intralesional agents▾
      
        #### Oral tranexamic acid (OTA)
        Oral tranexamic acid has strong evidence for efficacy in melasma treatment. Blocks conversion of plasminogen to plasmin, inhibiting downstream melanin-stimulating pathways and reducing inflammation. **Dosing:** 250–500 mg twice daily for 8–12 weeks; typically prescribed off-label. **NZ access:** tranexamic acid is not PHARMAC-funded for melasma; private pay. Often prescribed off-label by dermatologists and cosmetic clinicians.

          **Efficacy:** 50–60% MASI reduction in refractory cases; works synergistically with topical agents and lasers.
          **Onset:** 4–8 weeks; maximum benefit at 12 weeks.
          **Safety:** generally well-tolerated. **Screen for thromboembolic risk:** avoid or use with caution in history of DVT, PE, thrombophilia, or recent surgery. Monitor BP (can cause hypertension).
          **Side effects:** dyspepsia, headache, dizziness, rare GI upset.
          **Combinations:** OTA + topical triple combo synergistic; OTA + laser/microneedling enhances results.
        
        #### Intralesional tranexamic acid (ILTA)
        Intradermal injections (10–20 mg, every 2–3 weeks) targeting hyperpigmented lesions. **NZ status:** occasionally offered by cosmetic dermatology clinics; not standard practice. **Evidence:** comparable to OTA but more invasive; not superior in head-to-head trials. Generally reserved for refractory focal patches.

      ✨Laser & light-based therapies▾

          Lasers are third-line, not first-line; risk of worsening or post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation
          Laser therapy should only be attempted after optimising photoprotection and topical/oral agents. Patient must be pretreated with depigmenting agents (hydroquinone) and maintain strict SPF 50+. Heat can trigger melasma relapse; energy-based lasers (not heat) are safer.

        #### Most effective approaches (2024–2025 consensus)
        
          **Low-fluence Q-switched Nd:YAG laser (1064 nm):** Energy-based (not heat); most effective and safest. Works via selective photothermolysis of melanin. 2–8 sessions, 3–4 week intervals. Pretreatment with hydroquinone mandatory.
          **Q-switched Nd:YAG + intradermal tranexamic acid:** Superior to laser or TA alone; reduces MASI more effectively and improves long-term stability.
          **Picosecond lasers (newer):** Emerging data shows promise; fewer adverse effects than nanosecond lasers; limited RCT evidence but high patient satisfaction.
          **Microneedling + tranexamic acid (topical or systemic):** Non-ablative option; mechanically disrupts pigmentation and improves penetration of depigmentants. 2–4 sessions; higher patient satisfaction in dermal melasma.
          **Avoid:** heat-based IPL, ablative fractional lasers, and high-energy Q-switched—high risk of post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation and rebound worsening.
        
        #### Treatment considerations
        
          **Pretreatment:** 4–6 weeks of topical hydroquinone + strict SPF 50+ before laser.
          **Timing:** lower fluence, longer intervals (4 weeks) reduce adverse effects.
          **Post-laser care:** SPF 50+ (iron oxide-based), avoid heat, avoid topical irritants for 2 weeks.
          **Recurrence:** ~30–50% recur within 1–2 years without maintenance; ongoing hydroquinone + photoprotection essential.
          **Darker skin (Fitzpatrick V–VI):** Higher risk of adverse effects; use low-fluence, longer intervals, and consider non-ablative options (microneedling, chemical peels with depigmentants) first.

      🛣️Treatment algorithm & realistic expectations▾
      
        #### Step-wise approach
        **Step 1: Photoprotection (mandatory foundation)**
SPF 50+ daily, iron oxide sunscreen, avoid 10 a.m.–3 p.m., protective clothing. Assess & counsel on adherence barriers.

        **Step 2: First-line topical (weeks 1–12)**
Triple combination (hydroquinone + tretinoin + corticosteroid) OR hydroquinone 4% monotherapy if triple unavailable. Counsel on 4–6 week lag before noticeable improvement; manage irritation with moisturisers.

        **Step 3: Add systemic agent if inadequate response (week 8–12)**
Oral tranexamic acid 250–500 mg BD if 30–50% improvement only. Screen for thromboembolic risk. Synergises with topicals.

        **Step 4: Consider laser/microneedling if refractory (≥12 weeks of topical + OTA)**
After baseline depigmentation; low-fluence Q-switched Nd:YAG ± intradermal TA. Multiple sessions; high recurrence risk.

        **Step 5: Long-term maintenance (indefinite)**
After improvement, transition to maintenance regime: 2% hydroquinone 2–3×/week OR alternate hydroquinone with azelaic acid. Continue photoprotection. Periodic reassessment (8–12 weekly); reintensify at first sign of darkening.

        #### Realistic timeline & expectations
        
          **Months 1–2:** Minor improvement; patient adherence is key.
          **Months 2–4:** 30–50% clearance with optimised triple therapy + photoprotection.
          **Months 4–6:** 50–75% improvement; transition to maintenance.
          **Post-treatment:** ~50% recur within 1–3 years without maintenance; some lifelong maintenance needed.
          **Complete clearance:** Unrealistic in most; goal is significant (≥50%) improvement and halting progression.
        
        #### When to refer to dermatology
        
          - Unclear diagnosis (rule out other hyperpigmentation disorders, photosensitivity, drug-induced).
          - Poor response to 12 weeks of triple combo + photoprotection.
          - Refractory or rapidly progressing melasma.
          - Need for laser therapy or advanced procedural options.
          - Significant quality-of-life impact; psychosocial support or specialist cosmetic management.

      💡Clinical pearls & pitfalls▾
      
        #### Pearls
        
          ✦**Photoprotection is not optional.** Every other treatment fails without strict SPF 50+ and sun avoidance. This is the prerequisite, not an accessory.
          ✦**Triple combination outperforms monotherapy.** Hydroquinone + tretinoin + corticosteroid gold standard; ~89% improvement vs 30–50% with single agents.
          ✦**Melasma is chronic & relapsing.** Goal is 50–75% improvement with long-term maintenance, not cure. Set realistic patient expectations upfront.
          ✦**Irritation early; improvement late.** Erythema & scaling weeks 1–4 are normal with tretinoin. Reassure; do not abandon. Maximum benefit at 12+ weeks.
          ✦**Oral tranexamic acid works.** Strong evidence; add to refractory cases. Screen for DVT/PE risk.
          ✦**Lasers are third-line.** Only after optimised photoprotection & topical therapy. Low-fluence Q-switched Nd:YAG safest; combined with TA superior.
          ✦**Assess & support maintenance adherence.** Improvement fades quickly without ongoing hydroquinone + SPF; barrier to adherence (cost, irritation, time) often determinant of success.
          ✦**Dermal melasma harder to treat.** Topicals less effective; consider early addition of OTA or laser/microneedling.
          ✦**Pregnancy & OCP are modifiable triggers.** Not suggesting OCP discontinuation (risk-benefit trade-off), but discussing with patient may help counsel on realistic expectations during reproductive years.
        
        #### Pitfalls
        
          **Inadequate photoprotection.** Most common cause of treatment failure. Always reassess SPF adherence before escalating therapy.
          **Declaring monotherapy failure after 4 weeks.** Tretinoin-containing regimens require ≥12 weeks. Early abandonment is premature.
          **Using heat-based lasers (IPL, ablative fractionated).** High risk of post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation and rebound worsening; avoid except in expert hands.
          **Forgetting to pretreat with hydroquinone before laser.** Depigmentation reduces risk of heat-triggered melanin rebound.
          **Not screening for thromboembolic risk before OTA.** Tranexamic acid prothrombotic; contraindicated in VTE history.
          **Ignoring maintenance.** Improvement fades within weeks to months without ongoing depigmenting agents + photoprotection.
          **Overlooking HRT or OCP as contributor.** While not recommending cessation, discussion may set realistic expectations or explore alternatives if appropriate.
          **Expecting complete clearance.** Unrealistic; leads to patient dissatisfaction despite significant improvement. Frame goal as "significant fading with long-term maintenance."
