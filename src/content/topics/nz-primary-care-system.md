#### The GP-centric model
        New Zealand primary care is **GP-centric and gate-keeper based**. GPs are the first contact and primary coordinator of care for most patients. Unlike some countries with walk-in urgent care, NZ relies on scheduled GP appointments with a high barrier to direct access to specialists.

        #### Key structures
        
          **Primary Health Organisations (PHOs):** Not-for-profit or for-profit entities that contract with Health NZ to deliver primary care. ~80 PHOs across NZ.
          **General practices:** Independent or group-owned clinics operating within a PHO; usually employ 1-10+ GPs plus nurses and administrative staff
          **Health NZ:** Public secondary and tertiary care sector; now unified under Health NZ (2023 restructure)
          **Community health services:** Nurse-led and allied health clinics (physiotherapy, audiology, mental health); funded through PHOs or Health NZ

        #### Practice types
        
          **Public-funded practices:** Bulk-funded; GP visit heavily subsidised or free for enrolled patients (especially under 30, over 65, high needs)
          **Private practices:** User-pays; no subsidies; typically $50-100+ per visit
          **Mixed model (most common):** Public funding plus gap payments; patient pays co-payment in addition to subsidy

        #### Prescription and dispensing
        
          **Community pharmacies:** Most common; patients collect prescriptions from local pharmacies
          **In-practice dispensing:** Some rural/remote practices dispense medications directly; reduces access barriers
          **PHARMAC subsidy:** Most common medications funded; patient co-payment $5 per item (capped at 20 items/year, then free)

      📜How NZ general practice happened - a short history▾
      
        ### The opening problem

        New Zealand has a health system that performs remarkably well on some metrics and remarkably poorly on others. The Commonwealth Fund's 2024 international comparison ranked NZ first in the world on care process (continuity, coordination, prevention, patient engagement) and worst in the OECD on equitable access for low-income patients - a contradiction so stark it demands explanation.

        That explanation sits in history. General practice in New Zealand arrived at its current form not by design but through a succession of compromises, failed experiments, ideological reversals, and incremental adjustments, each of which solved some problems while entrenching others. Understanding the present requires understanding how we got here.

        ### The colonial period: 1840-1900

        #### The bag carrier era

        General practice in colonial New Zealand began with whoever arrived with a leather bag and some claim to medical training. The first formally registered medical practitioners came with the New Zealand Company and missionary groups in the 1840s, bringing whatever they had been trained in and improvisation as a primary tool.

        What they had been trained in was, by modern standards, not very much. Anaesthesia arrived in NZ in the late 1840s (ether in Auckland by 1847), but germ theory was still decades off, antibiotics a century away, and the pharmacopoeia consisted largely of opium, mercury, and hope. The "general practitioner" was less a clinician in the modern sense and more a general-purpose bearer of bad news while bodies recovered or didn't.

        The geographic problem was immediate and formidable. NZ is not large by area but is extremely awkward by terrain - a district surgeon covering the Maniototo or West Coast faced country with no roads, no telephone, and a horse that didn't want to go there. Rural "general practice" was whoever had done some medical training and happened to live nearby, supplemented by rongoā Māori practitioners whose botanical and diagnostic knowledge often exceeded that of the European-trained arrivals.

        #### Credentialism arrives

        The Medical Practitioners Act 1869 was the first attempt at credential verification - the claim that the right to practice medicine required some form of verification that you knew something. The 1914 Medical Act consolidated this into a recognisable registration framework. Neither piece of legislation said much about what general practice was or how it should be organised - those questions were considered obvious enough not to require legislation, which in retrospect was optimistic.

        By 1900, general practice in NZ was locally rooted, geographically dispersed, and operating without any formal college, specialist training pathway, or institutional coordination. It was also the only medical care most people could access. The problem of making it sustainable and equitable would not be seriously tackled for another thirty-eight years.

        ### The 1938 promise and its betrayal

        #### The Social Security Act moment

        New Zealand's claim to have created the first comprehensive welfare state rests substantially on the Social Security Act 1938. Passed by the first Labour government under Michael Savage, emerging from depression, the Act promised universal provision for the sick, the old, the unemployed, and - pertinently here - universal free access to a general medical service.

        The key word is "promised."

        The New Zealand Medical Association, representing the profession's interests, was not enthusiastic about universal free healthcare. The concern wasn't the principle - it was the implementation. "Free at the point of care" implied that doctors would become, in effect, public servants with government-determined income. This was not a career arrangement many had anticipated.

        #### The compromise that shaped everything

        The negotiations between government and profession were prolonged. The result was a compromise that has shaped NZ primary care for eighty-five years: GPs would receive a government payment per service (a "benefit"), but they retained the right to charge patients a co-payment above that benefit. The free general medical service Savage had envisioned did not materialise. What materialised instead was a subsidy-plus-copayment model that looked like free healthcare if you were already healthy enough not to need much of it, and looked increasingly like a barrier the sicker and poorer you were.

        This arrangement - capitation from the state, co-payment from the patient, GP as independent contractor rather than public employee - became the basic organisational fact of NZ general practice. It remains so today. The equity problem the Commonwealth Fund identified in 2024 is, in part, the 1938 compromise still on the books.

        #### What the compromise cost

        The trade-off was explicit: government subsidised care, but only partially. Patients paid the gap. The arithmetic meant that access was rationed not by clinical need but by ability to pay. Someone on a low income, facing a $35-50 copayment per visit in 2024, defers seeing a GP about a persistent cough or chest pain. Someone on an average income goes easily. The system worked brilliantly for the second group and catastrophically for the first.

        For eighty-five years, successive governments and health administrations did not fundamentally alter this structure. They adjusted around it, tinkered with it, and created parallel systems to handle the equity failures it produced. But they did not dismantle it. By 2024, the system had generated a measurable population-level harm: worse health outcomes, higher rates of presentation with advanced disease, lower preventive care uptake, all correlated strongly with deprivation.

        ### The college arrives late: 1973

        #### Twenty-one years behind Britain

        The Royal New Zealand College of General Practitioners was founded in 1973. The Royal College of General Practitioners in the UK was founded in 1952. NZ GPs practiced without a specialist college for twenty-one years after their British counterparts organised one.

        This gap is instructive. For those twenty-one years, general practice in NZ was professionally treated as the thing you did if you hadn't become a proper specialist. You finished your house jobs, possibly did a hospital year, then acquired a building, a receptionist, and the accumulated paperwork of whoever's practice you were buying. There was no formal training pathway, no specialist credential, no institutional voice for the argument that general practice was itself a specialty.

        #### What changed

        The RNZCGP gradually changed this. Fellowship (FRNZCGP) became a credential. The GPEP training programme provided a structured postgraduate pathway. By the 1990s, vocational registration as a GP required fellowship. The college produced the intellectual argument - systematic, disciplined, evidence-based - that general practice was a distinct specialty with its own consultation model, its own evidence base, and its own disciplinary knowledge.

        This argument was more accepted in some quarters than others. Hospitals and specialists remained sceptical. But the college gave general practice an institutional voice. That voice has been essential in every policy negotiation since - not always successful, but always there.

        ### The market experiment: 1993-2000

        #### Running hospitals like Telecom

        The 1993 health reforms under the National government occupy a special place in NZ policy history: the place occupied by instructive failures. The theory was elegant - elegant in the way economic theories applied to healthcare tend to be before they encounter healthcare. Area Health Boards were replaced by a purchaser-provider split: Regional Health Authorities (RHAs) would purchase; Crown Health Enterprises (CHEs) would provide. CHEs were hospitals reconstituted as state-owned enterprises, expected to operate on commercial principles, compete with one another, and produce a surplus.

        Hospital as competitive enterprise. This contained, as some observers noted, a theoretical difficulty: hospitals providing emergency neurosurgery and obstetric services for geographically captive populations do not compete in any meaningful sense. The experiment produced hospitals trying to improve their financial position by reducing services to populations who couldn't go elsewhere to get them. The "efficiency gains" were visible on balance sheets; the equity losses were visible in emergency departments.

        By 1996 the four RHAs had collapsed into one Health Funding Authority. By 2000, the incoming Labour government abolished the CHEs, replaced them with twenty DHBs under the 2000 Public Health and Disability Act, and restored a broadly public model. The DHB structure - twenty-one boards covering defined geographic populations with both funding and provider responsibilities - would survive until 2022.

        #### The catalyst for change

        For general practice, the 1990s reforms were destabilising but ultimately catalytic. The Primary Health Care Strategy of 2001 - which replaced the benefit-and-copayment model with capitation funding through Primary Health Organisations (PHOs) - was developed in direct response to the equity failures the reforms had exposed. The strategy was written by a government that had seen the market experiment fail and wanted to do better.

        ### PHOs and the long argument: 2001-2022

        #### The capitation model

        The PHO model was intended to align GP incentives with population health outcomes rather than service volume. Practices enrolled patient populations; PHOs received capitation funding weighted for age and gender; this was distributed to practices. Under-6s became free (extended to under-13s by 2022). VLCA (Very Low Cost Access) practices serving high-deprivation populations received higher funding. Community Services Card holders got subsidised visits.

        The co-payment problem did not disappear. It couldn't, while the model retained a co-payment. Working-age adults on lower incomes remained subject to co-payments that Commonwealth Fund data identifies as material access barriers.

        #### The capitation weighting problem

        A deeper problem: the capitation formula didn't adequately weight for patient complexity. A practice serving a population with high rates of multimorbidity, deprivation, and chronic disease received the same per-capita funding as a practice serving a healthy, wealthy population. The difference in required time and clinical work was substantial. The funding mechanism didn't account for it.

        This problem was identified almost immediately. It took twenty-three years to fix. In 2025, the capitation formula was reweighted to include deprivation, multimorbidity, and rurality for the first time since 2002. By the standards of health policy, this was rapid. By the standards of people who couldn't afford to see a GP in the intervening years, it represented a different kind of achievement.

        #### What worked and what didn't

        The PHO model stabilised general practice funding and created accountability structures. PHOs could require quality improvements, data collection, chronic disease management protocols. This was valuable. But the co-payment and underfunding meant that for two decades, access remained stratified by ability to pay. The system worked well for practices serving higher-income populations and was under constant pressure in practices serving deprived populations.

        ### The collapse and reversal: 2022-2024

        #### The 2022 consolidation

        The Pae Ora (Healthy Futures) Act 2022 collapsed the twenty DHBs into a single national entity, Health New Zealand | Te Whatu Ora. It also established Te Aka Whai Ora, a parallel Māori Health Authority with a specific commission to address persistent and well-documented health inequities for Māori populations.

        The rationale for the Māori Health Authority was clear and evidence-based: Māori health outcomes are substantially worse than for other populations across every major indicator; the existing system had not closed the gap despite decades of effort; a dedicated parallel authority with Māori leadership and autonomy might be more effective than attempts at integration into a system not designed for equitable care for Māori.

        #### The reversal

        Te Aka Whai Ora was disestablished by the coalition government in 2024 - a remarkable policy reversal by international standards. A major parallel commissioning structure was created, staffed, operated, and abolished within two years. The rationale given was administrative efficiency and the undesirability of a race-based health agency. Critics observed that the equity data motivating its creation had not changed - Māori health outcomes remained substantially worse - and that dismantling the authority was unlikely to improve them.

        The disestablishment of Te Aka Whai Ora represents a decision that efficiency mattered more than equity. That choice will be evident in health data for decades.

        ### The present: 2025-2026

        #### Health New Zealand under pressure

        Health New Zealand in 2025 is operating under a commissioner appointed in response to a financial position described as unsustainable. The capitation reweighting and full funding of GPEP training are substantive interventions. But the fundamental structural problems remain:

          **Co-payments:** Still exist, still create access barriers for lower-income patients
          **Workforce:** GP numbers are declining; recruitment is difficult; workload is high
          **Funding per capita:** Still lower in real terms than it was in 2010
          **Infrastructure:** General practice buildings and IT systems are aging; investment is reactive not planned
          **Equity:** Commonwealth Fund data shows NZ has among the worst equity metrics in the OECD despite having among the best general care process

        #### The structural problem that remains

        The fundamental issue is this: NZ general practice is expected to do the work of a universal healthcare system while being partially privately funded through copayments. This creates an unsolvable contradiction. You cannot deliver equitable care if access is rationed by ability to pay. The equity problem is not a bug - it's a feature of the model that has existed since 1938.

        Every government since 2001 has recognised this in principle and done little about it in practice. The cost of making primary care genuinely free (or very low cost) at the point of care across the population would be substantial - probably $500-800 million per year in forgone copayments and required funding increases. That cost is politically difficult. So the system limps forward, working brilliantly for some and failing others.

        ### What this explains

        Why is NZ ranked first on care process and worst on equity? Because the system was designed, starting with the 1938 compromise, to provide good care to people who had already decided they could see a doctor. It excels at coordination, continuity, and prevention for that population. But it fails at the harder problem: reaching the people who don't see doctors because they can't afford to.

        The history explains the contradiction. It also raises a question that policy has not seriously engaged with: is this contradiction fixable within the current model, or does the model itself need to change? Every reorganisation since 1993 has tried to fix it without changing the fundamental structure. None have succeeded.

        **Similarities:** Universal public systems; strong primary care; high life expectancy

          **Specialist access:** Faster than NZ/UK/Canada due to higher funding
          **Medications:** Subsidised; patient co-payments moderate
          **Primary care investment:** Higher per capita spending; more integrated services
          **Health outcomes:** Consistently ranked higher on global metrics

        #### United States (hybrid)
        **Differences:** Mixed private and public; no universal coverage; market-driven costs

          **GP visit (uninsured):** $100-300+; major barrier
          **Specialist access:** Faster if insured; high out-of-pocket costs
          **Medications:** Highly variable; major cost driver for chronic disease
          **Admin burden:** High; insurance navigation, pre-authorisation, coding complexity

      📊NZ system: Strengths, weaknesses and accessibility▾
      
        #### Strengths
        
          **Universal access:** Most New Zealanders can see a GP; no insurance requirement; equity focus on high-needs populations
          **Holistic care:** Capitation supports continuity and comprehensive management
          **Medication affordability:** PHARMAC subsidy makes chronic medications accessible; reduces non-adherence
          **Community health services:** Nurses, physios, mental health available through PHOs
          **Preventive focus:** Regular health checks, screening, immunisation through primary care
          **Low admin burden:** Less pre-authorisation hassle than USA/Australia

        #### Weaknesses and barriers
        
          **Equity gaps:** Pacific, Māori, and rural populations face longer waits and higher costs
          **Specialist access:** Long waiting lists (months to 12+); gap-funded private care expensive; two-tier emerging
          **GP workforce stress:** GPs underpaid; burnout common; practice closures in some regions
          **After-hours access:** Unevenly distributed; rural areas underserved
          **Mental health integration:** Secondary services often separate; primary care capacity limited for complex cases
          **Medication gaps:** Some new or specialist medications not PHARMAC-funded; patients pay privately ($100s-1000s)
          **Preventive medicine:** Time constraints limit proactive health promotion despite system design

        #### Where NZ stands
        New Zealand has achieved **universal access to primary care** with **moderate affordability**. The system prioritises equity through age and deprivation-based subsidies, positioning it between the UK (completely free but resource-stretched) and Australia (more expensive for patients but higher volumes). However, **specialist access, rural coverage, and GP workforce sustainability** remain significant challenges. Nordic comparators show that higher primary care investment yields both faster specialist access and better health outcomes - areas where NZ has room for improvement.

      🔮Future directions and reform drivers▾
      
        #### Current pressures and drivers for change
        
          **GP workforce shortage:** Burnout, early retirement, fewer medical graduates choosing general practice
          **Specialist demand exceeding capacity:** Elective waiting lists growing; private sector absorbing overflow
          **Health equity commitments:** Treaty of Waitangi; focus on reducing Māori and Pacific disparities
          **Chronic disease burden:** Increasing prevalence of diabetes, CVD, mental health; primary care overwhelmed
          **Ageing population:** More complex patients; higher medication costs

        #### Proposed and emerging reforms
        
          **Expanded community-based care:** Nurse practitioners, pharmacists, and allied health in primary care roles
          **Telehealth and digital health:** Growing uptake; improves rural and after-hours access
          **GP funding review:** Potential shift towards higher per-patient investment; recruitment and retention incentives
          **Specialist services in primary care:** Shared care models; reducing referral-dependent system
          **Mental health integration:** Better collaboration between primary and secondary mental health services
          **Free GP visits (policy proposals):** Some parties advocating removal of co-payments to reduce equity gaps
