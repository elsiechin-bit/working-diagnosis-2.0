          #### White coat and masked hypertension
          
            **White coat:** Elevated office BP, normal HBPM/ABPM; lower CV risk than true hypertension.
            **Masked:** Normal office BP, elevated HBPM/ABPM; similar CV risk to sustained hypertension; often missed.

        💊Pharmacological management▾
        
          #### First-line agents (ESC/ESH recommended)
          
            **ACE inhibitors (lisinopril, perindopril):** Reduce afterload; protective in diabetes and heart failure. Side effect: persistent dry cough (10–20%), hyperkalemia (monitor K+, creatinine).
            **Angiotensin receptor blockers (losartan, valsartan):** Similar efficacy to ACEi; no cough. First-line if ACEi intolerant.
            **Calcium channel blockers (amlodipine, nifedipine):** Reduce peripheral resistance; peripheral oedema common, reflex tachycardia with immediate-release forms.
            **Thiazide and thiazide-like diuretics (hydrochlorothiazide, chlorthalidone, indapamide):** Reduce blood volume and arterial stiffness. Side effects: hypokalaemia, hyperglycaemia, hyperuricaemia (gout), sexual dysfunction.
          
          #### Combination therapy
          
            **ACEi/ARB + CCB or diuretic:** Synergistic; most patients need 2+ agents.
            **ACEi/ARB + thiazide + CCB:** For resistant hypertension or high baseline BP.
            **Avoid ACEi + ARB together:** Increased hyperkalemia and renal impairment without added benefit.
          
          #### Second-line agents
          
            **Beta-blockers:** Third-line for hypertension alone (less effective than others); useful if prior MI, heart failure, or tachyarrhythmia.
            **Alpha-blockers:** Fourth-line; useful in prostate hyperplasia.
            **Methyldopa, hydralazine:** For resistant hypertension or pregnancy.
            **Spironolactone:** For resistant hypertension (especially if hypokalaemia on diuretics); monitor K+ and creatinine.

        🍎Lifestyle and non-pharmacological management▾
        
          #### Sodium restriction
          
            Target 
            - Greater effect in salt-sensitive individuals (African ancestry, older age, diabetes, CKD).
          
          #### Weight loss
          
            - 5–10 kg loss reduces BP by ~5–10 mmHg; most effective intervention for overweight patients.
          
          #### Exercise
          
            - 150 min/week aerobic exercise reduces BP by 5–7 mmHg.
            - Resistance training also effective.
          
          #### Alcohol reduction
          
            - Moderate alcohol (≤2 drinks/day men, ≤1 women) associated with lower CV risk; excess increases BP.
          
          #### Stress reduction and sleep
          
            - Meditation, yoga may modestly reduce BP.
            - Sleep apnoea screening and treatment important; can lower BP significantly.
          
          #### DASH diet
          
            - Emphasises fruits, vegetables, whole grains, lean protein, low fat; reduces BP by 8–14 mmHg.

        ❓Common questions▾
        
          #### Should I check my BP at home?
          Yes, if possible. Home readings are more representative of true BP and correlate better with target organ damage than office readings. Check at the same time daily, multiple times, and bring a log to your GP.

          #### Why is my BP higher at the doctor's office?
          White coat effect is common. If office BP is high but home BP is normal or near-normal, you may not need treatment. ABPM helps clarify.

          #### Can I stop blood pressure medication once BP is controlled?
          Usually no. Most people require lifelong treatment. Stopping medication usually results in BP rising again within weeks. Lifestyle changes can sometimes reduce medication doses, but rarely eliminate need.

          #### What if I forget a dose?
          Take it as soon as you remember, unless it's almost time for the next dose. Missing occasional doses is less harmful than stopping altogether, but consistency matters.
