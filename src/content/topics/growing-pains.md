## Overview


**Growing pains are recurrent episodes of leg pain in well children, typically bilateral, nocturnal, and functionally benign.** Despite the name, the pain is **not directly caused by growth** (no correlation with growth velocity or growth hormone surges). Aetiology remains incompletely understood; likely multifactorial: skeletal development, muscle traction on metaphyseal attachment sites, exercise-induced microtrauma, psychological stressor, or nocturnal muscle cramps. The key is diagnosis by exclusion of serious pathology.

        #### Classic presentation
        
          **Age:** 3–12 years, peak 5–8 years
          **Location:** Bilateral lower extremities (anterior/lateral thigh, calf, or anterior shin most common); rarely upper extremity; **not in joints** (extracapsular)
          **Timing:** Nocturnal, evening, or afternoon; episodic (days–weeks of pain, then pain-free period weeks–months); **not continuous**
          **Character:** "Aching," "throbbing," "cramping"; not sharp or localised to joint
          **Duration:** Usually 30 minutes to 2 hours per episode
          **Severity:** Can wake child at night; causes distress; may interfere with sleep but **not activity during daytime**
          **Activity level:** Pain may follow activity-heavy day (sports, physical play) or be unrelated to activity; **child walks/plays normally the next day**
          **Associated features:** No limp during daytime; normal strength, gait; child otherwise well; no fever, weight loss, or systemic symptoms
        
        #### What growing pains are NOT
        
          - Correlated with growth rate or height velocity
          - Associated with joint swelling, erythema, or warmth
          - Causing daytime functional impairment or avoidance of activity
          - Unilateral (in isolation) or constant
          - Accompanied by systemic symptoms (fever, weight loss, night sweats, rash)
          - Associated with limp, joint restriction, or inability to bear weight

      🔍Clinical assessment & red flags▾
      
        **Diagnosis is clinical.** The history is paramount. Investigations are unnecessary in typical cases and should be reserved for atypical presentations or red flags.

        #### History taking
        
          **Onset:** When did pain start? Gradual vs acute? Relation to injury/trauma?
          **Pattern:** Frequency of episodes? Duration? Time of day? Seasonal?
          **Location & character:** Bilateral? Which leg(s)? Joint vs muscle? Point tenderness or diffuse?
          **Associated symptoms:** Limp? Refusal to walk? Fever? Weight loss? Night sweats? Rash?
          **Activity history:** Sports? Recent increase in activity? Normal play the next day?
          **Impact:** Wakes at night? School absenteeism? Daytime activity avoidance?
          **Response to analgesia:** Does paracetamol/ibuprofen help? How quickly?
        
        #### Physical examination
        
          **Observation:** Gait normal? Weight-bearing pain-free? Child climbing on exam table without difficulty?
          **Legs:** No swelling, erythema, warmth, or bruising. No point tenderness over long bones. No tibial torsion or other skeletal deformity requiring specialist assessment.
          **Joints:** Full range of motion without pain. No joint effusion, warmth, or restriction. Hips, knees, ankles normal.
          **Strength:** Normal. No quadriceps wasting or muscle atrophy.
          **General:** Growth normal for age. No fever, lymphadenopathy, hepatosplenomegaly, or systemic findings.
        
        #### RED FLAGS (warrant imaging/further investigation)
        
          When to investigate
          Do NOT diagnose "growing pains" and send home if any of the following are present. Obtain imaging (plain X-ray of affected area) and consider referral:

          **Unilateral pain** (in isolation)
          **Pain in or around a joint** (hip, knee, ankle joint line) or **point tenderness over long bone**
          **Limp or refusal to weight-bear** on affected leg during day
          **Swelling, erythema, warmth,** or visible deformity
          **History of trauma or injury** preceding pain onset
          **Systemic symptoms:** Fever, night sweats, weight loss, fatigue, rash, lymphadenopathy
          **Persistent or worsening pain** despite analgesics; not episodic
          **Daytime functional impairment:** Avoidance of sports/play, school absence, difficulty sleeping (not just pain at night)
          Age 12 years (outside typical range; higher suspicion for pathology)

      ⚕️Differential diagnosis: serious pathology to exclude▾
      
        #### Malignancy (bone & soft tissue tumours)
        
          **Osteosarcoma, Ewing's sarcoma:** Painful swelling of long bone metaphysis; progressive, night pain not relieved by analgesics, limp, weight loss
          **Rhabdomyosarcoma:** Soft tissue mass; rapidly progressive; systemic symptoms
          **Neuroblastoma:** Back pain from spinal involvement; constitutional symptoms
          **Red flags:** Unilateral, progressive, point tenderness, swelling, night pain unrelieved by NSAIDs, systemic symptoms
        
        #### Infection
        
          **Osteomyelitis:** Acute onset; fever; limp; focal bone tenderness; elevated CRP/ESR; imaging changes
          **Septic arthritis:** Joint swelling; warmth; restricted range of motion; refusal to bear weight; fever
          **Red flags:** Fever, focal swelling, systemic toxicity, inability to weight-bear
        
        #### Inflammatory/systemic
        
          **Juvenile idiopathic arthritis (JIA):** Joint swelling, warmth, restriction, morning stiffness; systemic features (fever, rash, hepatosplenomegaly in systemic JIA)
          **Systemic lupus erythematosus, vasculitis:** Polyarthralgia, rash, constitutional symptoms, abnormal labs
          **Red flags:** Joint involvement, morning stiffness, fever, rash, elevated inflammatory markers
        
        #### Trauma & overuse
        
          **Stress fracture:** Unilateral, point tenderness over fracture site, limp, pain with weight-bearing
          **Tendinitis/overuse injury:** Localised tenderness; activity-related; may improve with rest (unlike growing pains)
          **Red flags:** History of specific injury, point tenderness, unilateral, functional impairment
        
        #### Haematologic
        
          **Leukaemia:** Bone pain, pallor, bruising, hepatosplenomegaly, lymphadenopathy, fever, rash
          **Sickle cell disease:** Vasoocclusive crisis; severe pain; history of haemolytic anaemia
          **Red flags:** Systemic symptoms, bleeding, hepatosplenomegaly, elevated WBC/low Hb
        
        #### Musculoskeletal/orthopaedic
        
          **Slipped capital femoral epiphysis (SCFE):** Adolescent (age 10–16); groin/inner thigh pain radiating to knee; limp; hip restricted internal rotation
          **Legg-Calvé-Perthes disease:** Age 5–9; hip pain/limp; reduced hip abduction/internal rotation
          **Red flags:** Hip pain, limp, restricted hip motion

      💊Management & reassurance▾
      
        **Growing pains are benign and self-resolving.** Management is entirely symptomatic: analgesia, warmth, massage, reassurance, and parental support.

        #### Acute pain relief
        
          **Paracetamol:** 15 mg/kg per dose (up to 1 g), 4-6 hourly, max 5 doses/24 h
          **Ibuprofen:** 5–10 mg/kg per dose (up to 400–600 mg), 6–8 hourly; superior anti-inflammatory effect; good first-line
          **Timing:** Give at onset of pain or before bed if pain predictably nocturnal
          **Efficacy:** Most children respond within 30 minutes; pain relief confirms benign nature (while supporting symptom control)
        
        #### Non-pharmacological comfort measures
        
          **Warmth:** Heating pad, warm shower, or warm compress to affected leg
          **Massage:** Gentle massage of affected muscles often provides relief; involves parents in care
          **Stretching:** Gentle leg stretches before bed; may reduce muscle cramps
          **Elevation:** Leg elevation on pillow
          **Distraction:** Television, stories, cuddles during pain episode
        
        #### Parental counselling & education
        
          **Reassurance of benign nature:** "These are growing pains—very common, not dangerous, and will go away on their own." Most parents fear serious illness.
          **Natural history:** Episodic pattern; pain-free periods normal; not progressive; usually resolve by age 12, rarely persist into adolescence
          **Not caused by growth:** Explain that pain is not directly related to height gain; helps set realistic expectations
          **Activity is normal:** Child should play normally the next day; pain at night does not mean restrict daytime activity
          **When to worry:** Red flags to watch for: persistent limp, daytime pain/avoidance, swelling, fever, or pain unresponsive to analgesics → return to GP
          **Normalisation:** Many parents experience relief from diagnosis; opportunity to discuss family history (growing pains may run in families)
        
        #### Follow-up
        
          **No routine follow-up needed** if diagnosis is clear and red flags absent
          **Safety netting:** Advise parents to return if symptoms change (e.g., limp develops, pain becomes unilateral and constant, swelling appears, fever develops)
          **Reassessment at next visit:** If still concerning on review, consider imaging to exclude occult pathology

      💡Clinical pearls & pitfalls▾
      
        #### Pearls
        
          ✦**Growing pains are a diagnosis of exclusion.** Classic presentation (bilateral, nocturnal, episodic, well child) needs no investigations. Red flags = imaging needed.
          ✦**Pain response to analgesia is diagnostic.** Pain that resolves within 30 min of paracetamol/ibuprofen supports benign diagnosis. Persistent pain despite analgesics warrants investigation.
          ✦**Unilateral pain is not growing pains.** Unilateral long-bone pain or joint pain until proven otherwise = imaging (X-ray); consider malignancy, SCFE, stress fracture.
          ✦**Reassurance is therapeutic.** Parents often fear serious illness. Clear explanation of benign nature, natural history, and red flags reduces anxiety and improves satisfaction.
          ✦**Daytime function is key discriminator.** If child walks, plays, and exercises normally during the day, likely benign. Daytime limp or activity avoidance = investigate.
          ✦**Hip pain is not growing pains.** Hip/groin pain requires careful assessment: SCFE, Perthes, JIA, septic arthritis. Never diagnose growing pains in hip pain without ruling these out.
          ✦**Growing pains are benign but real.** Child's pain is genuine; acknowledge discomfort without dismissing as psychological. Involve parents in comfort measures.
        
        #### Pitfalls
        
          **Diagnosing growing pains without excluding red flags.** Always ask: unilateral? Point tenderness? Limp? Swelling? Fever? Systemic symptoms? One "yes" = imaging first.
          **Assuming unilateral pain is growing pains.** Common error; unilateral pain carries higher malignancy risk. Low threshold for X-ray.
          **Dismissing child's pain as psychological.** Growing pains are real; parents resent being told pain is "in the head." Treat seriously while reassuring about benign nature.
          **Missing hip pathology.** Hip and groin pain (radiating to knee) = SCFE until proven otherwise in adolescent. Do not label as growing pains without assessing hip ROM.
          **Investigating every case.** Overinvestigation increases cost, radiation exposure, and parental anxiety. Typical presentation = history & exam only.
          **Forgetting safety netting.** Parents need to know what changes (limp, swelling, fever) warrant return. Unclear follow-up instructions breed anxiety.
          **Not reassessing if symptoms persist or evolve.** If pain pattern changes (becomes constant, unilateral, daytime, or persistent despite analgesics), revisit diagnosis and consider imaging.
