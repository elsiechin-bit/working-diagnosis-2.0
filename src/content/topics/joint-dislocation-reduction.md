#### When to reduce in primary care vs refer
        
          **Can reduce in primary care:** Simple dislocations (shoulder, finger, ankle) without fracture; neurovascular intact; patient cooperative
          **Refer urgently:** Complex dislocations, open dislocations, neurovascular compromise, failed reduction attempt, multiple fractures, patient sedation/GA needed

        #### Pre-reduction assessment
        
          **X-ray BEFORE reduction:** Confirm dislocation; rule out associated fractures (especially Bankart, Hill-Sachs)
          **Neurovascular exam:** Assess distal pulses, capillary refill, motor and sensory function BEFORE and AFTER reduction
          **Mechanism of injury:** High-energy trauma suggests occult fractures; elderly patients at high risk of fractures
          **Pain severity:** Pain = muscle spasm; adequate analgesia essential for successful reduction
          **Duration:** Longer duration = more muscle spasm and fibrosis; easier to reduce acutely

        #### Analgesia and muscle relaxation
        
          **Intra-articular injection:** Lidocaine 1% (10-20 mL) injected into joint space provides excellent pain relief and muscle relaxation without systemic effects
          **Systemic analgesia:** Paracetamol, NSAIDs, opioids (morphine 5-10 mg IM) if available
          **Benzodiazepines:** Diazepam 5-10 mg IM or midazolam if available; aids muscle relaxation
          **Nitrous oxide:** Entonox 50/50 mix if available; patient-controlled; rapid onset and offset
          **Sedation:** Only if facility supports airway management; otherwise refer for procedural sedation

        #### Contraindications to reduction in primary care
        
          - Open (compound) dislocation
          - Neurovascular compromise requiring urgent intervention
          - Associated fractures (especially fracture-dislocations)
          - Failed first reduction attempt (muscle spasm worsens; risk of soft tissue damage)
          - Patient unable to cooperate or requiring sedation beyond primary care capability
          - Polytrauma or multisystem injury
          - Very elderly with fragile bone stock (high fracture risk)

        #### Post-reduction protocol
        
          **X-ray immediately post-reduction:** Confirm reduction; check for occult fractures
          **Neurovascular re-check:** Document distal pulses, motor/sensory before immobilization
          **Immobilization:** Sling for shoulder; splint for elbow/finger/ankle; duration varies by joint
          **Analgesia:** Continue post-reduction; many patients have significant pain despite successful reduction
          **Ice and elevation:** Reduce swelling; may improve pain
          **Follow-up imaging:** Some dislocations need advanced imaging (CT, MRI) to assess cartilage and soft tissue injuries; arrange via orthopaedics

      💪Anterior shoulder dislocation▾
      
        Most common dislocation (95% of shoulder dislocations). Often reducible in primary care with adequate analgesia.

          Pre-reduction assessment
          **Classic presentation:** Arm held in abduction and external rotation; flattened shoulder; severe pain
          **X-ray (AP, lateral, axillary views):** Confirms anterior displacement; rule out fractures (Bankart, Hill-Sachs, tuberosity fracture)
          **Neurovascular:** Check axillary nerve (shoulder sensation, deltoid contraction); radial/ulnar pulses
          **First dislocation vs recurrent:** Recurrent dislocation may be easier to reduce; first dislocation often has associated fractures

          Reduction technique: External rotation method (preferred)
          
          **Advantages:** Gentle, requires less force, lower soft tissue trauma risk, no assistant required
          **Patient position:** Supine or semi-recumbent on bed or trolley; arm relaxed at side
          
          **Procedure:**
          
            1
            Flex elbow to 90°; rest forearm across patient's abdomen

            2
            Slowly externally rotate shoulder (bring forearm toward patient's head) over 5-10 minutes

            3
            Move slowly and steadily; stop if severe pain or resistance; wait for muscle relaxation (may take 5-10 min)

            4
            Once forearm reaches 90° external rotation, patient may feel "click" or shoulder reduction

            5
            Gently bring arm across body (internal rotation) to neutral position

            6
            Test for loss of anterior deformity; confirm with X-ray

          **Key point:** Patience and slow, gentle movement essential; force risk soft tissue damage

          Alternative: Scapular manipulation (adjunct)
          **Use when:** External rotation progressing slowly or appears stalled
          **Method:** Assistant presses on medial border of scapula toward midline (adduction); promotes reduction
          **Mechanism:** Scapular motion assists humeral head reduction into glenoid fossa

        #### Post-reduction
        
          - X-ray to confirm reduction and assess for fractures
          - Immobilize in sling for 3-4 weeks; some advocate early mobilization (discuss with ortho)
          - Physiotherapy for rotator cuff strengthening (prevents recurrence)
          - Referral to orthopaedics for assessment of Bankart/Hill-Sachs lesions and recurrence risk
          First-time dislocation: 50% recurrence risk in patients 

        #### Complications and when to refer
        
          **Failed reduction:** Refer urgently; may need procedural sedation or GA
          **Associated fracture:** Refer (Bankart, Hill-Sachs, tuberosity fracture); surgical intervention often needed
          **Axillary nerve injury:** Loss of shoulder sensation or deltoid weakness post-reduction; usually transient; refer for nerve assessment
          **Vascular injury:** Rare; if absent pulses persist post-reduction, urgent vascular surgery referral

      💪Posterior elbow dislocation▾
      
        Common in primary care; often reducible with good analgesia. Rule out fractures carefully.

          Pre-reduction assessment
          **Presentation:** Elbow flexed 45-90°; posterior prominence; severe pain; inability to extend
          **X-ray (AP, lateral, oblique):** Confirm dislocation; assess for associated fractures (coronoid, radial head, epicondyles)
          **Neurovascular:** Check radial, ulnar, brachial pulses; assess median, ulnar, radial nerve function
          **Duration:** Longer duration increases soft tissue fibrosis; may be harder to reduce

          Reduction technique
          
          **Patient position:** Prone on bed/trolley with arm hanging off edge; or supine with elbow flexed and shoulder abducted
          
          **Procedure:**
          
            1
            Apply longitudinal traction to forearm (steady pull distally, relieving muscle spasm)

            2
            While maintaining traction, apply anterior (volar) force to posterior aspect of proximal forearm

            3
            3
            Gently flex elbow while maintaining traction (as hand comes down, olecranon reduces into fossa)

            4
            Once reduced, gently extend and flex to confirm reduction; check for "clunk"

          **Key point:** Continuous traction essential; aggressive force risks neurovascular injury

        #### Post-reduction
        
          - X-ray immediately post-reduction; may reveal occult fractures not seen pre-reduction
          - Immobilize in 90° flexion sling for 2-3 weeks
          - Early mobilization (after 3-7 days) reduces stiffness; discuss with ortho
          - Physiotherapy for ROM and strength

        #### Red flags for referral
        
          - Fracture-dislocation (coronoid or radial head fracture); usually needs surgical fixation
          - Failed reduction after one attempt
          - Neurovascular compromise
          - Associated vascular injury

      🖐️Finger dislocation (PIP/DIP joint)▾
      
        Easiest dislocations to reduce in primary care. Usually straightforward; high success rate.

          PIP joint dislocation (most common)
          **Presentation:** Finger hyperextended or flexed; severe pain; swelling; prominent joint
          **X-ray:** Confirm direction (posterior most common, anterior less common); assess for chip fractures
          **Neurovascular:** Check digital pulses; sensory/motor of fingers
          
          **Reduction technique:**
          
            1
            Apply digital nerve block (2 mL 1% lignocaine medial and lateral to digit base)

            2
            Apply longitudinal traction to finger (steady pull distally for 30-60 seconds)

            3
            While maintaining traction, gently flex or extend finger (depending on direction of dislocation)

            4
            Feel for "clunk" as reduction occurs

            5
            Confirm with X-ray post-reduction

        #### Post-reduction
        
          - Immobilize with buddy taping (tape to adjacent finger) or small splint for 3-4 weeks
          - Allow early ROM once acute pain resolves
          - Physiotherapy if stiffness develops
          - Simple chip fractures usually settle with conservative care; displaced or intra-articular fragments may need referral

        #### When to refer
        
          - Failed reduction
          - Large chip fracture or fracture-dislocation with intra-articular involvement
          - Open dislocation (compound)
          - Vascular compromise

      🦵Ankle dislocation▾
      
        Relatively uncommon in isolation; usually associated with fracture. Reduce urgently to relieve neurovascular compression.

          Assessment
          **Presentation:** Severe pain; gross swelling; obvious deformity (foot displaced medially or laterally)
          **Mechanism:** High-energy trauma; fracture-dislocation common (lateral malleolus, medial malleolus, posterior tibial margin)
          **Neurovascular:** CRITICAL: Check dorsalis pedis and posterior tibial pulses; assess foot perfusion (colour, temperature)
          **X-ray:** Anteroposterior and lateral views; assess for associated fractures

          Reduction technique (posterior displacement most common)
          
          **Patient position:** Supine; knee slightly flexed to relax muscles
          
          **Procedure:**
          
            1
            Apply longitudinal traction to foot (steady pull distal; relieves muscle spasm)

            2
            Assess direction of displacement (anterior or posterior); often obvious from deformity

            3
            Apply gentle pressure in direction opposite to displacement (if posterior, push foot anteriorly)

            4
            Maintain traction throughout; move slowly and steadily

            5
            Feel for reduction; check foot alignment with tibia

            6
            X-ray immediately post-reduction; reassess neurovascular status

          **Key point:** Neurovascular urgency; if pulses absent pre-reduction, reduce immediately and reassess; refer urgently if pulses remain absent

        #### Post-reduction and referral
        
          - Nearly all ankle dislocations involve fracture; refer to orthopaedics for surgical fixation
          - Immobilize in neutral position pending ortho assessment
          - Check pulses regularly; if pulses absent post-reduction, urgent vascular surgery consultation
          - Admit for ortho management if not already admitted

      ⚠️Complications and when to refer urgently▾
      
        #### Neurovascular compromise (pre- or post-reduction)
        
          **Absent distal pulses:** Vascular injury or compression; urgent reduction if pre-reduction or vascular surgery if post-reduction
          **Neurological deficit (persistent post-reduction):** Nerve injury; assess type (axillary, radial, ulnar, median, etc.); most recover spontaneously; refer for nerve conduction studies if slow recovery
          **Pale or cold extremity:** Vascular compromise; urgent action needed

        #### Soft tissue injury
        
          **Open dislocation:** Wound contamination; urgent ortho/surgical involvement
          **Buttonhole (entrapped capsule/soft tissue):** Rare; may prevent reduction; refer for surgical exploration
          **Nerve entrapment:** Interposed soft tissue; assess clinically; may require surgical reduction

        #### Failed reduction
        
          - STOP after one gentle attempt; do not force
          - Refer urgently for procedural sedation and/or surgical reduction
          - Interposed soft tissue, fracture fragments, or muscle spasm usually responsible

        #### Associated fractures
        
          - Assess post-reduction X-rays carefully; may reveal occult fractures
          - Fracture-dislocation (especially elbow, ankle) usually requires surgical fixation
          - Refer to orthopaedics for surgical planning

        #### Recurrent dislocations
        
          - First-time anterior shoulder dislocation: 50-70% recurrence in young patients; assess risk and discuss ortho follow-up
          - Habitual dislocations (finger, shoulder) may reduce more easily but need specialist assessment for ligament reconstruction

        #### Checklist: When to attempt reduction vs refer
        
          - Simple dislocation without fracture? YES — can attempt
          - Neurovascular intact? YES — proceed with care
          - Patient cooperating and adequate analgesia possible? YES — attempt
          - Facility equipped with X-ray and sedation capability? IMPORTANT — have plan B
          - First reduction attempt likely to succeed? UNCERTAIN — refer if complex or high-risk
