#### Mechanism
        Histamine is released from mast cells and basophils in allergic reactions, binding H1 receptors on skin, blood vessels, airways, and GI tract. H1 antagonists block these receptors, reducing itching, wheal-and-flare reactions, oedema, and bronchoconstriction.

        #### First-generation (sedating) antihistamines
        
          **Examples:** Diphenhydramine, chlorphenamine (Piriton), promethazine.
          **Onset:** 30 minutes; peak 1-2 hours.
          **Duration:** 4-6 hours (shorter than second-generation).
          **Advantages:** Cheap; useful in acute reactions (strong anticholinergic effect); good for anaphylaxis adjunct (IM chlorphenamine 2mg).
          **Disadvantages:** Cross blood-brain barrier; sedation, impaired cognition, dry mouth, blurred vision. Not suitable for regular daytime use.
          **Use:** Acute allergic reactions, itching/urticaria at night, adjunct to adrenaline in anaphylaxis.
        
        #### Second-generation (non-sedating) antihistamines
        
          **Examples:** Cetirizine (Piriteze), loratadine (Claratyne), fexofenadine (Telfast).
          **Onset:** 20-60 minutes; peak 1-2 hours.
          **Duration:** 12-24 hours (longer than first-generation).
          **Advantages:** Minimal sedation (highly selective for peripheral H1); can be used regularly for chronic allergies; once or twice daily dosing.
          **Disadvantages:** Slightly slower onset than first-generation; more expensive.
          **Use:** First-line for allergic rhinitis, urticaria, eczema itch; preferred for regular use and driving.
        
        #### Dosing (adults)
        
          **Cetirizine:** 10mg once daily or 5mg BD.
          **Loratadine:** 10mg once daily.
          **Fexofenadine:** 180mg once daily or 120mg BD.
          **Chlorphenamine:** 4mg 3-4 times daily (acute); 4mg at night (chronic).
        
        #### Efficacy
        Reduce itch by 50-70%, wheal-and-flare reactions by 40-60%. Less effective for severe angioedema or anaphylaxis (need adrenaline). Therapeutic effect reaches plateau; increasing dose beyond recommended does not improve efficacy.

      🩹Topical and intranasal corticosteroids▾
      
        #### Intranasal corticosteroids (allergic rhinitis)
        
          **Examples:** Fluticasone propionate (Flonase), mometasone (Nasonex), beclomethasone.
          **Mechanism:** Reduce mucosal inflammation, suppress mast cell and eosinophil activation.
          **Efficacy:** Superior to antihistamines for nasal congestion, superior to or equal to antihistamines for sneezing/itch. Takes 2-3 days for full effect.
          **Dosing:** Fluticasone 2 sprays each nostril once or twice daily; mometasone 2 sprays once or twice daily.
          **Safety:** Minimal systemic absorption; safe in children and pregnancy. Local side effects: nasal irritation, epistaxis (rare).
          **Use:** First-line for moderate to severe allergic rhinitis; often combined with oral antihistamine for optimal control.
        
        #### Topical corticosteroids (allergic dermatitis, eczema)
        
          **Examples:** Hydrocortisone 1%, betamethasone, clobetasol (potency varies).
          **Mechanism:** Suppress local inflammation, reduce itching and dermatitis.
          **Potency:** Ultra-potent (clobetasol), potent (betamethasone), moderate (triamcinolone), mild (hydrocortisone). Choose based on site and severity.
          **Dosing:** Apply thin layer once or twice daily to affected areas. Do not use on face/intertriginous areas for prolonged periods (skin atrophy risk).
          **Duration:** Use for 1-2 weeks; reassess. Prolonged use risks skin atrophy, striae, telangiectasia.
          **Safety:** Minimal systemic absorption at typical doses. Avoid face and flexural areas with potent formulations.

      💉Systemic corticosteroids▾
      
        #### Use in allergy
        
          **Acute severe reactions:** Severe angioedema, acute asthma exacerbation (allergic trigger), serum sickness, acute urticaria unresponsive to antihistamines.
          **Anaphylaxis adjunct:** After adrenaline, give IV hydrocortisone 100mg to prevent biphasic reaction (recurrence 1-72 hours later).
        
        #### Dosing
        
          **Acute reaction (oral):** Prednisolone 0.5-1mg/kg once daily for 3-5 days (no taper needed for short courses).
          **IV (anaphylaxis/severe):** Hydrocortisone 100-500mg IV depending on severity.
        
        #### Not first-line for chronic allergies
        Oral corticosteroids carry significant long-term toxicity (bone loss, infection risk, metabolic effects). Avoid regular use for chronic allergic rhinitis or urticaria; prefer topical or biologic agents.

      🫁Leukotriene antagonists▾
      
        #### Mechanism
        Leukotrienes (LTC4, LTD4, LTE4) are inflammatory mediators released from mast cells and eosinophils. They cause airway bronchoconstriction, mucus secretion, and inflammation. Leukotriene receptor antagonists (LTRA) block these effects.

        #### Agents
        
          **Montelukast (Singulair):** Cys-LT1 receptor antagonist; 4-10mg once daily (evening).
          **Zafirlukast (Accolate):** Less commonly used; 20mg twice daily.
        
        #### Efficacy
        
          - Modest benefit for allergic rhinitis; usually combined with intranasal corticosteroid or antihistamine.
          - Useful for aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease (AERD) and allergic asthma with nasal symptoms.
          - May help with food allergy symptoms in some cases.
        
        #### Adverse effects
        
          - Generally well-tolerated. Rare: neuropsychiatric effects (mood changes, suicidality) in children; monitor closely.

      ⚡Adrenaline (epinephrine)▾
      
        #### Mechanism
        Catecholamine agonist. Alpha-1 effects cause vasoconstriction (counters hypotension, reduces angioedema). Beta-2 effects cause bronchodilation (relieves airway obstruction). Beta-1 effects increase heart rate and contractility.

        #### Anaphylaxis: first and only definitive treatment
        
          **Dose:** 0.3mg (0.3mL of 1:1000 solution) IM in lateral thigh muscle. Can repeat every 5-15 minutes if symptoms persist.
          **Timing:** Give immediately at first sign of anaphylaxis; do not delay for testing or transfer.
          **Route:** IM is safer and more effective than IV or SC; intramuscular administration ensures rapid tissue absorption.
        
        #### After adrenaline
        
          - IV access, fluid resuscitation, horizontal positioning (legs elevated unless vomiting).
          - H1 antihistamine (chlorphenamine 2mg IM/IV) once adrenaline given.
          - Corticosteroid (hydrocortisone 100mg IV) to prevent biphasic reaction.
          - Admit for observation (at least 4-6 hours).
        
        #### Auto-injectors (EpiPen)
        
          0.3mg (adult) or 0.15mg (child 
          - Prescribed for patients with severe/anaphylaxis allergy risk.
          - Training essential: jab into lateral thigh through clothing if needed, hold 3 seconds, massage injection site 10 seconds.
          - Carry two devices; use first at sign of anaphylaxis, call emergency (999/111), use second if no improvement after 5-15 minutes.
        
        #### Adverse effects (of adrenaline)
        Tachycardia, tremor, anxiety, headache — expected and transient. Risk of arrhythmia if very high dose or IV. Benefit far outweighs risk in anaphylaxis.

      🧬Biologic agents▾
      
        #### Omalizumab (anti-IgE)
        
          **Mechanism:** Monoclonal antibody against IgE; binds free IgE and prevents mast cell triggering.
          **Uses:** Moderate to severe IgE-mediated allergies (food, environmental, drug), chronic spontaneous urticaria, allergic asthma.
          **Dosing:** 150-375mg SC monthly based on baseline IgE and weight.
          **Efficacy:** Significant improvement in 50-70% of severe allergic rhinitis and urticaria patients; takes 4-8 weeks to reach full effect.
          **Cost:** Very expensive; requires prior authorisation in most public systems.
        
        #### Dupilumab (anti-IL-4 receptor)
        
          **Mechanism:** Blocks IL-4 receptor; reduces Th2 cytokine signalling (IL-4 and IL-13).
          **Uses:** Moderate to severe atopic dermatitis, allergic rhinitis, asthma.
          **Dosing:** 300-600mg SC loading, then 150-300mg every 2 weeks.
          **Efficacy:** Rapid improvement in itch and skin inflammation; marked improvement in 60-80% of patients with atopic dermatitis.
        
        #### Mepolizumab and reslizumab (anti-IL-5)
        
          **Mechanism:** Block IL-5 (eosinophil growth and survival factor).
          **Uses:** Severe eosinophilic asthma, eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis (EGPA).
          **Efficacy:** Reduce blood eosinophils by >95%; prevent asthma exacerbations in eosinophilic disease.
        
        #### Benralizumab (anti-IL-5 receptor)
        
          **Mechanism:** Binds IL-5 receptor on eosinophils and basophils, triggering apoptosis.
          **Uses:** Severe eosinophilic asthma, chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyps (CRSNP).
          **Dosing:** 30mg SC at weeks 0, 4, 8 then every 4 weeks (or every 8 weeks after initial doses).

      🧪Immunotherapy (desensitisation)▾
      
        #### Allergen immunotherapy (AIT)
        
          **Mechanism:** Gradual exposure to increasing allergen doses to induce immune tolerance (shift from Th2 to Treg/Th1).
          **Subcutaneous (SCIT):** Weekly or monthly injections over 3-5 years. Effective for pollen, dust mite, pet dander, insect venom.
          **Sublingual (SLIT):** Tablets/drops placed under tongue; growing popularity for grass and tree pollen, dust mite.
          **Efficacy:** 50-70% symptom reduction; benefits persist years after stopping. Works best for environmental allergies; less effective for food allergy.
          **Monitoring:** Give in controlled setting; anaphylaxis risk (low but real). Contraindicated if uncontrolled asthma.

      ⚖️Treatment algorithm by allergen type▾
      
        #### Allergic rhinitis
        
          **Mild:** Intranasal antihistamine (azelastine) or intranasal corticosteroid.
          **Moderate/severe:** Intranasal corticosteroid + oral antihistamine. Add leukotriene antagonist if inadequate.
          **Refractory:** Consider biologic (omalizumab, dupilumab) or allergen immunotherapy if specific allergen identified.
        
        #### Urticaria/angioedema
        
          **Acute:** H1 antihistamine (second-generation preferred); add systemic corticosteroid if severe or evolving anaphylaxis.
          **Chronic spontaneous:** H1 antihistamine (regular, not PRN). If inadequate, add cyclosporine or omalizumab.
        
        #### Allergic asthma
        
          **Mild intermittent:** SABA as needed.
          **Persistent:** ICS + LABA; consider add-on leukotriene antagonist if allergic rhinitis.
          **Severe/eosinophilic:** Biologic (mepolizumab, dupilumab) + high-dose ICS/LABA.
        
        #### Food allergy
        
          **Acute reaction:** Adrenaline IM (if anaphylaxis), H1 antihistamine, observation.
          **Prevention:** Strict avoidance; carry adrenaline auto-injector. Oral immunotherapy experimental (not routine).
        
        #### Anaphylaxis
        
          **Emergency:** Adrenaline 0.3mg IM immediately. Then IV access, fluids, antihistamine (chlorphenamine 2mg IM), corticosteroid (hydrocortisone 100mg IV).
          **Prevention:** Adrenaline auto-injector prescription, allergen avoidance, MedicAlert bracelet.

      ❓Common questions▾
      
        #### Is antihistamine enough for anaphylaxis?
        No. Antihistamine alone does NOT stop anaphylaxis. Only adrenaline (epinephrine) given IM immediately is effective. Antihistamines help reduce itch and swelling but cannot reverse airway obstruction or hypotension. Always give adrenaline first.

        #### Can I take antihistamines regularly long-term?
        Yes, second-generation antihistamines (cetirizine, loratadine) are safe for long-term daily use. First-generation (sedating) antihistamines are not recommended for regular daytime use due to cognitive effects. Tachyphylaxis (tolerance) is rare with appropriate dosing.

        #### Why do intranasal corticosteroids work better than antihistamines for nasal congestion?
        Congestion is mainly caused by mucosal swelling (vascular engorgement), not histamine. Antihistamines reduce itch and wheal-and-flare but do not address mucosal inflammation well. Corticosteroids suppress the inflammatory cascade (mast cells, eosinophils, cytokines) and reduce oedema more effectively.

        #### Can I use adrenaline auto-injectors myself or only paramedics?
        You (or a bystander) can and should use an auto-injector immediately if anaphylaxis occurs. Do not wait for paramedics. Strap it into the thigh (through clothing is OK), hold for 3 seconds, massage 10 seconds. Call 999/111 after administering.

        #### How long do biologics take to work?
        Most biologics take 2-8 weeks to reach full effect. Omalizumab: 4-8 weeks. Dupilumab: 2-4 weeks. Mepolizumab: 4-8 weeks. Start treatment and continue for at least 8-12 weeks before assessing response. Do not stop topical treatments immediately.

        #### Are biologics safe in pregnancy?
        Some biologics have safer data than others. Omalizumab and dupilumab have been used in pregnancy with reassuring safety data. Immunotherapy (AIT) is generally continued if already established. Consult your allergist for individual risk-benefit.
