## Overview


**Emollients are first-line therapy for all dry skin conditions & dermatitis.** They repair barrier dysfunction, reduce water loss, and diminish need for topical steroids. Safe for long-term use; can be used alongside or instead of topical steroids depending on severity.

        #### How they work
        
          **Barrier repair:** Occlusive lipids (petrolatum, oils) seal in water, restoring stratum corneum integrity
          **Hydration:** Humectants (glycerol, urea) draw moisture into epidermis
          **Anti-inflammatory:** Emollients reduce transepidermal water loss (TEWL), lower pruritus & scratching cycle
          **Reduce steroid need:** Daily emollient use often reduces or eliminates topical steroid requirement in mild–moderate dermatitis
        
        #### PHARMAC-funded emollients (NZ)
        
          Product nameType & key constituentsFormUse
          
            **Aqueous cream**Emulsion (cetyl stearyl alcohol, liquid paraffin, water)CreamMild dermatitis; face & body; washable
            **Sorbolene cream**Emulsion (sorbitol, glycerol, lanolin-free)CreamEczema & dry skin; gentle; low irritancy
            **Liquid paraffin / white soft paraffin**Occlusive oilLiquid or ointmentSevere barrier dysfunction; bath oil (liquid) or emollient (ointment)
            **Unguentum M**Emulsion (water-in-oil); arachis oil, beeswaxOintmentIntense dry skin; face & body; highly emollient

          Key: "Ointment" occludes better than "cream"
          Ointments (oil-based, water-free) provide superior barrier seal; creams (emulsions) are lighter & more cosmetically acceptable. Choose ointment for severe barrier dysfunction; cream for mild–moderate or face.

        #### Application technique & frequency
        
          **Amount:** Liberal application; "two-finger rule" (tip of index + middle finger ≈ 500 mg) covers forearm; scale up proportionally for larger areas
          **Frequency:** Daily, at least 2–3 times; more often if skin feels tight or itchy. No upper limit—frequent use is safe and encouraged
          **Timing:** Apply immediately after bathing (within 3 minutes) to seal in hydration; reapply throughout day as needed
          **Bath oil:** Add liquid paraffin to warm bath; slippery floor risk—supervise children; emerges less effective than applied ointment but improves compliance in some patients
        
        #### Properties of common emollients
        
          **Aqueous cream:** Affordable, washable, face-friendly; less occlusive than ointment; adequate for mild dermatitis maintenance
          **Sorbolene:** Lanolin-free; good for sensitive skin; emollient base for compounded preparations
          **White soft paraffin / Vaseline:** Maximum occlusion; greasy; excellent for severe dry skin; some patients dislike feel
          **Unguentum M:** Gold standard for severe barrier dysfunction; highly occlusive; non-comedogenic base; arachis oil (peanut allergen risk if ingested)
          **QV cream:** Light, less greasy; non-comedogenic; good cosmetic acceptability; eczema-specific formulation

      💊Topical steroids: potency classification & PHARMAC-funded agents▾
      
        **Topical steroids are potent anti-inflammatory agents for acute flares of dermatitis, psoriasis, and eczema.** Classified by potency (Class I–IV in NZ/Australasian system); potency drives efficacy, but also systemic absorption risk and skin atrophy liability.

        #### Potency classification (Australasian)
        
          ClassPotencyExamples (PHARMAC)Typical use
          
            **I**Super-potentClobetasol propionate 0.05% (Dermovate, Temovate)Resistant plaques, palms/soles; ≤2 weeks
            **II–III**Potent–very potentBetamethasone dipropionate 0.05% (Diprosone), Fluocinonide, Mometasone furoateModerate–severe dermatitis, face OK for short courses
            **III–IV**Moderate–mildTriamcinolone acetonide 0.1% (Triacin, Ledercort), Hydrocortisone 1% (Hydrocortone), Clobetasone butyrate 0.05% (Eumovate)Mild dermatitis, face, flexures, children

          Potency varies by base & concentration
          Same active ingredient in different bases has different potency (e.g., betamethasone 0.1% cream is Class III; 0.05% ointment is Class II). Ointments are more potent than creams due to enhanced penetration.

        #### How topical steroids work
        
          **Mechanism:** Bind glucocorticoid receptors in dermal fibroblasts & immune cells → suppress inflammatory mediators (cytokines, prostaglandins, leukocyte infiltration)
          **Onset:** Rapid (hours to 1–2 days); peak effect by day 3–7
          **Systemic absorption:** Varies by site (face ~35% absorption vs forearm ~2%) & potency; even "mild" steroids have measurable absorption if applied extensively
          **Duration:** Effects persist 12–24 hours after application; accumulation with daily dosing
        
        #### Pharmacokinetics by site (% transcutaneous absorption)
        
          **High risk (>20%):** Face, neck, axilla, groin, anogenital, inframammary folds
          **Moderate (5–20%):** Forearms, trunk
          Low ( Palms, soles (thick stratum corneum; poor absorption; allows super-potent steroids here)
        
        #### PHARMAC-funded topical steroids (NZ selection)
        
          **Hydrocortisone 1% (Class IV, mild):** Safe for face & intertriginous areas; first-line for mild dermatitis; can be OTC; long safety record
          **Clobetasone butyrate 0.05% (Class III–IV, mild–moderate):** Eumovate; face-friendly; short-term use ≤2 weeks face, ≤4 weeks body
          **Triamcinolone acetonide 0.1% (Class III, moderate):** Ledercort, Triacin; widespread use; reasonable for flexures; avoid prolonged face use
          **Betamethasone dipropionate 0.05% (Class II, very potent):** Diprosone; for resistant body dermatitis; NOT face; ≤2 weeks
          **Clobetasol propionate 0.05% (Class I, super-potent):** Dermovate; palms/soles/scalp/anogenital; ≤2 weeks; last-line only

      🎯When to use emollients vs steroids & step-up/down strategy▾
      
        **Emollients form the baseline of all dermatitis management. Topical steroids are added for active inflammation, then stepped down or ceased as skin clears.** The goal is to minimise steroid exposure while controlling symptoms.

        #### Clinical decision-making
        
          Clinical pictureFirst-lineAdd steroid if…Steroid choice
          
            **Dry skin, no inflammation**Emollient alone (2–3× daily)N/A (usually not needed)—
            **Mild dermatitis (erythema, mild pruritus)**Emollient + hydrocortisone 1% (≤2 weeks)Fails after 7–10 days or lesions spreadingHydrocortisone 1% or clobetasone 0.05%
            **Moderate dermatitis (marked erythema, excoriation, ooze)**Emollient + Class III (triamcinolone or clobetasone) ≤2 weeks face, ≤4 weeks bodyAlready indicated; escalate if resistantTriamcinolone 0.1% or betamethasone 0.05%
            **Severe or resistant dermatitis**Emollient + Class II–I steroid (betamethasone, clobetasol) ≤2 weeksDefine treatment window; consider systemic steroid or dermatology referralBetamethasone 0.05% or clobetasol 0.05% (short course only)
            **Face dermatitis**Emollient + hydrocortisone 1% OR clobetasone 0.05% (NOT stronger)Persistent after 10 days; referHydrocortisone 1% preferred
            **Intertriginous (groin, axilla, inframammary)**Emollient + hydrocortisone 1% (maceration risk; avoid potent steroids)Poor response after 10 daysHydrocortisone 1% only; refer if refractory

        #### Step-up (during flare)
        
          **Mild flare:** Increase emollient frequency; add hydrocortisone 1% for 7–10 days
          **No response after 10 days:** Move to Class III (triamcinolone 0.1%) for 2 weeks; ensure correct application (large amounts, not just dabbing)
          **Severe/failing Class III:** Short course Class II steroid (betamethasone) or refer to dermatology (consider systemic steroid, phototherapy, immunosuppression)
        
        #### Step-down (as inflammation resolves)
        
          **Week 1–2 flare control:** Hydrocortisone 1% or Class III (triamcinolone) twice daily
          **Week 3–4 (improvement evident):** Reduce frequency to once daily, or switch to alternate days
          **Week 4–5:** Cease steroid; continue emollient 2–3× daily indefinitely
          **Maintenance (no active dermatitis):** Emollient alone; no steroid needed
          **Never:** Stop abruptly after prolonged steroid course (rebound flare). Taper frequency, not dose.

          Emollients + step-down = deprescribing success
          Patients on chronic topical steroids (Class II–III) often respond to increased emollient frequency + slower steroid taper. 60–80% can achieve control on emollients alone if barrier repair is prioritised.

      ⚠️Adverse effects & safety considerations▾
      
        #### Topical steroid adverse effects
        
          **Local (skin):** Atrophy (skin thinning, telangiectasia, striae) with potent steroids >4 weeks; maceration in intertriginous areas; burning/stinging; hypopigmentation; folliculitis (bacterial superinfection if skin breakdown)
          **Secondary infection:** Bacterial or fungal overgrowth; potent steroids impair local immunity
          **Perioral dermatitis:** Chronic facial steroid use → erythematous papules around mouth; rebounds when steroid ceased
          **Systemic (with overuse):** Measurable hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis suppression if extensive potent steroid applied daily >2–3 weeks; cushing-like features (rare but documented in childhood). Risk is low with appropriate potency/duration matching, but rises significantly with misuse.
        
        #### Emollient adverse effects
        
          **Skin maceration:** Excessive occlusion in intertriginous areas → warmth, moisture, secondary infection. Mitigate by drying intertriginous areas before application & using lighter cream instead of ointment.
          **Allergic contact dermatitis:** Rare; ingredients (preservatives, lanolin, arachis oil) can sensitise. If worsening on emollient, switch product or patch-test.
          **Systemic absorption:** Emollients have minimal systemic absorption; safe for long-term use even in high-risk groups (pregnant, breastfeeding, children).
        
        #### Red flags for systemic steroid absorption (overuse risk)
        
          - Applying potent steroid (Class II–I) daily for >4 weeks on large body surface area
          - Using potent steroid on face, neck, groin regularly (high absorption sites)
          - Occlusive dressing (plastic wrap) under topical steroid (increases absorption 100-fold)
          - Child receiving potent steroid on >30% body surface area
          - Patient reports striae, skin atrophy, easy bruising, or mood/sleep changes (HPA suppression signs)

          Steroid-induced skin atrophy is slow to reverse
          Chronic potent steroid use causes permanent dermal thinning, telangiectasia, and striae. Prevention (appropriate potency/duration) is far superior to treatment. Always deprescribe when control is achieved.

      🏥Application technique & practical prescribing▾
      
        #### Topical steroid application
        
          **Amount:** Fingertip unit (FTU; tip of adult index finger from distal interphalangeal crease to tip ≈ 0.5 g) covers one hand's palm surface. Scale: each FTU covers ~2% body surface area. Use 2–4 FTU for moderate flare on limb or trunk.
          **Frequency:** Once or twice daily, depending on potency & site. Hydrocortisone 1% can be used 2–3 times daily; potent steroids ≤once daily.
          **Duration:** Limit to 2 weeks for potent steroids (Class II–I); 2–4 weeks for moderate (Class III); hydrocortisone 1% can be extended if responsive. Face ≤2 weeks for any steroid.
          **After application:** Allow to dry; do not occlude with dressing unless specifically instructed (eg, scalp psoriasis under shower cap overnight).
        
        #### Emollient application
        
          **Amount:** Liberal; "two-finger rule" for forearm, scale proportionally. Use 1–2 teaspoons per bath for bath oil.
          **Frequency:** 2–3 times minimum; more if skin feels tight. Encourage patients to apply immediately after bathing (within 3 minutes of drying skin lightly).
          **Layer with steroid:** If using both, apply emollient first, let dry (~5 min), then steroid on active lesions. (Or: steroid in morning, emollient at night, both morning & night if moderate flare.)
        
        #### Prescribing example (moderate eczema flare)
        
          **Day 1–7:** Triamcinolone acetonide 0.1% cream, twice daily to affected areas. Sorbolene cream, 2–3 times daily (emollient baseline).
          **Day 8–14:** Triamcinolone once daily; increase sorbolene frequency to 4 times daily.
          **Day 15 onwards:** Cease triamcinolone; sorbolene alone, 2–3 times daily indefinitely (maintenance).
          **Safety netting:** Return if flare recurs; avoid prolonged steroid use; use largest quantities of emollient patient can tolerate.
        
        #### Counselling points
        
          **Emollients are not creams/lotions:** They are treatment; apply liberally and often. Do not ration.
          **Steroids work fastest when used correctly:** Apply to actively inflamed areas; do not dilute with emollient on same spot (reduces efficacy). Use steroid on active lesions, emollient everywhere.
          **Gradual weaning prevents rebound:** Stopping steroid abruptly → flare. Reduce frequency, not dose; taper over 1–2 weeks.
          **Maintenance is emollients:** Even "cured" eczema requires ongoing emollient use (often 2× daily) to prevent relapse.

      🧠Deprescribing topical steroids & long-term management▾
      
        **Many patients remain on chronic potent topical steroids unnecessarily.** Structured deprescribing using aggressive emollient therapy is often successful.

        #### Red flags for steroid dependence/overuse
        
          - Patient applying potent steroid daily for >6 months
          - Using large quantities (>30 g/week of potent steroid cream)
          - Visible skin atrophy, telangiectasia, or easy bruising
          - Steroid-sparing attempts previously unsuccessful
          - Flares immediately when steroid ceased (may be rebound or undertreated dermatitis)
        
        #### Deprescribing strategy
        
          **Step 1: Audit.** Document current steroid use, potency, duration, application sites. Assess for complications (atrophy, telangiectasia).
          **Step 2: Emollient intensification.** Increase frequency to 4–6 times daily; switch to ointment if using cream. May take 2–4 weeks for barrier to repair before weaning steroid.
          **Step 3: Steroid step-down.** If skin stable, reduce potency class first (e.g., betamethasone 0.05% → triamcinolone 0.1%); then reduce frequency (daily → alternate days); then cease. Duration: 4–8 weeks.
          **Step 4: Rebound management.** If flare occurs during taper, return to previous potency for 1 week, then retry slower taper. Support patient expectations (temporary worsening is normal).
          **Step 5: Long-term maintenance.** Emollient 2–3× daily indefinitely; hydrocortisone 1% or Class III steroid on standby for acute flares (not chronic use).
        
        #### Success rate & barriers
        
          **Success:** 60–80% of chronic steroid users can achieve control on intensive emollient therapy ± occasional short-burst steroid during flares.
          **Barriers:** Poor compliance with frequent emollient application; impatience during rebound flare; cost of high-quantity emollients (advocate for bulk supply).
          **When deprescribing fails:** Consider systemic therapy (oral corticosteroid or steroid-sparing: methotrexate, azathioprine, calcineurin inhibitors) or dermatology review. Some patients genuinely require ongoing topical steroid; goal then is to use lowest potency & frequency needed to control symptoms.

          Patient education is key
          Explain that emollients are long-term maintenance, steroids are acute flare treatment. Many patients fear steroids (rightly) but underestimate emollients. Framing deprescribing as "training your skin to repair itself" improves adherence.

      👶Special populations: children, pregnancy, breastfeeding▾
      
        #### Children (neonates–16 years)
        
          **Emollients:** Safe from birth; first-line for all dermatitis. No upper limit on use or frequency. Consider ingredients (peanut allergy for arachis-oil products if child at risk).
          **Topical steroids:** Use lowest potency appropriate to site. Hydrocortisone 1% safe for face, neck, flexures. Avoid potent steroids (Class II–I) except palms/soles; risk of HPA axis suppression higher in children due to higher skin permeability & body surface area ratio.
          **Duration:** ≤2 weeks even for milder steroids (Class III–IV) on face/flexures; ≤4 weeks on trunk/limbs. Close follow-up essential.
          **Occlusion:** Avoid occlusive dressings in children (very high systemic absorption risk).
        
        #### Pregnancy
        
          **Emollients:** Completely safe; use liberally throughout pregnancy. No teratogenic risk.
          **Topical steroids:** Generally safe in pregnancy if used appropriately (low potency, short duration, non-occluded). Hydrocortisone 1% preferred for face/flexures. Potent steroids (Class II–I) should be avoided or limited to short courses on resistant lesions. No strong teratogenic evidence, but prenatal safety data limited; prescribe only if benefit clearly outweighs risk.
          **Safe in pregnancy:** Emollients, hydrocortisone 1%, clobetasone butyrate 0.05%.
          **Caution:** Triamcinolone, betamethasone, clobetasol.
        
        #### Breastfeeding
        
          **Emollients:** Safe. No systemic absorption or transfer to milk.
          **Topical steroids:** Minimal transfer to breast milk with moderate-potency steroids applied appropriately (non-occluded, short duration). Hydrocortisone & clobetasone safe. If applying to breast/areola, wipe off before feeding. Potent steroids (Class II–I) acceptable for short bursts but avoid chronic use; long-term systemic absorption risk to mother.
          **Safe options:** Hydrocortisone 1%, clobetasone butyrate, triamcinolone (short-term).

      💡Clinical pearls & pitfalls▾
      
        #### Pearls
        
          ✦**Emollients are the foundation.** Every dermatitis patient needs regular emollients. Often emollients alone control mild–moderate cases if applied frequently & generously.
          ✦**Match potency to site.** Face = hydrocortisone 1% only. Palms/soles = Class I allowed. Intertriginous = hydrocortisone 1% only (maceration risk). Wrong potency site = poor efficacy or harm.
          ✦**Duration limits prevent atrophy.** Class III steroids on face ≤2 weeks; Class II ≤2 weeks anywhere; hydrocortisone can run longer if needed. Enforce limits; patient memory is unreliable.
          ✦**Taper reduces rebound.** Never stop potent steroid abruptly. Reduce frequency over 1–2 weeks. Rebound flare is normal; counsel patient upfront to prevent premature cessation.
          ✦**Apply steroid on active lesions only.** Do not mix with emollient; does not combine efficacy. Use: emollient on normal/recovering skin, steroid on inflamed areas, both morning & night if needed.
          ✦**Patients underestimate emollient quantity.** "Two-finger rule" feels excessive; educate that liberal use reduces steroid need. Large quantities (100+ g/week) are safe for long-term maintenance.
          ✦**Deprescribing works if emollient adherence is high.** 60–80% of chronic steroid users can step down if emollients increased to 4–6× daily & patient educated on rebound management.
        
        #### Pitfalls
        
          **Using potent steroid on face long-term.** Leads to perioral dermatitis, atrophy, telangiectasia. Limit hydrocortisone 1% or clobetasone ≤2 weeks face.
          **Not increasing emollient when steroid inadequate.** If dermatitis worsening on appropriate steroid, often the problem is insufficient emollient, not need for higher-potency steroid.
          **Prescribing without counselling duration limits.** Patient applies potent steroid indefinitely. Set clear stop date; document duration target; follow up to confirm cessation.
          **Occlusive dressing without warning.** Plastic wrap under steroid → 100-fold absorption increase. If recommending occlusion (psoriasis, severe lichenification), use hydrocortisone 1% only & ≤overnight.
          **Mistaking rebound for treatment failure.** Stopping steroid → temporary flare (rebound); patient interprets as "steroid wasn't working" or "I need stronger." Explain rebound upfront; lower threshold for temporary hydrocortisone 1% during taper.
          **Ignoring secondary infection signs.** Potent steroids mask infection; watch for pustules, crusting, purulent drainage. If present, treat infection (antibacterial/antifungal wash or oral agent) before or alongside steroid.
