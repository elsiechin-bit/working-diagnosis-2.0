## The conversation you're actually having
      Insomnia is not a disease—it's a symptom of something. But in our rush to medicate it, we've turned it into a label. A patient comes in saying "I can't sleep," and we hear a sleep problem. What we should hear is: something changed. A job ended. A relationship changed. Money got tight. Grief landed. The cortisol rhythm shifted. Caffeine intake doubled. Exercise stopped. Screen time tripled at 9pm.

      The history is where the diagnosis lives. Not in polysomnography. Not in biomarkers. In the story.

      ## Structural approach: the timeline
      Start with the **when**. When did this start? Not "a few months ago"—push for specificity. Was it after a particular event? A season change? A medication change? This matters because the trajectory tells you whether you're dealing with adjustment insomnia (finite), chronic psychophysiological insomnia (reinforced by years of learned arousal), or insomnia secondary to a psychiatric or medical condition.

      ### Acute onset (days to weeks)
      
        - Think: acute stressor, new medication (SSRIs, stimulants, corticosteroids, pseudoephedrine), recent illness, pain, jet lag, shift work.
        - Prognosis: usually resolves when stressor resolves or medication changed. Most don't need pharmacotherapy if you address the driver.

      ### Subacute onset (weeks to months)
      
        - Think: sustained stress, unresolved grief, untreated anxiety, gradual caffeine creep, screen time habits, bedroom environment changes.
        - Risk: begins to shift from reactive to learned (brain starts predicting sleeplessness; anxiety about sleep onset develops; vicious cycle begins).

      ### Chronic onset (months to years)
      
        - Think: psychophysiological insomnia (the brain has learned to be awake in bed), chronic pain, sleep apnea undiagnosed, mood disorder, substance use/withdrawal cycles, conditioned hyperarousal.
        - Prognosis: requires active intervention; rarely resolves without structured approach.

      ## The pattern questions
      Don't ask "How are you sleeping?" Ask specifics:

      ### Onset: "When you go to bed, what happens?"
      
        - Do they fall asleep within 15–20 minutes? (Normal.)
        - Do they lie awake for 30, 60, 90 minutes? (Sleep-onset insomnia.)
        - Does this happen every night or episodically?
        - What's their mind doing during that time—racing thoughts, catastrophic thinking, physically tense, or just "can't shut off"?

      ### Maintenance: "Do you wake in the night?"
      
        - How often? How many times per night?
        - How long are they awake? (5 minutes vs 1 hour is a huge difference.)
        - Can they fall back asleep easily or do they ruminate?
        - What wakes them—noise, bathroom need, sweating, racing heart, just... awake?

      ### Early morning awakening: "What time do you wake?"
      
        - Waking 5–6am and unable to return to sleep is classic depression, especially if they feel worst in the morning.
        - Waking 3–4am and staying awake often suggests cortisol dysregulation or anxiety.
        - Waking to urinate is normal (nocturia), but frequency >2× per night warrants urinary assessment (UTI, diabetes, BPH, diuretics).

      ### Total sleep time: "How many hours are you actually sleeping?"
      
        - Many people in bed 8 hours but sleeping 5–6. This creates anxiety ("I'm only sleeping 5 hours!"), which reinforces insomnia.
        - Ask: total time asleep, not time in bed.

      ### Daytime function: "How does it affect you?"
      
        - Fatigue vs cognitive fog vs mood changes are different. Daytime sleepiness during the day (actually falling asleep) is different from fatigue.
        - If genuinely sleeping well but daytime tired → think sleep apnea, narcolepsy, depression, anemia, not insomnia.

      ## The driver assessment
      Once you have the pattern, now dig into what's driving it:

      ### Environmental
      
        - Bedroom temp? (Optimal ~16–18°C. Most NZ bedrooms are too warm.)
        - Light? (Blackout blinds? Phone light exposure?)
        - Noise? (Partner snoring, traffic, restless sleeper in bed?)
        - Comfort? (Mattress age? Pillows?)

      ### Behavioural
      
        - Screen time in evening? (Blue light delays melatonin ~2 hours; can last if scrolling in bed.)
        - Caffeine? (Half-life ~5 hours; afternoon coffee affects 11pm bedtime.)
        - Alcohol? (Sedating initially, disrupts REM sleep & causes fragmentation; rebound wakefulness at 3–4am.)
        - Exercise timing? (Too late = stimulating; too early/none = no sleep pressure.)
        - Eating patterns? (Full stomach or hungry both disrupt. Late heavy meals worst.)
        - Bed use? (Is bed for sleep/sex only, or for work, eating, scrolling? If the latter, bed = arousal context.)

      ### Medical
      
        - Pain? (Chronic pain is a major insomnia driver. Untreated pain + sleep deprivation = escalating cycle.)
        - Respiratory? (Sleep apnea, GORD symptoms at night, asthma.)
        - Neuroendocrine? (Perimenopause/menopause with hot flushes, thyroid dysfunction, cortisol dysregulation.)
        - Neurological? (Restless legs, REM behavior disorder, periodic limb movements.)
        - Medications? (Check: SSRIs in early phase, stimulants, corticosteroids, beta-blockers [paradoxical], certain antihistamines, decongestants.)

      ### Psychological
      
        - Anxiety disorder? (Racing thoughts, catastrophic thinking, physical tension.)
        - Depression? (Early morning waking, unrefreshed despite sleep, anhedonia, guilt, hopelessness.)
        - Trauma? (Hypervigilance, nightmares, dissociation, fragmented sleep.)
        - Substance use/withdrawal? (Alcohol, cannabis withdrawal cause rebound insomnia for weeks.)

      ### Social/contextual
      
        - Work stress? (Rumination about work at bedtime.)
        - Relationship issues? (Shared bed becoming conflict zone? Partner's snoring/restlessness?)
        - Financial worry?
        - Recent loss or grief?

        **Red flag:** Insomnia that appeared suddenly + unexplained daytime somnolence + witnessed apneas or gasping = sleep apnea, not primary insomnia. Refer.

      ## The crucial conversation about expectations
      Here's where most GPs miss the boat. Most people expect to fall asleep instantly and sleep through 8 uninterrupted hours. This is not normal. Normal sleep is biphasic or fragmented—brief awakenings are expected, especially with age. A 60-year-old waking twice per night is not necessarily broken; they might just be noticing normal physiology for the first time.

      Ask: "What would be 'enough' sleep for you?" Often it's unrealistic. Reframe: 6–7 hours consistently is excellent; 5 quality hours beats 8 fragmented hours; brief awakenings are expected and don't necessarily disrupt sleep quality.

      ## The assessment you document
      
        **Onset:** Acute/subacute/chronic; what triggered it
        **Pattern:** Sleep onset vs maintenance vs early morning; frequency; total sleep time
        **Daytime impact:** Functional impairment; fatigue vs somnolence; mood changes
        **Drivers identified:** Environmental, behavioural, medical, psychological, social
        **Red flags excluded:** Sleep apnea signs, narcolepsy features, bipolar (insomnia without next-day tiredness), psychosis
        **Insomnia subtype:** Adjustment/psychophysiological/secondary to [condition]

      ## Why this matters
      Because insomnia treated as a neurochemical problem when it's actually a grief problem will fail. Insomnia treated with sedatives when it's actually sleep apnea will make it worse. Insomnia treated with CBT when there's untreated depression will be frustrating. The history isn't a luxury—it's the foundation. Everything else is pharmacology. The history is medicine.
