#### Chronic pain definition
        Pain persisting beyond expected healing time, typically defined as greater than 3 months. Affects 20 percent of the global population; major public health burden.

        #### Three main pain types
        
          **Nociceptive:** Tissue damage activates pain receptors (nociceptors). Examples: osteoarthritis, post-surgical pain, musculoskeletal injury. Usually responds to NSAIDs, paracetamol, physical therapy.
          **Neuropathic:** Damage or dysfunction of peripheral or central nervous system. Examples: diabetic neuropathy, post-herpetic neuralgia, nerve injury pain. Requires pregabalin, gabapentin, SNRIs, tricyclics.
          **Nociplastic (central sensitization):** Amplified pain processing in the CNS; pain disproportionate to tissue damage. Examples: fibromyalgia, IBS, tension headache, some musculoskeletal conditions. Benefits from multimodal approach: pain neuroscience education, exercise, CBT, pregabalin.
        
        #### Biopsychosocial model
        Chronic pain is not purely biomedical; psychological (depression, anxiety, catastrophizing) and social factors (social support, work, stress) profoundly influence pain experience and disability.

      🧠Central sensitization and neuroplasticity▾
      
        #### Central sensitization
        Enhanced responsiveness of nociceptive neurons in the CNS; increased excitability of spinal dorsal horn and brain. Results in amplified pain perception, temporal summation (wind-up), and reduced pain threshold.

        #### Mechanisms
        
          - Deficient descending inhibitory pathways (reduced serotonin, noradrenaline, dopamine).
          - Elevated excitatory neurotransmitters (glutamate, substance P).
          - Neuroinflammation: microglial activation, pro-inflammatory cytokines.
          - Reorganization of cortical sensory maps.
        
        #### Triggers and perpetuating factors
        
          - Initial injury, infection, surgery; but pain persists beyond tissue healing.
          - Repetitive strain, poor posture, immobility, psychological stress, poor sleep, maladaptive coping (fear-avoidance).

      💊Pharmacological management▾
      
        #### First-line agents
        
          **Paracetamol:** 500 mg three to four times daily; limited efficacy; hepatotoxicity risk at high doses.
          **NSAIDs:** Ibuprofen 200-400 mg three times daily, naproxen 250-500 mg twice daily; effective for inflammatory pain; GI, renal, cardiovascular risks with long-term use.
          **Pregabalin, gabapentin:** For neuropathic and nociplastic pain. Pregabalin 75-600 mg/day (divided dosing); gabapentin 900-3600 mg/day. Reduce renal impairment.
          **SNRIs (duloxetine, venlafaxine):** Increase noradrenaline and serotonin; effective for neuropathic and musculoskeletal pain. Duloxetine 30-120 mg/day.
          **Tricyclic antidepressants (amitriptyline, nortriptyline):** Noradrenaline-serotonin reuptake inhibition; also analgesic effect independent of mood. Amitriptyline 10-100 mg at bedtime; sedating.
        
        #### Second-line agents
        
          **Topical agents:** Capsaicin cream, lidocaine patches for localized neuropathic pain.
          **Muscle relaxants:** Baclofen, tizanidine; limited evidence; use cautiously.
          **Injections:** Local corticosteroid injections (joint, bursa, nerve) for inflammatory joint pain, facet joint pain.
        
        #### Opioids: conservative approach
        
          **Limited evidence:** Poor long-term outcomes for non-cancer chronic pain; risk of dependence, tolerance, hyperalgesia, respiratory depression, overdose.
          **Indication:** Short-term acute pain or cancer pain; last-line for severe non-cancer pain if all other options exhausted.
          **Monitoring:** If prescribed: baseline opioid risk assessment (OARRS, SOAPP), regular review (3-6 monthly), urine drug screening, concurrent psychological support, naloxone co-prescription for high doses.

      🏃Non-pharmacological approaches▾
      
        #### Exercise and physiotherapy
        
          - Graded exercise therapy, aerobic exercise, resistance training, yoga, Pilates.
          - Improves pain, function, mood; low risk if tailored to capacity.
        
        #### Psychological interventions
        
          **Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT):** Address catastrophizing, fear-avoidance, unhelpful thought patterns; teaches pain coping strategies.
          **Mindfulness-based stress reduction (MBSR):** Meditation, body scan; reduces pain intensity and emotional distress.
          **Acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT):** Accept pain as present; commit to valued activities despite pain.
        
        #### Other modalities
        
          - Acupuncture (modest evidence), manual therapy, massage, heat/cold therapy, TENS (low-quality evidence).
          - Sleep optimization, stress management, pacing (activity planning to avoid boom-bust cycles).

      ❓Common questions▾
      
        #### Is chronic pain "all in my head"?
        No. Pain is always real, even if it originates from changes in the nervous system (central sensitization) rather than tissue damage. The brain and nervous system are physical; altered pain processing is a biological phenomenon.

        #### Can exercise make chronic pain worse?
        If done too aggressively or without pacing, yes. Gradual, graded exercise tailored to current capacity is beneficial. Fear of movement can worsen outcomes.

        #### Do I need to take opioids forever?
        No. Opioids are not recommended for long-term non-cancer pain management. Multimodal approaches (exercise, psychology, non-opioid medications) offer better long-term outcomes and lower risk.

        #### Will my pain ever go away?
        Chronic pain may not fully resolve, but it can improve significantly with appropriate treatment. Goal is not necessarily elimination, but reduction in intensity and improved quality of life and function.
