          - Bilateral leg pain/sciatica.
          - Saddle anaesthesia (perineal numbness).
          - Urinary retention or incontinence.
          - Faecal incontinence.
          - Progressive bilateral leg weakness.
          **Action:** EMERGENCY. MRI urgent (same-day). Contact neurosurgery. Do not delay.

      🔍Mechanical vs neuropathic pain▾
      
        #### Mechanical back pain (95% of cases)
        
          **Features:** Ache in lower back, varies with movement/posture, no radiation below knee (usually).
          **Causes:** Muscle strain, facet joint pain, degenerative disc disease, discogenic pain. Rarely serious structural cause.
          **Examination:** Limited range of motion, possible focal tenderness, normal neurology.
          **Imaging:** Usually not needed; does not change management in absence of red flags.
        
        #### Radiculopathy (sciatica)
        
          **Features:** Pain radiates below knee, dermatomal distribution, pins-and-needles, numbness, weakness in same distribution.
          **Causes:** Nerve root compression (disc herniation, foraminal stenosis ~90%), not muscle pain.
          **Examination:** Positive nerve tension tests (straight leg raise), dermatomal sensory/motor loss, reduced reflexes (L5-S1 relevant).
          **Imaging:** Consider MRI if symptoms persist >6 weeks or surgery being contemplated.
        
        #### Spinal stenosis
        
          **Features:** Bilateral leg pain/claudication, worse with walking/extension, improves with flexion (walking uphill OK, downhill not).
          **Examination:** May have minimal findings; neurogenic claudication on history.
          **Imaging:** MRI confirms diagnosis; CT if MRI contraindicated.

      📊Imaging decisions▾
      
        #### When NOT to image (non-specific mechanical pain)
        
          Uncomplicated back pain 
          - Plain X-rays show degenerative changes that do not correlate with symptoms; often misleading.
          - Imaging does not improve outcomes for mechanical pain; increases costs and anxiety.
        
        #### When to consider imaging
        
          **Red flag features present:** MRI (preferred) or CT (if MRI contraindicated).
          **Persistent radiculopathy >6 weeks:** MRI to assess degree of nerve compression and inform surgical decision.
          **Spinal stenosis suspected:** MRI confirms diagnosis and severity; helps guide referral.
          **Trauma or osteoporosis:** X-rays (look for fracture); CT if plain films inconclusive.
        
        #### Advanced imaging (MRI)
        
          - Superior for soft tissue (disc, nerve, spinal cord) visualisation.
          - Not emergency unless cauda equina suspected.
          - Wait lists variable; private option faster in NZ.

      💊Treatment approach▾
      
        Acute mechanical pain (
        
          **Reassurance:** Majority (80%) recover within 4 weeks.
          **Stay active:** Modified activity better than bed rest. Walking, gentle movement encouraged.
          **Analgesia:** Paracetamol 500mg TDS or ibuprofen 400mg TDS (for 1-2 weeks max). NSAIDs no more effective than paracetamol long-term.
          **Muscle relaxants:** Baclofen, tizanidine offer modest benefit; sedating (avoid prolonged use).
          **Physiotherapy:** Early referral (week 1-2) for exercise programme; superior to no intervention.
          **Heat/ice:** Patient preference; modest short-term relief.
        
        #### Chronic pain (>12 weeks)
        
          **Multidisciplinary approach:** Physiotherapy, occupational therapy, psychology (cognitive behavioural therapy).
          **Exercise:** Core stability, flexibility. Group classes (Pilates, yoga) effective and cost-effective.
          **Medications:** Avoid opioids long-term (no evidence of benefit, high dependence risk). Consider pregabalin if neuropathic component; tricyclic antidepressants (amitriptyline 10-50mg nocte) for pain modulation.
          **Workplace modifications:** Ergonomic assessment, activity pacing.
          **Psychological support:** CBT reduces pain and disability in chronic pain; addresses catastrophising and fear avoidance.
        
        #### Radiculopathy
        
          **Conservative first:** NSAIDs, physiotherapy, activity modification. 80-90% improve without surgery.
          **Epidural steroid injection:** May provide relief (median duration 3-6 weeks); consider if conservative fails and patient reluctant to consider surgery.
          **Surgery (microdiscectomy):** Indicated if severe/progressive neurological deficit or failure of conservative care >6-12 weeks. Fast symptom relief but long-term outcomes similar to conservative care.

      📋When to refer▾
      
        #### Urgent referral (same-day or next-day)
        
          - Cauda equina syndrome (neurosurgery emergency).
          - Spinal cord compression with progressive neurological deficit.
        
        #### Routine referral to physiotherapy
        
          - Most patients benefit from early physiotherapy (within 2-3 weeks of onset).
          - Public wait lists variable (4-12 weeks in many DHBs); private option faster.
        
        #### Routine referral to spinal surgeon/neurosurgeon
        
          - Persistent radiculopathy >6-12 weeks despite conservative care and patient considering surgery.
          - Severe neurological deficit (drop foot, progressive weakness).
          - Red flag features confirmed on imaging (infection, cancer, etc.).
        
        #### Referral to pain specialist/rheumatology
        
          - Chronic pain not responding to standard treatment.
          - Suspected inflammatory condition (ankylosing spondylitis, reactive arthritis).

      ❓Common questions▾
      
        #### Do I need an X-ray or MRI for back pain?
        Not usually. For uncomplicated mechanical back pain without red flags, imaging does not improve outcomes and often shows normal age-related changes that worry patients unnecessarily. MRI is helpful if red flags present, severe radiculopathy, or considering surgery.

        #### Is bed rest good for back pain?
        No. Prolonged bed rest delays recovery and increases disability risk. Staying active (within comfort limits) speeds recovery. Early movement and physiotherapy are evidence-based.

        #### When should I worry about cauda equina?
        Seek immediate medical attention if you develop saddle numbness (perineal area), loss of bladder/bowel control, or rapidly progressive leg weakness. These are spinal emergencies requiring urgent MRI and possible surgery.

        #### Should I take opioids for chronic back pain?
        Generally not recommended long-term. Evidence shows opioids no more effective than other painkillers for chronic non-cancer pain, and carry high risk of dependence, tolerance and overdose. Non-opioid approaches (exercise, physiotherapy, psychological support) have better long-term outcomes.
