## Overview


**Strep throat is acute pharyngitis caused by Group A Streptococcus (GAS, Streptococcus pyogenes).** Gram-positive, β-haemolytic coccus; accounts for 5–15% of sore throats in primary care, higher in children. Mostly self-limiting but treatment recommended to reduce complications (suppurative: abscess, mastoiditis; non-suppurative: rheumatic fever, post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis).

        #### Epidemiology
        
          **Peak age:** 5–15 years; rare 45 years
          **Seasonal pattern:** Winter and early spring (temperate climates)
          **Transmission:** Airborne droplets; symptomatic or asymptomatic carriers; contagious 24–48 hours after antibiotics start, or until fever subsides if untreated
          **Incubation:** 1–3 days; symptoms peak day 3–4 then gradually resolve (1–2 weeks untreated)
          **NZ context:** GAS remains common; rheumatic fever burden higher in Māori and Pacific populations; regionally variable disease patterns (South Auckland, Bay of Plenty endemic)
        
        #### Risk factors for severe disease
        
          Age 45 years (severe complications, invasive disease risk)
          **Immunosuppression** (HIV, organ transplant, chronic corticosteroids)
          **Acute leukaemia, chemotherapy** (invasive GAS risk)
          **Varicella infection** (concurrent or recent; markedly ↑ invasive GAS, toxic shock syndrome risk)
          **Chronic kidney disease, diabetes** (post-streptococcal GN risk)

      🔍Clinical presentation & diagnosis▾
      
        #### Symptoms
        
          **Sore throat:** Sudden onset; moderate-to-severe; localised pharyngeal pain; odynophagia
          **Fever:** 38–40°C; often high; onset same day or day after sore throat
          **Headache, malaise, myalgia** (non-specific; often severe)
          **Nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain** (more common in children)
          **Absence of cough, coryza, hoarseness** (helps distinguish from viral URI)
          **Duration:** 3–5 days untreated; symptoms decline over 1–2 weeks
        
        #### Physical examination
        
          **Pharyngitis:** Red pharynx, exudate (white/yellow, non-membranous; membrane differentiates diphtheria), petechiae on soft palate, posterior pharyngeal wall
          **Tonsillitis:** Enlarged, erythematous, sometimes with exudate
          **Anterior cervical lymphadenopathy:** Tender, sometimes suppurative (→ abscess)
          **Scarlet fever rash:** Finely textured, sandpaper-like, erythematous; onset chest day 1–2, spreads to trunk/proximal limbs; spares palms/soles; Pastia's lines (linear darkening in skin folds). May desquamate in weeks 2–3.
          **Absence of rash common;** rash indicates systemic inflammatory response
        
        #### Clinical scoring: Centor & FeverPAIN criteria
        **Centor (4 points; predict GAS likelihood):** Fever (1), exudate (1), anterior cervical lymphadenopathy (1), absence of cough (1). Score ≥3 = ~50% GAS; ≥2 in children ~25%; 

      🧪Diagnosis: rapid antigen and throat culture▾
      
        #### Rapid antigen test (RAT)
        
          **Method:** Throat swab (tonsillar/pharyngeal exudate sampled); immunoassay detects GAS carbohydrate antigen in 5–15 minutes
          **Sensitivity:** 85–95% in symptomatic pharyngitis (varies by test kit, operator, swab technique; lower in low-pretest-probability)
          **Specificity:** ~95–99% (false positives rare)
          **Positive RAT = treat; negative RAT in high-probability case (Centor ≥3, scarlet fever) = follow-up with culture**
          **Cost:** ~NZ$10–30 per test; widely available in primary care, ED
        
        #### Throat culture
        
          **Method:** Sheep blood agar plate; incubate 24–48 hours; β-haemolytic colony ID'd as GAS by latex agglutination or MALDI-TOF
          **Gold standard;** ~95% sensitivity if proper technique (swab posterior pharynx, avoid saliva, transport in appropriate media)
          **Slower (2–3 days);** mainly used to confirm negative RAT or identify carriers vs active infection
          **Used in NZ for:** Back-up to negative RAT, complicated cases, post-treatment follow-up (if concerns about relapse/carrier state)
        
        #### Recommended testing strategy (BPAC NZ, NICE)
        
          Low probability (Centor  No test; symptomatic care (paracetamol, ibuprofen, fluids); educate on red flags
          **Intermediate probability (Centor 2–3, FeverPAIN 3–4):** Rapid antigen test; if positive, treat; if negative, symptomatic care (no culture needed unless high suspicion)
          **High probability (Centor ≥3, FeverPAIN ≥4, scarlet fever rash):** Rapid antigen test; if positive, treat; if negative, consider culture (especially if high-risk patient or contacts)
          **Recurrent/severe:** Culture if relapse; identify persistent GAS vs reinfection; assess for carrier state
        
        #### Special situations
        
          **Post-treatment culture:** NOT recommended routinely (may identify asymptomatic carrier; antibiotics reduce but do not eliminate carriage); only if symptoms persist or recur within weeks
          **Asymptomatic GAS carrier:** No symptoms; GAS on throat culture. Does not require treatment (does not prevent rheumatic fever, does not predict future disease); exclude carrier only if recurrent pyoderma in household or athlete/contact sport setting

      💊Antibiotic treatment & timing▾
      
        #### First-line antibiotics (NZ, BPAC)
        
          DrugDoseDurationNotes
          
            **Penicillin V (phenoxymethylpenicillin)**500 mg QID (children: 125–250 mg QID)10 daysFirst-line; cheap; effective (GAS resistance rare globally); must take 4×/day (adherence issue)
            **Amoxicillin**500 mg TDS (children: 15 mg/kg TDS)10 daysAlternative; better taste than penicillin V; only 3×/day (better adherence); ~20% cost premium
            **Cephalexin**500 mg QID (children: 12.5 mg/kg QID)10 daysIf penicillin allergy (non-anaphylaxis); cross-reactivity ~2%; safe in mild-moderate allergy
            **Azithromycin (macrolide)**500 mg day 1, then 250 mg OD (children: 12 mg/kg day 1, then 6 mg/kg OD)5 daysIf true IgE-mediated penicillin allergy; increasing GAS resistance (20–30% in some regions); less effective for rheumatic fever prevention; use cautiously

        #### When to treat
        
          **Confirmed GAS (RAT positive, culture positive):** Treat all cases; reduces symptom duration by 1–2 days, fever by 12–24 hours, reduces complications (suppurative and non-suppurative)
          **High suspicion, no test available:** Consider empiric treatment (especially if scarlet fever, high Centor score, immunocompromised, recent varicella, elderly, symptoms severe)
          **Clinical judgment required:** Some GPs treat FeverPAIN ≥4 or Centor ≥3 empirically; others require test-confirmation
        
        #### Timing of antibiotics
        
          **Rheumatic fever prevention:** Treatment within 9 days of symptom onset prevents acute rheumatic fever (ARF). Even late treatment (days 9–10) has ~50% protective effect. Earlier treatment (day 1) not markedly superior to day 5–9 for ARF prevention, but reduces symptoms faster.
          **Suppurative complications:** Treatment within first 3–5 days reduces abscess risk; delay increases risk but does not eliminate it
          **Practical: test and treat without delay** if GAS confirmed; empiric treatment reasonable in high-probability cases
        
        #### Treatment failure & relapse
        
          **Persistent symptoms after 48–72 hours:** Assess adherence, consider alternative diagnosis (viral URI superimposed, infectious mononucleosis, epiglottitis); if treating, continue 10-day course
          **Relapse within weeks:** Reinfection (new exposure) vs relapse (inadequate treatment or carrier state). Repeat RAT/culture; if positive, retreat with same or different antibiotic class
          **Carrier state:** Asymptomatic GAS on culture after treatment. No need to re-treat unless recurrent pyoderma, contact sports, or healthcare worker. Carriers are not at risk of rheumatic fever.

      ⚠️Suppurative complications▾
      
        #### Peritonsillar abscess
        
          **Incidence:** 1 in 2000–10000 GAS pharyngitis (rare with early antibiotic treatment)
          **Presentation:** Severe unilateral throat pain, trismus (difficulty opening mouth), dysphagia to saliva, "hot potato" voice, fever, halitosis; deviation of uvula, bulge in lateral pharyngeal wall
          **Diagnosis:** Clinical (intraoral swelling) or CT/ultrasound (if diagnosis unclear)
          **Treatment:** IV antibiotics (ampicillin-sulbactam or ceftriaxone), analgesia; aspiration or incision & drainage if abscess >2 cm; possible tonsillectomy (elective, after acute phase resolves)
        
        #### Retropharyngeal abscess
        
          **Rare, serious;** GAS or mixed flora in retropharyngeal space
          **Presentation:** Severe pharyngitis with neck rigidity, dysphagia, stridor, respiratory compromise, fever, neck swelling/bulge posterior to sternocleidomastoid; trismus
          **Risk:** Mediastinitis, airway obstruction, sepsis; high mortality if untreated
          **Diagnosis:** CT neck with contrast (confirms collection, airway patency)
          **Treatment:** URGENT; IV antibiotics (broad-spectrum), airway management, incision & drainage, possible ICU admission
        
        #### Epiglottitis
        
          **Life-threatening airway emergency;** GAS (less commonly Haemophilus influenzae type b with ↓ vaccination)
          **Presentation:** Severe pharyngitis, dysphagia, drooling, stridor, tripod posture, respiratory distress, fever; rapid progression hours
          **Diagnosis:** Clinical suspicion + lateral neck X-ray (thumbprint sign) or direct laryngoscopy (confirms red, swollen epiglottis); do NOT attempt airway examination if not trained (risk complete obstruction)
          **Treatment:** EMERGENCY; secure airway (intubation or surgical), IV antibiotics (ceftriaxone or ampicillin-sulbactam), admit ICU
        
        #### Acute mastoiditis
        
          **GAS or mixed flora; rare; complication of untreated otitis media or direct extension from pharyngitis**
          **Presentation:** Otalgia, drainage from ear, postauricular swelling/erythema, fever, conductive hearing loss; pain with mastoid tenderness
          **Diagnosis:** Clinical + CT temporal bone (osteolytic lesion in mastoid)
          **Treatment:** IV antibiotics (broad-spectrum), myringotomy/drainage if otitis media component, possible mastoidectomy if advanced
        
        #### Invasive GAS (post-varicella, immunocompromised)
        
          **Toxic shock syndrome (TSS):** Streptococcal pyrogenic exotoxin-mediated systemic inflammation; presents with high fever, rash, hypotension, multi-organ failure, rapid progression; mortality ~30%
          **Necrotising fasciitis:** Rapid tissue necrosis along fascial planes; severe pain, skin erythema → purple/black, systemic toxicity; mortality ~20–30%
          **Risk:** Varicella (skin barrier breakdown, toxin delivery), immunosuppression (malignancy, HIV, immunosuppressive therapy)
          **Treatment:** EMERGENCY; high-dose IV antibiotics (ceftriaxone, flucloxacillin, clindamycin), surgical debridement, ICU support, IVIG considered

      🫀Non-suppurative sequelae: rheumatic fever & glomerulonephritis▾
      
        #### Acute rheumatic fever (ARF)
        
          **Pathophysiology:** Molecular mimicry; antibodies to GAS M protein cross-react with cardiac myosin, tropomyosin, keratin → autoimmune inflammation of heart, joints, CNS, skin
          **Epidemiology:** Post-GAS pharyngitis (NOT pyoderma) latency 2–3 weeks. Incidence varies globally (~3–5 per 100,000 in high-income countries, much higher in lower-income/crowded settings). NZ rates ~2–3 per 100,000 nationally, but 10–20 per 100,000 in Māori and Pacific populations (health equity concern).
          **Risk factors:** Young age (5–15 years peak, rare >45), recurrent GAS infection, immunologic predisposition (genetic, socioeconomic deprivation, crowded housing, poor hygiene)
          **Jones Criteria (2015 revision):** Diagnosis requires either 2 major + 1 minor manifestation (or 3 minor) + evidence of preceding GAS infection (RAT, culture, elevated anti-streptococcal serology: anti-streptolysin-O, anti-DNase B)
            
              **Major:** Carditis (clinical or subclinical on echo), polyarthritis (or polyarthralgia + fever in low-risk populations), chorea, erythema marginatum, subcutaneous nodules
              **Minor:** Fever ≥38.5°C, polyarthralgia, elevated CRP/ESR, prolonged PR interval on ECG

          **Cardiac sequelae:** Acute carditis (endocarditis, myocarditis, pericarditis) → chronic rheumatic heart disease (mitral stenosis most common, aortic regurgitation, combined lesions). Risk of stroke (atrial fibrillation, mural thrombus), heart failure, sudden cardiac death. ~30–50% with carditis develop permanent valve damage.
          **Prevention:** Prompt treatment of GAS pharyngitis within 9 days prevents ARF in 90%. Secondary prophylaxis (monthly benzathine penicillin IM or daily oral penicillin V) prevents recurrence in patients with history of ARF or chronic rheumatic heart disease.
        
        #### Post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis (PSGN)
        
          **Pathophysiology:** Immune complex deposition (GAS antigen + antibody) in glomerular basement membrane; complement activation; primarily IgG-mediated
          **Epidemiology:** Latency 1–3 weeks post-pharyngitis (or 2–6 weeks post-pyoderma). Incidence ~1 in 100,000 in high-income countries. Peak age 3–7 years; male:female 2:1.
          **Presentation:** Gross or microscopic haematuria (cola/tea-coloured urine), proteinuria, hypertension, oedema (periorbital, lower limbs), malaise, oliguria in severe cases. Often asymptomatic on screening.
          **Diagnosis:** Urinalysis (haematuria + proteinuria + casts), elevated creatinine (mild), low complement C3 (recovers weeks to months), anti-streptococcal serology positive (ASO titre, anti-DNase B)
          **Prognosis:** Excellent in children; ~95% spontaneous complete recovery within 6–12 months. Adults have worse prognosis (10–15% progress to chronic kidney disease/ESRD). Severe cases may develop hypertensive crisis, pulmonary oedema, acute kidney injury.
          **Management:** Supportive (fluid restriction, diuretics if oedema, antihypertensives), treat infection (antibiotics); corticosteroids if rapidly progressive; antibiotic treatment does NOT prevent PSGN (immune-mediated, not directly prevented by killing bacteria).

      🇳🇿NZ prescribing & management pathways▾
      
        #### BPAC NZ guidance
        
          **Rapid antigen testing recommended** for sore throat in primary care; FeverPAIN or Centor score guides need for testing
          **Penicillin V (PHARMAC-funded) first-line;** amoxicillin preferred if adherence concern (TDS vs QID dosing)
          **10-day course essential** for rheumatic fever prevention (shorter courses not recommended)
          **No repeat culture after treatment** unless symptoms persist or recur; assess adherence, consider alternative diagnosis
        
        #### Funding & access (NZ)
        
          **Rapid antigen tests:** Available in primary care, ED, DHB urgent care; cost varies by provider (~$10–30)
          **Penicillin V, amoxicillin:** PHARMAC-funded; generic, cheap (~NZ$2–5 per course)
          **Azithromycin:** PHARMAC-funded if penicillin allergy documented
          **Specialist referral:** Recurrent pharyngitis, complicated cases (abscess, severe infection), immunocompromised, post-varicella invasive GAS
        
        #### Public health notification (NZ)
        
          **Acute rheumatic fever is notifiable** (Health Act 1956); all cases reported to Ministry of Health and regional PHU
          **Group A Streptococcal infection (invasive) is notifiable** if bacteraemia/invasive disease suspected
          **Routine GAS pharyngitis not notifiable** unless part of outbreak (school, childcare setting)
        
        #### Infection control & return to work/school
        
          **Contagious until:** 24–48 hours after first antibiotic dose, or fever resolves if untreated; then safe for return
          **School/childcare:** Return after 24 hours of antibiotics (or after fever resolves if no antibiotics given)
          **Healthcare workers:** Standard precautions; return after 24 hours of treatment (if symptomatic treatment)

      💡Clinical pearls & pitfalls▾
      
        #### Pearls
        
          ✦**Absence of cough favours GAS.** Viral URIs often have cough; strep pharyngitis typically spares respiratory tract. Use clinically to guide suspicion.
          ✦**Rapid antigen test enables same-day treatment.** Symptomatic benefit (1–2 day faster recovery) modest, but rheumatic fever prevention crucial. Test/treat streamlines care.
          ✦**Rheumatic fever is preventable.** Treating GAS within 9 days prevents ARF ~90%. NZ rates (especially in Māori/Pacific) unacceptably high; low threshold for testing + treatment in young patients.
          ✦**Post-varicella GAS is dangerous.** Skin barrier broken; toxin penetrance ↑; risk of TSS, necrotising fasciitis. Heightened vigilance if GAS symptoms + recent varicella or varicella vaccination (rare live-vaccine strain).
          ✦**Asymptomatic carriers need not be treated.** GAS on culture but asymptomatic; no risk of rheumatic fever; no need to re-treat (exception: recurrent pyoderma in contacts).
          ✦**Non-adherence or late treatment still helps.** Treatment day 9 after onset reduces ARF risk ~50%; imperfect but significant. Treat even if several days into illness.
        
        #### Pitfalls
        
          **Treating viral pharyngitis empirically.** ~85% of acute pharyngitis is viral; empiric antibiotics → resistance, adverse effects, cost. Test first (RAT/clinical score) or limit empiric therapy to high-risk, high-probability presentations.
          **Treating asymptomatic carrier.** Positive culture but no symptoms = carrier state; no need to re-treat. Wastes antibiotics; disrupts microbiome.
          **Premature cessation of antibiotics.** Shorter courses (
          **Repeat culture after treatment.** Asymptomatic carriers common (10–30% after treatment); repeat culture without symptoms is unnecessary and leads to inappropriate re-treatment.
          **Missing complications.** Peritonsillar abscess, retropharyngeal abscess, epiglottitis, post-varicella invasive GAS—maintain high index of suspicion if symptoms worsen despite antibiotics or red flags emerge (stridor, neck rigidity, airway concerns).
          **Overlooking rheumatic fever risk in Māori/Pacific patients.** Higher disease burden; lower threshold for testing, treatment, and secondary prophylaxis.
