## Overview


Both present with discharge and vulvovaginal symptoms, but they are biologically opposite: BV is a **bacterial dysbiosis** (too few lactobacilli, raised pH), while thrush is a **fungal overgrowth** (normal pH, intense itch). Neither is classed as a sexually transmitted infection, but both are common and both are over-the-counter self-treated - often wrongly.

          Classic isolated pictures are the exception.       🦠Bacterial vaginosis▾
      
        #### What it is
        **Bacterial vaginosis (BV)** is a polymicrobial dysbiosis: protective hydrogen-peroxide-producing lactobacilli are replaced by a mixed anaerobic flora (Gardnerella vaginalis, Prevotella, Atopobium vaginae, Mobiluncus). The vaginal pH rises and volatile amines are produced - the source of the characteristic odour. It is not an STI, but it is associated with sexual activity, new or multiple partners, douching and the loss of lactobacilli.

        #### Presentation
        
          - Thin, homogeneous, grey-white discharge that coats the vaginal walls
          **Fishy odour**, classically worse after intercourse and after menses (alkaline triggers)
          - Little or no itch or inflammation - irritation suggests something else or co-infection
          - Up to half of affected people are asymptomatic

        #### Diagnosis (Amsel criteria - 3 of 4)
        
          - Thin, homogeneous discharge
          **Clue cells** on saline microscopy (epithelial cells studded with bacteria)
          - Vaginal pH > 4.5
          - Positive whiff (amine) test - fishy odour on adding potassium hydroxide
        
        Gram-stain Nugent scoring is the laboratory gold standard; in practice the diagnosis is usually clinical with a high vaginal swab where needed.

        #### Treatment
        
          **Oral metronidazole 400 mg twice daily for 7 days** - first-line (a 2 g single dose is less effective and more relapse-prone)
          **Intravaginal metronidazole gel** or **clindamycin cream** - equally effective alternatives, useful if oral not tolerated
          Counsel to **avoid alcohol** with metronidazole (disulfiram-like reaction); clindamycin cream weakens latex condoms
          - Treat asymptomatic BV before termination of pregnancy or some gynae procedures; otherwise treat symptomatic disease
          - Routine partner treatment is not recommended (though emerging evidence in male partners is being studied)

        #### Why it matters
        
          - Recurrence is common (over half within a year)
          - Associated with increased acquisition of STIs and HIV, pelvic inflammatory disease, post-procedure infection
          - In pregnancy, associated with preterm birth and late miscarriage - treat symptomatic disease

      🍞Vulvovaginal candidiasis (thrush)▾
      
        #### What it is
        **Thrush** is symptomatic overgrowth of *Candida* - around 90% *C. albicans*, the remainder non-albicans species (notably *C. glabrata*) that matter in recurrent or resistant disease. Candida is a normal commensal in many people; it is the symptomatic overgrowth, not mere presence, that defines disease. It is not an STI.

        #### Presentation
        
          **Vulval itch and soreness** - the dominant feature
          - Thick, white, non-odorous "cottage cheese" discharge (can be scant or absent)
          - Vulval erythema, oedema, fissuring, satellite lesions
          - Superficial dyspareunia and external (splash) dysuria

        #### Risk factors
        
          - Recent antibiotics, pregnancy, high oestrogen states
          - Poorly controlled diabetes; immunosuppression
          - Local irritants, occlusive clothing

        #### Diagnosis
        
          - Largely clinical; vaginal pH is normal (≤ 4.5)
          Microscopy shows spores/pseudohyphae; **culture (HVS)** if recurrent, atypical, or treatment-resistant - to confirm species and guide therapy
          - Check glucose / consider diabetes in recurrent disease

        #### Treatment
        
          **Topical azole** - clotrimazole pessary/cream - or **oral fluconazole 150 mg single dose**; comparable efficacy, patient preference guides choice
          Topical therapy is preferred in **pregnancy** (avoid oral fluconazole); a longer course may be needed
          **Non-albicans (e.g. C. glabrata):** often azole-resistant - longer topical azole, nystatin or intravaginal boric acid under specialist guidance
          - Treat the vulva and vagina; emollient/soap-substitute skin care helps
          - Partner treatment not required unless the partner is symptomatic

          Recurrent thrush (≥ 4 episodes/year)
          Confirm the diagnosis by culture, exclude diabetes and identify non-albicans species. Manage with an induction-then-maintenance regimen (e.g. fluconazole induction over a week or two followed by weekly suppression for several months), tapering as tolerated.

      🔬The work-up: pH, microscopy and when to swab▾
      
        #### A pragmatic primary-care approach
        
          **History does a lot of the work:** itch-dominant and odourless points to thrush; odour-dominant with little itch points to BV
          **Vaginal pH** is the single most useful bedside test - raised in BV (and trichomoniasis), normal in thrush
          **Examine** the vulva and vagina (speculum) where the picture is unclear, recurrent or not responding

        #### When to take swabs
        
          - Diagnostic uncertainty, recurrent or persistent symptoms, treatment failure
          - Pregnancy, postpartum, post-instrumentation, or immunocompromise
          **High vaginal swab (HVS):** Candida culture/species, BV (Gram stain), trichomonas
          **Consider STI testing** (vulvovaginal/endocervical NAAT for chlamydia and gonorrhoea) if risk factors, pelvic pain, intermenstrual/postcoital bleeding, or cervicitis

          Don't anchor on "discharge = thrush"
          Self-diagnosis of thrush is unreliable - many who buy over-the-counter antifungals actually have BV, trichomoniasis or another cause. If empirical treatment fails, stop, examine and swab rather than repeating it.

      🤰Pregnancy, recurrence and other practical points▾
      
        #### Pregnancy
        
          **BV:** treat symptomatic disease; oral or vaginal metronidazole is used in pregnancy (follow local guidance)
          **Thrush:** use **topical** azoles; avoid oral fluconazole in pregnancy; a longer course is often needed

        #### Recurrent disease
        
          **Recurrent BV:** consider extended or suppressive metronidazole regimens; address modifiable factors (douching, smoking); lactobacillus/probiotic evidence is mixed
          **Recurrent thrush:** culture to confirm and speciate, exclude diabetes, then induction-maintenance suppression

        #### Self-care that genuinely helps
        
          - Avoid douching, vaginal washes and harsh soaps - they disrupt the flora and worsen BV
          - Use a soap substitute / emollient for vulval skin in thrush
          - Avoid unnecessary antibiotics, which precipitate thrush

      ⚠️Red flags and when to refer▾
      
        #### Reconsider the diagnosis if…
        
          - Symptoms persist or recur despite appropriate treatment
          Frothy yellow-green discharge or strawberry cervix - think **trichomoniasis** (an STI)
          Pelvic/lower abdominal pain, deep dyspareunia, fever - exclude **PID**
          - Intermenstrual or postcoital bleeding - assess the cervix
          Vulval pain, whitening, scarring or a non-resolving lesion - consider **dermatoses** (lichen sclerosus/planus) or neoplasia; biopsy/refer
          Postmenopausal dryness/irritation - consider **atrophic vaginitis**
          - A retained tampon or foreign body (offensive discharge) - examine and remove

        #### Refer / escalate
        
          - Recurrent or resistant thrush, especially non-albicans species
          - Diagnostic uncertainty, suspected dermatosis or malignancy
          - Recurrent BV refractory to standard therapy
          - Sexual-health review where STI is suspected or risk is high
