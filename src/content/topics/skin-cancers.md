#### Why is NZ at such high risk?
        Aotearoa sits at southern latitude (40–47°S) where the ozone layer is thinner. Combined with outdoor lifestyle, fair skin prevalence (Pākehā and European ancestry), and high reflectance from white buildings and beaches, NZ residents receive intense UV exposure year-round.

        #### Key melanoma risk factors
        
          **Phototype:** Fair skin (Fitzpatrick I–II) carries 10–20x higher risk than dark skin.
          **Sun exposure history:** Intermittent, intense exposure and sunburns in childhood are stronger risk than cumulative occupational exposure.
          **Family history:** 5–10% of melanomas are familial (CDKN2A mutations in ~30% of families).
          **Atypical naevi:** Having 5+ dysplastic naevi increases melanoma risk 10-fold.
          **Previous melanoma:** ~5–10% risk of second primary melanoma within 10 years.
          **Age:** Peak incidence 40–60 years, but can occur at any age.
          **Immunosuppression:** Transplant recipients, HIV, prolonged corticosteroids.

          Sun protection matters
          SPF 30–50+, UVA/UVB protection, reapply every 2 hours. Shade, clothing and hats reduce melanoma risk by 40–50% over a lifetime.

      🔍Melanoma: classification and staging▾
      
        #### ABCDE rule — when to refer
        
          **A — Asymmetry:** One half doesn't mirror the other.
          **B — Border irregularity:** Jagged, notched or poorly demarcated edge.
          **C — Colour variation:** Mix of brown, black, red, pink, white or blue.
          **D — Diameter:** >6mm (though small melanomas exist).
          **E — Evolving:** Change in size, shape, colour, or symptoms over weeks to months.
        
        #### Breslow thickness (most important prognostic factor)
        
          **<0.8mm (Stage I):** 90–95% 10-year survival.
          **0.8–1.0mm (Stage IB):** 85–90% 10-year survival.
          **1.0–2.0mm (Stage IIA):** 75–85% 10-year survival.
          **2.0–4.0mm (Stage IIB):** 65–75% 10-year survival.
          **>4.0mm (Stage IIC):** <60% 10-year survival; consider adjuvant therapy.
        
        #### Additional staging factors
        
          **Ulceration:** Presence downgrades stage by 1 level.
          **Mitotic rate:** >1 mitosis/mm² increases stage.
          **Clark level:** Depth in dermis (less used now, replaced by Breslow).
          **Sentinel lymph node (SLN) biopsy:** Indicated for thickness >0.8mm (some recommend from 0.5mm).
        
        #### Types of melanoma
        
          **Superficial spreading:** 70% of cases; variable colour, irregular borders, often large at presentation.
          **Nodular:** 15% of cases; darkly pigmented, vertical growth, often thicker at diagnosis.
          **Lentigo maligna:** 10% of cases; on sun-exposed face/neck in elderly; very slow growth, excellent prognosis.
          **Acral lentiginous:** 5% of cases; palms, soles, nail beds; poorer prognosis due to delayed detection.

      🔬Basal cell carcinoma (BCC)▾
      
        #### Overview
        Most common skin cancer. Arises from basal keratinocytes. Excellent prognosis; rarely metastasises (**Nodular BCC (most common):** Pearl-like papule, central ulcer ("rodent ulcer"), telangiectasia.
          **Superficial BCC:** Scaly, erythematous patch; often mistaken for dermatitis.
          **Infiltrative BCC:** Flesh-coloured, indurated; can be aggressive, requires wide excision.
          **Morpheaform:** Scar-like, poorly demarcated; high recurrence risk.
        
        #### Risk factors
        
          - Chronic sun exposure (cumulative, unlike melanoma).
          - Fair skin (Fitzpatrick I–II).
          - Age >40.
          - Immunosuppression (transplant patients: 100x higher risk).
          - Ionising radiation.
          - Genetic predisposition (nevoid basal cell carcinoma syndrome / Gorlin syndrome).
        
        #### Management
        
          **Excision:** Mohs micrographic surgery (MMS) offers highest cure rate (99%+), especially for high-risk tumours (infiltrative, morpheaform, recurrent).
          **Curettage and cautery:** 95–98% cure for small, primary, low-risk lesions.
          **Topical imiquimod:** For small superficial BCCs, though lower cure rates (~75–85%).
          **Radiotherapy:** For elderly or unfit patients; contraindicated in genetic predisposition syndromes.
        
        #### Follow-up
        Annual full-body skin check. Patients with BCC have 50% risk of second BCC within 5 years; sun protection counselling essential.

      🔬Squamous cell carcinoma (SCC)▾
      
        #### Overview
        Second most common skin cancer. Arises from epidermal keratinocytes. Risk of metastasis ~5–10% (much higher than BCC). Often on sun-exposed areas, lips, ears, hands.

        #### Clinical presentation
        
          **Well-differentiated:** Scaly, erythematous papule or nodule; keratotic surface.
          **Poorly differentiated:** Ulcerated, infiltrative; may present with regional lymphadenopathy.
          **SCC in situ (Bowen's disease):** Scaly, well-demarcated plaque; <5% progress to invasive SCC.
          **Keratoacanthoma:** Rapidly growing dome, central keratotic plug; can involute spontaneously but treated as SCC.
        
        #### Risk factors
        
          - Chronic sun exposure (cumulative).
          - Fair skin (Fitzpatrick I–II).
          - Age >60.
          - Immunosuppression (transplant recipients: 100-250x higher risk; HIV).
          - Chronic inflammation or wounds (Marjolin's ulcer — SCC arising in burn scars, chronic ulcers).
          - HPV infection (for anogenital SCC and head/neck SCC).
          - Arsenic exposure (occupational, old medications).
          - Xeroderma pigmentosum (inherited DNA repair disorder).
        
        #### Prognostic factors and risk stratification
        
          **Low risk:** Well-differentiated, <4mm thick, <2cm diameter, no poor features.
          **High risk:** Poor differentiation, >4mm thick, >2cm, on lip/ear/genitals, rapid growth, recurrent, perineural invasion.
          **Perineural invasion (PNI):** Associated with higher recurrence and metastatic risk.
        
        #### Management
        
          **Excision:** Standard; Mohs for high-risk or recurrent tumours.
          **Lymph node assessment:** For high-risk SCC, consider ultrasound and possible sentinel lymph node biopsy.
          **Radiotherapy:** For patients unfit for surgery or extensive disease.
          **5-Fluorouracil (5-FU):** Topical for SCC in situ; systemic for advanced/metastatic disease.
          **Cetuximab or other systemic therapy:** For unresectable, metastatic or recurrent SCC in selected cases.
        
        #### Follow-up
        6–12 monthly skin checks for 2 years, then annually. Higher-risk patients warrant regional lymph node imaging and more frequent surveillance.

      💊Melanoma: systemic therapy and follow-up▾
      
        #### Adjuvant therapy (for high-risk melanoma)
        
          **Stage III (nodal disease) or high-risk Stage IIB/IIC:** Consider adjuvant immunotherapy.
          **Checkpoint inhibitors (anti-PD-1):** Nivolumab or pembrolizumab improves recurrence-free survival in Stage III/IV.
          **BRAF inhibitor + MEK inhibitor:** For BRAF-mutant melanoma; offers quicker response than immunotherapy but inferior long-term outcomes.
          **Interferon-alpha:** Older agent; less commonly used now due to toxicity and inferior efficacy to checkpoint inhibitors.
        
        #### Metastatic melanoma
        
          **Immunotherapy (first-line):** Nivolumab or pembrolizumab; response rate ~40%, durable responses in ~20% of patients.
          **Combination: CTLA-4 + PD-1 inhibitors:** Ipilimumab + nivolumab; higher response (~50%) but more toxicity.
          **BRAF/MEK inhibition:** For BRAF V600E-mutant tumours; rapid response but shorter duration.
          **Radiotherapy:** For brain metastases, oligometastatic disease.
          **Surgery:** For resectable isolated recurrence or oligometastatic disease.
        
        #### Surveillance post-treatment
        
          **Stages IA/IB:** Clinical exam every 6–12 months for 5 years, then annually.
          **Stage II:** Every 3–6 months for 2 years, then 6–12 monthly for 3 years.
          **Stage III:** Every 1–3 months for 2 years, then 6–12 monthly; consider imaging (CT/PET) annually or if symptoms.
          **Self-monitoring:** Patient education on ABCDE; any new or changing lesion warrants urgent assessment.

      🌏NZ vs international management▾
      
        #### New Zealand screening and public health approach
        
          **No national melanoma screening programme:** Unlike Australia's SunSmart campaign and opportunistic screening.
          **Skin cancer clinics:** Available in major centres; referral via GP.
          **Dermatology access:** Limited by waiting lists; public system can take 6–12 weeks.
          **Private sector:** Faster access but user-pays; ~NZ$200–500 for initial assessment.
          **Mohs surgery:** Limited availability; mainly in Auckland, Wellington; not funded by DHBs in most regions.
        
        #### Australia
        
          **Higher incidence:** 15,000+ new melanomas annually (similar rates to NZ).
          **SunSmart campaign:** Public education; "Slip, Slop, Slap" messaging.
          **Public funding:** Melanoma excision and SLN biopsy funded for Stage IB+.
          **Mohs access:** More widely available through public and private dermatology.
        
        #### United Kingdom
        
          **Lower incidence:** ~2,500 new melanomas annually; lower latitude, temperate climate.
          **NHS access:** Two-week wait for suspected melanoma; public funding for all treatments.
          **Mohs surgery:** More established in NHS trusts.
        
        #### United States
        
          **Variable incidence:** Higher in southern states; ~100,000 new melanomas annually.
          **Insurance-dependent:** Access varies widely; cost major barrier for uninsured patients.
          **Mohs availability:** Widely established in dermatology centres.
          **Immunotherapy access:** Available but often requires prior auth; high cost (~US$10,000+ per month).

      ❓Common questions▾
      
        #### How do I know if a spot is melanoma?
        Use ABCDE: Asymmetry, Border irregularity, Colour variation, Diameter >6mm, Evolving. If any concern, see your GP for referral to dermatology. Most suspicious lesions turn out to be benign, but early detection saves lives.

        #### Should I get a full-body skin check?
        If you have fair skin, multiple naevi, family history of melanoma, or previous skin cancer, annual full-body checks are recommended. Those with few naevi and low risk can have less frequent checks.

        #### What's the difference between a normal mole and melanoma?
        Normal moles are often symmetrical, have uniform colour, regular borders, and don't change. Melanomas tend to be asymmetrical, multi-coloured, have irregular borders, and evolve over weeks to months.
