#### Clinical features

              #### Symptoms
              Dyspnoea on exertion, orthopnoea, PND
              Fatigue, exercise intolerance
              Ankle swelling, abdominal distension
              Bendopnoea (dyspnoea on bending forward)

              #### Signs
              Raised JVP, S3 gallop
              Bibasal crackles (pulmonary oedema)
              Pitting oedema (peripheral)
              Tachycardia, displaced apex beat, hepatomegaly

          #### Investigations
          
            TestPurpose / interpretation
            
              BNP / NT-proBNPBest initial test. BNP <35 pg/mL or NT-proBNP <125 pg/mL has high NPV - effectively rules out HF in a low-pre-test patient. Elevated in HF, but also in AF, PE, CKD, older age.
              EchocardiogramDefines EF (HFrEF vs HFpEF), wall motion, valves, pericardium. Essential for diagnosis and treatment planning. Request promptly once HF suspected.
              ECGRarely normal in HF. Look for: AF, LVH, LBBB (CRT eligibility), ischaemic changes, QRS duration.
              CXRCardiomegaly, pulmonary vascular redistribution, Kerley B lines, pleural effusion, interstitial oedema.
              BloodsFBC, UEC, LFTs, glucose/HbA1c, TFTs (thyroid dysfunction causes HF), lipids, ferritin/TSAT (iron deficiency worsens HF outcomes).
              UrinalysisProteinuria suggests renal disease or cardiorenal syndrome. Glycosuria if diabetes not yet diagnosed.

            Use natriuretic peptides first
            BNP/NT-proBNP is the best initial test in primary care. A normal result makes HF very unlikely and avoids unnecessary echocardiography. If elevated, arrange echocardiogram and initiate investigation - do not wait for specialist review before starting diuretics if symptomatic.

          #### Common causes to identify and treat
          
            **Ischaemic cardiomyopathy:** most common in NZ - prior MI, coronary artery disease
            **Hypertension:** pressure overload causing HFpEF or HFrEF
            **Atrial fibrillation:** tachycardia-induced cardiomyopathy; often reversible with rate/rhythm control
            **Valvular disease:** aortic stenosis, mitral regurgitation
            **Alcohol:** alcoholic cardiomyopathy - can recover significantly with abstinence
            **Thyroid disease:** both hypo- and hyperthyroidism
            **Chemotherapy:** anthracyclines, trastuzumab, immune checkpoint inhibitors

        📊Classification - HFrEF, HFmrEF, HFpEF▾
        
          Ejection fraction (EF) on echocardiogram divides HF into three phenotypes - which determines pharmacotherapy, prognosis and device eligibility.

              HFrEF
              EF < 40%
              Reduced ejection fraction - **"systolic" HF**. Best evidence base. The four pillars significantly reduce mortality and hospitalisation. Most responsive to drug therapy.

              HFmrEF
              EF 40–49%
              Mildly reduced EF - a transitional category. Many patients shift to HFrEF or HFpEF over time. Treat as HFrEF; evidence for full four pillars is growing.

              HFpEF
              EF ≥ 50%
              **"Diastolic" HF**. More common in older women with hypertension, obesity, diabetes, AF. Diuretics for congestion; SGLT2i now have evidence. Address all risk factors.

            EF can recover
            With optimal therapy, up to 40% of HFrEF patients improve their EF to ≥50% - termed **"HF with recovered EF" (HFrecEF)**. Do not stop HF medications when EF recovers - discontinuation frequently leads to relapse.

        💊HFrEF - the four disease-modifying therapies▾
        
          All four drugs independently reduce all-cause and cardiovascular mortality in HFrEF. Target is to **up-titrate all four to maximum tolerated doses**, not to stop at a low dose. Start all four as early as possible - order of initiation matters less than achieving therapeutic doses.

              Pillar 1
              #### ACEi / ARB / ARNI
              **Drugs:** ramipril, enalapril; losartan; sacubitril-valsartan (ARNI)
              **Benefit:** ~17% reduction in mortality (ACEi); ARNI superior to ACEi - switch if tolerated
              **Target:** ramipril 10 mg daily; sacubitril-valsartan 97/103 mg BD
              **Monitor:** K⁺, creatinine, BP. Expect small creatinine rise - acceptable up to ~30%
              **Caution:** avoid ACEi + ARB together. Avoid in pregnancy, bilateral RAS, angio-oedema

              Pillar 2
              #### Beta-blocker
              **Drugs:** carvedilol, bisoprolol, metoprolol succinate (evidence-based; not all beta-blockers)
              **Benefit:** ~34% reduction in mortality; reduces sudden cardiac death
              **Target:** carvedilol 25 mg BD; bisoprolol 10 mg daily
              **Start low, titrate slow** - at least 2-weekly increments
              **Caution:** do not start in acutely decompensated HF; do not stop abruptly

              Pillar 3
              #### Mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist (MRA)
              **Drugs:** spironolactone, eplerenone
              **Benefit:** ~30% reduction in mortality (RALES trial)
              **Target:** spironolactone 25–50 mg daily
              **Monitor:** K⁺ closely - risk of hyperkalaemia especially with ACEi/ARB + CKD
              **Avoid if:** eGFR <30, K⁺ >5.0 mmol/L. Side effect: gynaecomastia (spiro) - use eplerenone

              Pillar 4
              #### SGLT2 inhibitor
              **Drugs:** dapagliflozin, empagliflozin
              **Benefit:** ~25% reduction in HF hospitalisation and CV death (DAPA-HF, EMPEROR-Reduced)
              **Target:** dapagliflozin 10 mg daily; empagliflozin 10 mg daily
              **Benefit regardless** of diabetes status - prescribe for HFrEF irrespective of HbA1c
              **Caution:** avoid if eGFR <20; UTI/genital infection risk; DKA (rare)

          #### Additional HFrEF therapies

              #### Ivabradine
              **When:** sinus rhythm + HR ≥70 despite max beta-blocker + EF ≤35%
              Reduces hospitalisation for worsening HF
              **Dose:** 5–7.5 mg BD

              #### IV iron (ferric carboxymaltose)
              **When:** iron deficiency (ferritin <100, or 100–299 with TSAT <20%) regardless of Hb
              Reduces HF hospitalisation; improves symptoms and quality of life
              Check ferritin + TSAT in all HF patients

              #### Digoxin
              Reduces HF hospitalisation in HFrEF + AF or sinus rhythm
              No mortality benefit; narrow therapeutic window
              Low-dose (0.0625–0.125 mg) in elderly; monitor levels and renal function

              #### Hydralazine + isosorbide dinitrate
              Alternative if ACEi/ARB/ARNI not tolerated (e.g. severe CKD, bilateral RAS, angio-oedema)
              Mortality benefit shown in Black patients in AHEFTE trial

            Aim for target doses
            The mortality benefits in trials were achieved at **target doses**, not arbitrary low doses. A patient on bisoprolol 1.25 mg is not on a beta-blocker for HF - up-titrate at every opportunity, at least 2-weekly. Use shared care with heart failure nurses or specialist clinics to achieve this.

        🔵HFpEF - the harder phenotype▾
        
          HFpEF has historically lacked disease-modifying therapy - unlike HFrEF, no drug convincingly reduced mortality until the SGLT2i trials. Management focuses on symptom control and risk factor reduction.

          #### Diagnosis pitfall
          HFpEF is a diagnosis of exclusion - confirm that symptoms and elevated BNP are not explained by obesity, deconditioning, pulmonary disease or AF alone. Diastolic dysfunction on echo + raised filling pressures (H2FPEF score or exercise testing) is required.

          #### Management
          
            **SGLT2 inhibitors:** empagliflozin and dapagliflozin both reduce HF hospitalisation in HFpEF - now recommended. The only class with positive HFpEF trial data.
            **Diuretics (frusemide, bumetanide):** relieve congestion - the mainstay of symptom management. Titrate to euvolaemia; avoid over-diuresis.
            **Treat all contributing conditions aggressively:** hypertension (target <130/80), diabetes, obesity, AF rate control, sleep apnoea, CKD.
            **Exercise training:** improves functional capacity and symptoms - refer to cardiac rehabilitation.
            **Spironolactone:** the TOPCAT trial was neutral overall but may reduce HF hospitalisation - reasonable to add in HFpEF if tolerated, especially with hypertension.

            HFpEF is not benign
            HFpEF has similar hospitalisation rates and mortality to HFrEF. It disproportionately affects older women and is closely linked with metabolic syndrome. Frailty and comorbidity make management complex - specialist input is valuable.

        🚨Acute decompensated heart failure▾
        
          #### Recognition
          
            - Worsening dyspnoea, orthopnoea, PND, new or increasing oedema
            - Weight gain >2 kg in 48 hours (patient should be weighing daily)
            - Raised JVP, new S3, worsening crackles, new or worsening oxygen desaturation

          #### Precipitants - find and treat the cause
          
            PrecipitantClue / action
            
              Medication non-adherenceMost common - dietary indiscretion + missed diuretics
              Atrial fibrillationNew AF or uncontrolled rate - rate control, anticoagulate
              ACS / ischaemiaTroponin, ECG - urgent if suspected
              InfectionPneumonia, UTI - FBC, CRP, urine MCS, CXR
              AnaemiaFBC - transfuse cautiously (small volumes)
              Renal deteriorationUEC - drug causes (NSAIDs, overdiuresis)
              Poorly controlled HTNBP measurement - up-titrate antihypertensives
              New medicationNSAIDs, verapamil, diltiazem, thiazolidinediones, steroids

          #### In-hospital management (overview)
          
            **Oxygen:** target SpO2 ≥94%; CPAP/NIV for cardiogenic pulmonary oedema
            **Diuresis:** IV frusemide (at least equivalent oral dose IV); response-guided titration
            **Vasodilators:** IV GTN if hypertensive and no haemodynamic compromise
            **Avoid:** stopping beta-blocker unless haemodynamic compromise - reduce dose if needed, restart before discharge
            **Do not start** ARNI, new MRA or new beta-blocker during acute decompensation

            When to admit
            Admit for: SpO2 <90% on air, respiratory rate >25, new or worsening haemodynamic compromise (SBP <90), new AF with rapid ventricular rate, suspected ACS, or failure to respond to intensified oral diuresis within 24 hours.

          #### After decompensation
          
            - Identify and address the precipitant
            - Optimise all four HFrEF pillars before discharge or at first post-discharge visit
            - Review in 1–2 weeks - this is the highest-risk period for re-hospitalisation and death
            - Ensure self-monitoring plan: daily weighing, salt restriction, flexible diuresis plan

        ⚡Device therapy - ICD and CRT▾

              #### ICD - implantable cardioverter-defibrillator
              **Indication:** EF ≤35% on optimal medical therapy for ≥3 months, NYHA Class II–III, expected survival >1 year
              **Benefit:** prevents sudden cardiac death from VT/VF - ~23% relative risk reduction in mortality
              **Not if:** NYHA Class IV (poor prognosis), non-cardiac life expectancy <1 year, within 40 days of MI

              #### CRT - cardiac resynchronisation therapy
              **Indication:** EF ≤35% + LBBB with QRS ≥130 ms (optimal with ≥150 ms), NYHA Class II–IV
              **Benefit:** improves EF, symptoms, hospitalisation and mortality - ~36% reduction in mortality
              CRT-D (with defibrillator) preferred if ICD criteria also met

          #### Before referral for device therapy
          
            EF must be reassessed after **at least 3 months of optimal medical therapy** - many patients improve significantly and may no longer meet device criteria
            - Treat reversible causes first: revascularisation for ischaemia, thyroid replacement, abstinence from alcohol
            - Ensure all four HFrEF drugs are at target or maximum tolerated dose
            - Refer to heart failure clinic or cardiologist for device evaluation

        ⚠️Drugs to avoid in heart failure▾
        
          Several commonly prescribed drugs worsen heart failure or are dangerous in HF patients. Review the medication list at every visit.

            ✗**NSAIDs (incl. COX-2 inhibitors)** - sodium retention, worsening renal function, blunting diuretic effect. Avoid in all HF.
            ✗**Non-dihydropyridine CCBs** (verapamil, diltiazem) - negative inotropy, worsens HFrEF. Avoid in EF <40%.
            ✗**Thiazolidinediones** (pioglitazone, rosiglitazone) - fluid retention, worsening oedema. Avoid in HF.
            ✗**Most antiarrhythmics** (flecainide, sotalol, dronedarone) - pro-arrhythmic in structural heart disease. Use amiodarone only if needed.
            ✗**Systemic corticosteroids** - sodium and fluid retention, weight gain, destabilises HF.
            ✗**Tricyclic antidepressants** - negative inotropy, arrhythmia risk. Use SSRIs or SNRIs instead.
            ✗**Metformin** - use with caution; withhold if eGFR <30 or during decompensation / contrast procedures.
            ✗**High-dose alpha-blockers** (doxazosin as monotherapy) - fluid retention, increased HF hospitalisation (ALLHAT).

        📈Monitoring & self-management▾
        
          #### Routine monitoring
          
            ParameterFrequencyNotes
            
              UEC + eGFR1–2 weekly after drug changes; then 3–6 monthlyK⁺ and creatinine - especially with ACEi/MRA/SGLT2i combination
              Blood pressureEach visitTarget <130/80; hold or reduce if SBP <90
              Heart rateEach visitBeta-blocker target HR 60–70 bpm at rest in sinus rhythm
              WeightDaily by patient; each visitDry weight reference point; alert plan for >2 kg gain
              BNP/NT-proBNPAt diagnosis; after significant changesUseful for tracking trajectory; falling = improvement
              EchocardiogramAt diagnosis; after 3–6 months of optimal therapy; if clinical changeReassess EF before considering ICD/CRT
              Ferritin + TSATAnnuallyIron deficiency common and treatable; target ferritin >100

          #### Self-management plan
          
            **Daily weighing:** same time, same scales, before breakfast - baseline dry weight established by clinician
            **Salt restriction:** <2 g sodium/day - no added salt, avoid processed food
            **Fluid restriction:** 1.5–2 L/day in moderate-severe HF (less emphasis in mild HF)
            **Flexible diuretic plan:** increase frusemide dose by one step if weight rises >2 kg over 2 days; contact GP if no response in 48 hours
            **Alcohol:** abstain in alcoholic cardiomyopathy; <1 standard drink/day otherwise
            **Exercise:** cardiac rehabilitation - improves functional capacity and quality of life in stable HF
            **Vaccinations:** influenza annually, pneumococcus, COVID-19 (HF is high-risk condition)

        💡Pearls & pitfalls▾
        
          #### Pearls
          
            - Check BNP/NT-proBNP first - a normal result confidently excludes HF and avoids unnecessary echo referrals.
            - Start all four pillars early and up-titrate - low doses provide little benefit. Most adverse events resolve with dose adjustment, not discontinuation.
            - SGLT2 inhibitors benefit HF patients regardless of diabetes status - prescribe for the HFrEF or HFpEF, not for glucose.
            - Check iron studies in every HF patient - ferritin <100 is common, treatable, and meaningfully improves symptoms and hospitalisation rate.
            - Do not stop beta-blockers during decompensation unless haemodynamic compromise - reduce the dose instead and restart before discharge.
            - EF can recover - re-echo after 3–6 months of optimal therapy before referring for ICD/CRT.
          
          #### Pitfalls
          
            - Diagnosing HFpEF without ruling out obesity-related dyspnoea, deconditioning or pulmonary disease.
            - Stopping the four pillars when the patient "feels better" - HF drugs are lifelong.
            - Prescribing NSAIDs for joint pain in a patient with HF - always find an alternative.
            - Missing AF as a precipitant or cause - always check for new onset AF in decompensation.
            - Not following up within 1–2 weeks after hospital discharge - highest risk period for re-admission and death.
            - Failing to up-titrate beta-blockers because HR is "already low" - target HR 60–70 bpm is the goal, not a contraindication.
