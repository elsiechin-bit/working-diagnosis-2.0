## Part I: The numbers in New Zealand
        
        The 2024 RNZCGP Workforce Survey found that **38% of NZ GPs rated themselves as highly burnt out** (level 7-10 on an eleven-point scale), and 70% rated themselves as moderately-to-highly burnt out (level 4-10). The 38% figure is down from 48% in 2022, but substantially higher than the 22% measured in 2016 and 31% measured in 2020 1. Whatever has happened in NZ general practice over the last decade has been measurable, and most of it has not been good.

        The other workforce indicators are no more reassuring. The median age of NZ GPs is 51; 17% are at or past retirement age (65 or older), and 6% are over 70 1. The pipeline does not match the outflow: NZ has approximately 74 GPs per 100,000 population in 2021, projected to fall to 70 per 100,000 by 2031, with a projected shortfall of approximately 300 GPs against population need over the next decade 1. Among GPs aged 40-44 - the cohort most likely to determine the workforce shape in 2035 - 17% are considering leaving NZ, and 6% intend to do so within five years 1.

        A career-recommendation question gives a direct read on professional morale. Asked whether they would recommend a career in general practice, 54% said yes in 2020, 39% in 2022, and 43% in 2024 1. The 2024 number is up modestly from the 2022 trough but remains well below pre-pandemic levels.

        These numbers describe a workforce under sustained pressure, aged faster than it has replaced itself, and in distress at rates that no other profession in NZ would consider acceptable.

        ## Part II: What burnout is - and what it isn't
        
        The Maslach framework, which has dominated burnout research since the 1980s, defines occupational burnout as three features: **emotional exhaustion, depersonalisation (cynicism), and reduced personal accomplishment** 2. The framework is empirically useful - the three features cluster reliably and correlate with adverse outcomes for clinicians and patients.

        It is also, on its own, the wrong frame for most of what NZ GPs are experiencing.

        ### Burnout vs. moral injury

        The most influential reframing in recent physician-distress literature is Talbot and Dean's 2018 distinction: **"Physicians aren't 'burning out.' They're suffering from moral injury."** [3,4]

        **Burnout** is a description of symptoms in the individual. It implies a problem of individual capacity - that the clinician has run out of resources. It directs interventions at the individual: build resilience, manage stress, practise mindfulness, take breaks, see a counsellor. None of these is wrong; on the evidence, none is sufficient.

        **Moral injury** names a different phenomenon: the repeated experience of being unable to deliver the care one knows the patient needs, because of constraints beyond one's control. The 14-minute slot for a consultation that needs forty minutes. The medication that would help, that isn't funded. The community-based mental health support that doesn't exist. The hospital admission that prevents a death you would have prevented yourself if you'd had time. The slow accumulation of compromises with what the work should be.

        Moral injury reframes the locus of the problem from the individual to the system. It reframes the appropriate response from individual fortification to structural change.

        The RNZCGP's 2020/21 burnout survey, conducted after the 2020 Workforce Survey results came out, identified three themes through 1,495 responses: **increased patient need and complexity, structure of the funding formula, and administrative burden** 5. None of these is an individual-resilience problem.

        ## Part III: Drivers in NZ general practice
        
        ### Patient complexity has risen faster than capacity

        Multimorbidity, mental health complexity, and social complexity have all increased over the last decade, while consultation length has not. The capitation funding formula (until the 2025 reweighting, which included multimorbidity, rurality, and deprivation as factors for the first time since 2002) has not adequately captured time required for higher-complexity patients 6. The practices serving the highest-needs populations have been systematically underfunded relative to the time their patients require. The gap between "the consultation we can fund" and "the consultation the patient actually needs" lives somewhere in every working day, and it is not closed by individual resilience.

        ### Administrative burden has risen substantially

        The combination of electronic health records, prescribing review systems, audit and accreditation requirements, ACC documentation, secondary-care discharge management, and the slow accretion of compliance tasks over a decade has produced a documentation load unrecognisable to a GP from twenty years ago. International data suggest that for every hour of patient contact, primary care physicians now spend roughly one to two additional hours on documentation and administration 7. Most of this work is unpaid relative to capitation funding; much happens outside scheduled hours.

        ### The secondary-care interface has degraded

        Long waits for elective referrals, tightening criteria for specialist services, and chronic difficulty accessing community-based mental health services all push clinical work back into general practice. The GP becomes the holder of the patient who cannot get the care they need elsewhere - which is, in moral-injury terms, exactly the position that produces the most damage.

        ### Workforce shortage compounds everything

        Each GP who retires, leaves the country, or moves to part-time without replacement increases the load on those remaining. The 17% of mid-career GPs considering leaving NZ is not just a statistic; it is a feedback loop. Workforce loss raises pressure on those left, which raises burnout, which raises further workforce loss.

        ### Isolation is structural

        General practice is, by its nature, the work of one clinician with one patient in one room. Unlike hospital medicine, there is little institutional structure for processing difficult clinical encounters together - no team handovers, no debrief structures, no peer review built into the day. The administrative isolation of small practices compounds the clinical isolation of the consultation.

        New Zealand GPs report the highest consultation volumes relative to list size and time allocation, followed by Australia. This creates a structural constraint: when patient lists remain constant or increase while appointment time is fixed or decreases, the only accommodation is physician time compression (earlier starts, later finishes, skipped breaks).

        ### Working hours across jurisdictions

        GPs across high-income countries report working substantially beyond contracted hours, primarily to manage non-billable work. The UK average is 42.8 hours in direct patient care, with 8-10 additional hours on administration, totalling 50-52 hours weekly 10. German GPs report 45-48 hour weeks including admin. Dutch GPs, supported by stronger regulatory limits, average 42-44 hours. Australian data show 48-52 hour weeks for full-time practitioners. New Zealand qualitative reports suggest 45-55 hour weeks, though this is not systematically measured.

        ## Part V: Structural factors associated with sustainable practice
        
        Jurisdictions and practices with the following characteristics report higher satisfaction and lower burnout rates:

              Factor
              Evidence for impact

              **Controlled workload**
              Maximum list size regulations (Netherlands, Germany) or softer targets with flexibility are associated with lower burnout. Systems without list-size constraints (USA, parts of Australia and NZ) show higher burnout 11.

              **Protected consultation time**
              Systems that regulate or culturally enforce minimum consultation times show better outcomes. Netherlands (13-17 min avg) and Germany (legislated minimums) report lower burnout than systems where appointment times are driven by productivity metrics (NZ, Australia, USA avg 10-12 min).

              **Administrative support**
              Practices with adequate admin and reception staff proportionate to patient volume report lower burnout. UK data show a correlation between practice staff ratios and GP wellbeing: practices with ≥0.6 administrative staff per 1,000 patients report significantly lower burnout 12.

              **Regulatory time protection**
              Systems where non-patient time is built into contracts (Germany, Denmark, Netherlands) rather than squeezed from patient care show better outcomes. In UK and NZ, protected admin time depends on individual practice design, creating wide variation.

              **Team cohesion**
              Practices reporting high team support and psychological safety show lower burnout regardless of patient volume. This correlates with practice size, stability, and leadership culture 13.

        ## Part VI: What the evidence shows about interventions
        
        ### Individual vs. organisational interventions

        The most-cited synthesis is West, Dyrbye, and colleagues' 2016 Lancet meta-analysis of 52 controlled studies 14. The headline finding: **organisational-focused interventions produce roughly twice the effect size of individual-focused ones.**

        **Individual interventions** with some evidence include mindfulness-based stress reduction, cognitive behavioural approaches, structured peer support groups, and reflective practice 14. Effect sizes are modest. None is structurally sufficient for the level of distress the workforce data describe.

        **Organisational interventions** with evidence include reductions in workload (the most direct lever), changes to schedule structure, reductions in EHR/documentation burden, leadership development, and team-based clinical reorganisation [14,15]. The Stanford and Mayo Clinic literature is most-cited; both organisations have invested heavily in organisational-level interventions and report measurable reductions in burnout indicators.

        ### The wellness industry critique

        The wellness industry - meditation apps, resilience workshops, rapid-response wellness teams - has been criticised for both empirical and ethical reasons. Empirically, effect sizes are small and do not address structural drivers. Ethically, the framing implies that the appropriate response to a structurally damaged work environment is to better fortify the workers, which is the same logic that would suggest the appropriate response to lead in the water is to teach individual children to drink more carefully 4.

        This is not an argument against personal practices that help individual clinicians get through the day. It is an argument against the implicit message that those practices, alone, are the answer.

        ## Part VII: What helps, in practice
        
        ### Naming the problem accurately

        If most of what you're experiencing is moral injury rather than burnout, calling it that - to yourself, to your colleagues, to the people who manage health systems - changes what conversations are possible. It prevents the mistake of assuming the problem is in you when the problem is largely in the conditions of the work.

        ### Colleague relationships

        The single intervention with the most consistent evidence alongside workload reduction is structured peer connection - protected time to discuss difficult cases, reflect on hard consultations, and process accumulated clinical experience with people who understand it [14,15]. This is harder in solo or small-practice settings than larger group practices, and is one of the structural advantages of practice consolidation the literature supports.

        ### Boundaries that hold

        The evidence on individual interventions is mixed, but things that consistently help include: not checking work email outside work hours, genuinely protected lunch breaks, leaving on time when possible, not taking patient files home. None of these is heroic. All of them are harder to do than they sound.

        ### Reducing the unpaid load

        Time spent on admin outside work hours is the single most modifiable individual contributor to burnout in the recent literature 15. Where the practice can fund clerical or nurse-led support to reduce documentation load, that change has more measurable impact than any wellness intervention. Where it cannot, identifying documentation tasks that absolutely must be done by the GP versus those that could be delegated is a meaningful exercise.

        ### Knowing when to seek help

        GP rates of mental ill-health, suicidal ideation, and completed suicide are higher than population baseline, and significantly higher in some studies for female physicians 16. The Medical Council of New Zealand and the Doctors' Health Advisory Service exist for this reason. They are confidential, colleague-staffed, and the right call long before the situation feels desperate. The single most evidence-supported predictor of seeking help in time is having decided in advance that you would.

        ### Structural advocacy

        The work of arguing for capitation reweighting, funded clerical support, reduced compliance burden, and sustainable workforce policy is the work of managing burnout - inseparable from it. Every individual GP cannot fix the system, but the College, the GP Leaders' Forum, and sector advocacy bodies do meaningful work that depends on members staying engaged enough to support it.

        ## Closing note
        
        The numbers are what they are. The drivers are what they are. The evidence on interventions is what it is. The reframing from burnout to moral injury is not magic; it is a more accurate description of what most of us are dealing with most of the time. That accuracy is the thing that makes structural change possible to argue for, and individual coping more sustainable when the structural change is slow.

        If you are reading this and recognising yourself in the high-burnout 38%, or in the 17% considering leaving - the recognition is itself the start of the work. Talk to a colleague. Talk to the Doctors' Health Advisory Service. Talk to a GP you trust who isn't in your practice. Most of us have been somewhere along this distribution at some point. None of us are alone in it, even when the consultation room makes it feel that way.
