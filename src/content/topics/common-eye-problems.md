#### Overview
        Myopia (short-sightedness), hyperopia (long-sightedness) and astigmatism result from mismatch between corneal curvature and axial length. Most common cause of blurred vision globally.

        #### Myopia
        
          **Mechanism:** Eye too long or cornea too steep; light focuses in front of retina.
          **Symptoms:** Blurred distance vision, difficulty seeing board at school or distant objects.
          **Management:** Glasses, contact lenses or LASIK/refractive surgery. Progressive myopia in children now managed with atropine or orthokeratology to slow progression.
          **Screening:** Preschool and school vision checks recommended.
        
        #### Hyperopia
        
          **Mechanism:** Eye too short or cornea too flat; light focuses behind retina.
          **Symptoms:** Blurred near vision, eye strain, headaches (from accommodation effort).
          **Management:** Glasses (especially for near work), contact lenses or refractive surgery.
        
        #### Astigmatism
        
          **Mechanism:** Cornea has different curvatures in different meridians.
          **Symptoms:** Blurred vision at all distances, distortion, eye strain.
          **Management:** Cylindrical glasses, toric contacts or refractive surgery.
        
        #### GP role
        Perform simple visual acuity screening with Snellen chart. If abnormal, refer to optometrist for full refraction. Optometrists can prescribe glasses and contacts; refer to ophthalmologist if refractive surgery desired or if vision abnormality not explained by refraction alone.

      💧Dry eye (keratoconjunctivitis sicca)▾
      
        #### Pathophysiology
        Insufficient tear production or excessive tear evaporation leads to corneal desiccation and epithelial damage. Common with screen time, hormonal changes (menopause), and Sjögren syndrome.

        #### Symptoms
        
          - Gritty sensation, burning, foreign body feeling.
          - Paradoxical tearing (reflex tearing due to irritation).
          - Worse at end of day and with screen work.
        
        #### Management
        
          **Lifestyle:** Screen breaks (20-20-20 rule: every 20 minutes, look 20 feet away for 20 seconds), humidifier, reduce air conditioning exposure.
          **Artificial tears:** Lubricating eye drops 4-6 times daily (non-preserved preferred for frequent use).
          **Ointments:** At night (thicker formulation), e.g., lacrilube.
          **Warm compresses:** Twice daily to improve meibomian gland secretion.
          **Cyclosporine drops:** If moderate/severe (prescription); reduces inflammation.
          **Referral:** To optometrist or ophthalmologist if unresponsive to conservative treatment.

      ✨Floaters and flashes▾
      
        #### Floaters
        
          **Cause:** Vitreous opacities (collagen, pigment, blood). Posterior vitreous detachment (PVD) is most common; benign.
          **Symptoms:** Cobwebs, dots or shadowy shapes in visual field, move with eye movement.
          **Benign features:** Gradual onset, stable over time, no vision loss, normal examination.
          **Red flags:** Sudden onset or increase in floaters (retinal tear/detachment risk), photopsia (flashing lights), vision loss.
          **Management:** If benign, reassure; most stabilise over months. If sudden onset or red flags, refer urgently to ophthalmology (same-day or next-day).
        
        #### Flashes (photopsia)
        
          **Cause:** Vitreous traction on retina (PVD, retinal tear).
          **Features:** Lightning-like flashes in peripheral vision, typically last seconds.
          **Significance:** PVD alone is benign if no other signs; but presence of flashes + floaters suggests retinal tear risk.
          **Action:** Refer to ophthalmology urgently if flashes present (rule out retinal tear/detachment).

      🔴Red eye: differential diagnosis▾
      
        #### Non-urgent red eye (manage in primary care)
        
          **Conjunctivitis (bacterial, viral, allergic):** Mild discomfort, discharge, normal vision. Fluorescein staining: negative (unless bacterial with keratitis). Management: supportive care, artificial tears; antibiotics if bacterial.
          **Dry eye:** Burning, gritty sensation, worse with screens. Management: lubricants, warm compresses, screen breaks.
          **Subconjunctival haemorrhage:** Painless, bright red under conjunctiva, no discharge. Benign; resolves in 2-3 weeks. No treatment needed.
        
        #### Urgent referral (same-day ophthalmology)
        
          **Corneal abrasion/ulcer:** Pain, tearing, photophobia, fluorescein-positive staining. Risk of scarring and vision loss if untreated.
          **Acute angle-closure glaucoma:** Severe eye pain, blurred vision, halos around lights, nausea/vomiting. IOP >40mmHg. Medical emergency; needs IV acetazolamide, pilocarpine, possible laser.
          **Iritis (anterior uveitis):** Pain worse on looking at light, photophobia, ciliary flush (red around cornea), cells and flare in anterior chamber on slit lamp.
          **Keratitis (infectious, herpes, corneal ulcer):** Severe pain, photophobia, discharge, vision change.

      👁️When to refer to optometrist vs ophthalmologist▾
      
        #### Refer to optometrist
        
          - Refractive error (blurred vision due to need for glasses).
          - Presbyopia (age-related reading difficulty).
          - Mild dry eye unresponsive to conservative treatment.
          - Routine eye examination and prescription updates.
          - Contact lens fitting and troubleshooting.
        
        #### Refer to ophthalmologist
        
          - Red eye with pain, vision loss, or discharge (see urgent differentials above).
          - Sudden vision loss or flashing lights (retinal concerns).
          - Floaters with flashes or recent sudden increase (retinal tear/detachment risk).
          - Refractive surgery (LASIK, PRK) consultation.
          - Glaucoma or cataract diagnosis and management.
          - Diabetic or hypertensive retinopathy screening and treatment.
          - Intraocular pressure elevation (glaucoma screening).

      ❓Common questions▾
      
        #### Should I check blood pressure or glucose if patient has eye symptoms?
        Yes, if red eye, sudden vision loss or floaters. Hypertension and diabetes cause retinopathy. Screen all patients >40 years with these risk factors annually (dilated fundoscopy or retinal photography).

        #### How urgent is a patient with flashing lights?
        Same-day or next-day ophthalmology referral. Flashes suggest vitreous traction; if accompanied by floaters or vision changes, risk of retinal tear is high. Retinal detachment can follow if untreated.

        #### What's the difference between conjunctivitis and keratitis?
        Conjunctivitis: conjunctival injection, discharge, normal vision. Keratitis: corneal involvement, pain, photophobia, fluorescein staining of cornea. Keratitis is urgent; conjunctivitis is not.

        #### Can I prescribe eye drops for red eye empirically?
        Only if clearly infectious conjunctivitis (mild discharge, no pain, normal vision, normal cornea). Avoid topical steroids without specialist diagnosis (risk of prolonging or worsening keratitis if herpes simplex). Refer if any diagnostic uncertainty.
