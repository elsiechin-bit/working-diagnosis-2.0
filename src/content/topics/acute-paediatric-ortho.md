## Overview


**Paediatric orthopaedic injuries differ fundamentally from adults:** growing bones are weaker and more flexible, fractures follow different patterns, growth plates can be damaged, and healing is faster but also carries risk of malunion/non-union if poorly managed. Primary care role is rapid triage, appropriate imaging, and timely referral.

        #### First principles of paediatric ortho assessment
        
          **Neurovascular status first:** Pulse, capillary refill, temperature, sensation, motor function **before and after any reduction attempt**
          **Age & mechanism:** Mechanism of injury predicts fracture pattern; some injuries are pathognomonic (supracondylar from fall on outstretched hand, spiral femur from fall, metaphyseal chip fractures from inflicted injury)
          **Soft tissue assessment:** Bruising, swelling, skin integrity (open vs closed injury)
          **Functional assessment:** Active range of motion, ability to weight-bear (if lower limb)
          **Red flags for emergent intervention:** Neurovascular compromise, open fracture, compartment syndrome, vascular injury
        
        #### Imaging approach
        
          **Plain X-rays:** Standard first-line; two perpendicular views (AP & lateral) of injured area including adjacent joints
          **Comparison views:** Contralateral side often helpful in young children (unfamiliar anatomy) to distinguish fracture from normal epiphyseal separation or ossification centre
          **CT:** Reserved for complex metaphyseal fractures, acetabular injuries, or preoperative planning
          **MRI:** Physeal injuries, ligamentous disruption, avascular necrosis; low threshold when plain film inconclusive
          **Ultrasound:** Excellent in young children for visualising supracondylar fractures, excluding effusion/haemarthrosis

      ⚡Emergent presentations (ED referral same-day)▾
      
        #### Neurovascular compromise
        
          **Signs:** Absent or diminished pulse, pale/cool limb, severe pain, paresthesia, weakness, compartment syndrome (pain out of proportion, pain on passive stretch, tense compartments)
          **Common fractures causing this:** Supracondylar (anterior interosseous or radial artery), posterior knee dislocation (popliteal), tibia/fibula (anterior compartment), femur (vascular)
          **Action:** Immediate ED + ortho. Reduction often restores perfusion; if not, urgent vascular surgery.
          **Compartment syndrome:** Surgical emergency (fasciotomy). Do not wait; if suspected clinically, proceed to ED for urgent pressure measurement & release.
        
        #### Open fracture (skin breach over fracture site)
        
          **Risk:** Infection, non-union, growth disturbance
          **Action:** Do not attempt reduction in community. ED same-day. IV antibiotics (flucloxacillin ± cephalosporin), tetanus prophylaxis, nil-by-mouth (likely operative).
        
        #### Supracondylar fracture (distal humerus)
        
          **Epidemiology:** 40–50% of paediatric elbow injuries; age 5–8 most common; mechanism: fall on outstretched hand with elbow extended
          **Clinical signs:** Severe pain, gross swelling, posterior displacement, "S-shaped" deformity, loss of normal arm contour
          **Neurovascular risk:** 10–20% have nerve injury (anterior interosseous, radial, median); pulse may be lost but perfusion preserved via collaterals (check capillary refill, colour, warmth)
          **Imaging:** X-ray (lateral + AP); look for posterior displacement, loss of anterior humeral line
          **Action:** ED same-day; immobilise in sling. Displaced supracondylars usually require surgery (ORIF or percutaneous pinning).
          **Undisplaced (Gartland grade I):** Immobilise in sling/collar-and-cuff; review ortho outpatients within 1–2 weeks.
        
        #### Posterior knee dislocation
        
          **Mechanism:** High-energy dashboard injury, fall with leg bent backward
          **Risk:** Popliteal artery injury (amputation risk); 50% have vascular injury even with gross perfusion
          **Action:** Do not reduce in community. ED same-day with vascular assessment (duplex ultrasound or angiography).
        
        #### Femoral shaft fracture
        
          **Mechanism:** High-energy (fall from height, motor vehicle), or low-energy + suspicion for inflicted injury
          **Risk:** Significant blood loss (up to 1–2 units in thigh), fat embolism, vascular injury
          **Action:** ED same-day. IV access, fluid resuscitation if indicated. Immobilise in traction or splint. Operative fixation usually required.
          **Red flag:** Low-energy femur fracture in child 
        
        #### Ankle dislocation
        
          **Risk:** Vascular compromise, skin necrosis if not rapidly reduced
          **Action:** ED same-day. Reduction + vascular assessment.

      🏥Common fracture patterns & NZ referral pathway▾
      
        #### Greenstick & buckle (torus) fractures
        
          **Pattern:** Incomplete fractures (metaphyseal compression in torus fracture, incomplete break in greenstick). Common in forearm (radius/ulna), tibia.
          **Stability:** Generally stable; low neurovascular risk
          **Management:** Immobilisation (plaster backslab or full cast) 3–4 weeks. No anaesthesia usually needed if undisplaced. Primary care or ED imaging + ortho outpatients (can often be managed in clinic without surgery).
          **Referral:** Outpatient paediatric ortho (1–2 week wait acceptable if uncomplicated)
        
        #### Metaphyseal fractures (including Salter-Harris)
        **Salter-Harris classification (prognostic for growth disturbance risk):**

          **I (transverse through physis):** Usually stable; low risk of growth disturbance
          **II (fracture line extends through metaphysis):** Most common (60%); generally stable; moderate growth disturbance risk
          **III/IV (intra-articular):** Higher risk of growth disturbance & non-union; often require surgery
          **V (crush injury to physis):** Worst prognosis; growth disturbance common

          **Management:** Undisplaced → immobilisation, outpatient ortho. Displaced → ED + ortho for assessment ± reduction.
        
        #### Forearm fractures (radius & ulna)
        
          **Epidemiology:** Most common paediatric fracture (~50% of all paediatric fractures)
          **Types:** Buckle (torus) → stable, immobilise; both bone complete fracture → often requires reduction ± surgery
          **Displacement risk:** Assess initial angulation; some accept 10–15° angulation if stable
          **Management:** Buckle/undisplaced → cast 4 weeks, primary care or ED referral to ortho outpatients. Displaced → ED for reduction ± percutaneous pinning.
        
        #### Monteggia & Galeazzi fractures
        
          **Monteggia:** Ulnar shaft fracture + radial head dislocation (injury to both bones)
          **Galeazzi:** Radius shaft fracture + distal radioulnar joint (DRUJ) disruption
          **Risk:** Easy to miss DRUJ disruption on initial X-ray; compare with contralateral side
          **Management:** ED + ortho. Usually require ORIF to restore anatomy & prevent growth disturbance.
        
        #### Lateral condyle fractures (distal humerus)
        
          **Epidemiology:** 2nd most common elbow fracture; age 5–10; mechanism: fall on outstretched hand, varus stress
          **Risk:** Easily displaced or missed on initial film; high non-union & AVN risk if not anatomically reduced
          **Imaging:** Comparison views essential; may need CT/MRI if displacement unclear
          **Management:** Undisplaced, stable → immobilise 4 weeks, ortho follow-up at 1 week. Displaced → ED + ortho for consideration of ORIF.
          **Complications:** Cubitus valgus, tardy ulnar nerve palsy if healing poor
        
        #### Tibia/fibula fractures
        
          **Epidemiology:** 3rd most common; age varies; mechanism: direct blow, torsional (spiral), or pathological (low-energy)
          **Displacement & angulation:** Assess carefully; children tolerate some angulation due to remodelling potential
          **Management:** Undisplaced → cast (long leg initially 2 wks, then short leg 4–6 wks). Displaced → ED for reduction ± ORIF.
          **Referral:** Outpatient ortho for undisplaced; ED for displaced or neurovascular compromise.
          **Red flag:** Spiral fracture in toddler from minor trauma = inflicted injury until proven otherwise.

      🔍Growth plate injuries & physeal considerations▾
      
        **The physis (growth plate) is the weakest part of the paediatric skeleton.** Injuries to the physis can cause growth disturbances (limb length inequality, angular deformity), non-union, or premature closure. Proper classification and management are critical.

        #### Why physeal injuries matter
        
          **Healing potential:** Excellent callus formation; rapid union (weeks vs months in adults)
          **Growth disturbance risk:** Depends on injury type (Salter-Harris grade), location, and age. Distal femur, proximal tibia = highest risk.
          **Remodelling capacity:** Children remodel significant angulation; less tolerance for rotation, shortening, or translation
        
        #### Red flags for physeal injury
        
          **Joint-space widening on X-ray** (physeal injury)
          **Metaphyseal fracture near joint** (likely Salter II or higher)
          **Mechanism with varus/valgus stress** (lateral condyle, ankle)
          **Pain over growth plate** with minimal X-ray findings (MRI or ultrasound may be needed)
        
        #### Salter-Harris injuries & prognosis
        
          GradePatternFrequencyGrowth riskManagement
          
            **I**Transverse through physis5–10%LowImmobilise; usually painless at 3–4 weeks
            **II**Extends through metaphysis ("hockey stick")60%Low–moderateImmobilise; may displace, requires serial X-rays
            **III**Intra-articular, through epiphysis & physis8%Moderate–highORIF often required; risk of premature closure, angular deformity
            **IV**Intra-articular through epi, physis, metaphysis10%HighORIF usually required; anatomic reduction critical
            **V**Crush injury through physis (rare)RareVery highNo specific treatment; high growth disturbance rate; follow-up imaging

        #### Long-term follow-up
        
          **Serial imaging:** X-rays at 6 weeks, 3 months, 6 months, 12 months to detect premature physeal closure or angular deformity
          **Growth monitoring:** Periodic measurement of limb lengths; assess gait for varus/valgus
          **High-risk injuries (Salter III/IV, distal femur, proximal tibia):** Require prolonged ortho follow-up (years) to detect and manage growth disturbances

      💊Primary care management & analgesia▾
      
        #### Immobilisation & analgesia
        
          **First aid:** RICE (rest, ice, compression, elevation); immobilise in position of comfort; elevate to reduce swelling
          **Analgesia (before imaging/reduction):**
            
              - Paracetamol: 15 mg/kg per dose (up to 1 g), 4–6 hourly
              - Ibuprofen: 5–10 mg/kg per dose, 6–8 hourly (good for swelling)
              - Codeine: 0.5–1 mg/kg per dose with paracetamol (moderate analgesia); avoid if renal/hepatic impairment
              - Morphine: If severe pain; titrate in small doses (0.1–0.2 mg/kg IV/IM) for ED management

          **Procedural sedation/anaesthesia:** Required for most fracture reductions in ED (ketamine, propofol); not primary care role
        
        #### Sling/cast application (primary care role in uncomplicated cases)
        
          **Sling (arm injuries):** Broad arm sling or collar-and-cuff, check circulation at fingers frequently
          **Backslab (forearm, tibia):** Padded plaster back slab, wraps partially around limb, allows for swelling
          **Full cast:** Applied after swelling subsides (3–4 days); ensure room for circulation assessment
          **Weight-bearing:** Crutches for lower limb injuries; ensure child can safely navigate
        
        #### Safety netting & follow-up instructions
        
          **Neurovascular checks:** Educate parents on checking colour, temperature, sensation, swelling. Return if fingers/toes become pale, cold, or numb.
          **Swelling management:** Elevation, ice in first 48 hours, continue analgesia as directed
          **Immobilisation compliance:** Arm slings in particular are often removed prematurely by children; reinforce importance of rest
          **Follow-up imaging:** Advise on when and where repeat X-rays are needed (usually 1 week with ortho if displaced, 4 weeks if undisplaced)
          **Referral confirmation:** Ensure parent has clear ortho appointment; many undisplaced fractures managed in outpatient clinic without admission

      🚨Safeguarding & non-accidental injury patterns▾
      
        **Orthopaedic injuries are the 2nd most common sign of child abuse after head injury.** Certain fracture patterns carry high suspicion for inflicted injury; always ask mechanism carefully and assess consistency with age/developmental stage.

        #### High-suspicion fracture patterns (inflicted injury)
        
          **Metaphyseal "corner" or "bucket-handle" fractures:** Highly specific for abuse; result from shaking/jerking force
          **Spiral fractures in non-mobile children:** Requires significant rotational force; inconsistent with accidental fall narrative
          Femoral shaft fracture in child  Rare from accidental mechanism; high abuse risk
          **Multiple fractures at different healing stages:** Suggests repeated injury
          **Fractures in non-weight-bearing locations (humerus, ribs):** Uncommon accident patterns; concerning in context
          **Rib fractures:** Highly specific for abuse; result from direct chest impact or squeezing
          **Posterior metaphyseal (corner) fractures:** From pulling/jerking; highly suspicious
        
        #### Red flags in history
        
          - Mechanism inconsistent with age (e.g., fall from furniture in non-mobile infant)
          - Delay in seeking medical care ("found him crying yesterday")
          - Changing or vague account; parent unaware of injury
          - Prior bruising, other injuries, or previous suspected abuse
          - Isolation, failure to thrive, or behavioural concerns
        
        #### Safeguarding action
        
          **Suspicion of abuse:** Do not discharge to parent without assessment by appropriate authority (ED physician, safeguarding team)
          **Contact:** Oranga Tamariki (NZ child protection); local police if acute risk
          **Full imaging:** Skeletal survey (not just injured area) to detect other injuries
          **Documentation:** Careful, objective note of mechanism, injuries, and clinical concerns; may be used in court

      💡Clinical pearls & pitfalls▾
      
        #### Pearls
        
          ✦**Supracondylar fracture = ED same-day.** 40% of elbow injuries; high neurovascular risk. Check pulse before & after any manipulation.
          ✦**Lateral condyle fractures are easy to miss.** Comparison views essential; missed diagnosis → non-union & AVN. Low threshold for CT/MRI if displacement uncertain.
          ✦**Posterior knee dislocation = vascular emergency.** 50% have popliteal artery injury even with gross perfusion. Do not reduce; ED + vascular assessment.
          ✦**Greenstick fractures can wait.** Stable, low-risk; immobilise in backslab, refer outpatient ortho. No need for ED if uncomplicated.
          ✦**Children remodel angulation; they cannot remodel rotation.** Significant angulation acceptable with fracture pattern; rotation is not.
          ✦**Comparison views are gold in young children.** Normal ossification centres can mimic fractures. Contralateral side clarifies anatomy.
          ✦**Monteggia & Galeazzi fractures are frequently missed.** Both-bone fractures with associated ligamentous/joint injury. Always check DRUJ position.
          ✦**Metaphyseal fractures carry growth plate risk.** Salter-Harris classification predicts prognosis. High-grade injuries need prolonged ortho follow-up for growth disturbance detection.
          ✦**Spiral femur in toddler = inflicted injury until proven otherwise.** Safeguarding assessment mandatory; skeletal survey for occult injuries.
        
        #### Pitfalls
        
          **Delaying neurovascular assessment.** Check pulse, capillary refill, colour, sensation, motor before imaging. Compartment syndrome can develop rapidly.
          **Missing displaced fractures on initial X-ray.** Single view insufficient. Always obtain AP & lateral; compare with contralateral side in young children.
          **Attempting reduction in community for potentially complex fractures.** Supracondylar, lateral condyle, ankle dislocation = ED. Increased risk of neurovascular damage, non-union if poorly reduced.
          **Overlooking Monteggia/Galeazzi fractures.** DRUJ disruption easily missed; carries risk of malunion & functional loss. High index of suspicion for both-bone fractures.
          **Not recognising red flags for inflicted injury.** Spiral tibia in toddler, metaphyseal "corner" fractures, rib fractures, multiple fractures at different stages = safeguarding alert.
          **Underestimating immobilisation importance.** Child comfort & compliance critical. Inadequate immobilisation → malunion, delayed healing, functional loss.
          **Losing neurovascular monitoring during immobilisation.** Educate parents to check fingers/toes regularly for colour, swelling, sensation; return if compromise.
