#### Classic presentation
        
          **Onset:** Third trimester (most weeks 32–38), rarely postpartum
          **Lesions:** Intensely pruritic, erythematous, urticarial papules & plaques; hive-like; some with central vesicles
          **Distribution:** Begins on striae of abdomen (pathognomonic); spreads to thighs, buttocks, arms. Spares face, neck, palms, soles, periumbilical area
          **Pruritus:** Severe; keeps most patients awake at night. Excoriation uncommon (unlike eczema).
          **Duration:** 4–6 weeks (average), sometimes longer postpartum
        
        #### Diagnosis: clinical, no confirmatory test
        
          **History + exam:** Primigravida, late pregnancy, striae-onset papules, classic distribution
          **Skin biopsy:** Nonspecific; superficial perivascular lymphohistiocytic infiltrate. Direct immunofluorescence negative (important for ruling out herpes gestationis)
          **Serum serology:** Indirect immunofluorescence or NC16a ELISA negative (to exclude pemphigoid gestationis if diagnosis unclear)

          Key diagnostic feature: periumbilical sparing
          Lesions do NOT involve the umbilicus or immediate periumbilical area. This distinguishes PUPPP from herpes gestationis (pemphigoid gestationis), which often starts in the umbilicus. Periumbilical sparing = strong support for PUPPP diagnosis.

        #### Risk factors for PUPPP
        
          - Primigravidity (more common first pregnancy; only 7% recurrence in multiparous women)
          - Multiple gestation (twins/triplets: 8–12× higher incidence)
          - Excessive maternal weight gain
          - Higher BMI
          - Prolonged progesterone support (in vitro fertilization pregnancies)
        
        #### Natural history
        **Extremely benign:** No effect on fetal or maternal health. Rarely, infant born with transient mild rash that quickly fades. No increased adverse outcomes.

        #### Other pregnancy dermatoses (less likely if classic PUPPP)
        
          **Prurigo of pregnancy:** Excoriated papules (not urticarial), usually early pregnancy (1st–2nd trimester)
          **Intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy:** Pruritus often intense but no primary rash; elevated bile acids; risk of fetal complications
          **Atopic eruption of pregnancy:** Eczema-like; face/neck involvement; often in atopic patients
          **Drug reaction:** Timeline; medication history; less stereotyped distribution

          "Red flags" warranting further workup
          **Consider serology if:** Periumbilical involvement, prominent blisters, early-onset (first trimester), mucosal involvement, or atypical distribution. Serum for indirect IF ± NC16a ELISA to exclude pemphigoid gestationis.

      💊Management & treatment▾
      
        **Goal:** Symptom control (pruritus). PUPPP will resolve post-delivery regardless of treatment; therapy hastens comfort.

        #### First-line: topical corticosteroids
        
          **Mild-to-moderate rash:** Topical corticosteroid cream (e.g. clobetasone butyrate 0.05% / hydrocortisone 1% in pregnancy; TCS group III–IV safe in pregnancy with normal duration)
          **Frequent application:** 2–4 times daily; often effective for mild disease
          **Emollients:** Frequent moisturiser (aqueous cream, sorbolene); cool compresses
          **Emollient bath additives:** Oats, colloidal treatments; may reduce itch
        
        #### Second-line: systemic corticosteroids
        
          **When:** Severe pruritus interfering with sleep/function despite potent topical TCS
          **Regimen:** Short course; prednisolone 0.5–1 mg/kg once daily × 1–2 weeks, taper over days
          **Safety in pregnancy:** Systemic corticosteroids safe in short courses (risks of overt hyperglycemia, infection low; no proven teratogenicity at these doses)
          **Coordination:** Inform obstetric team; consider glucose monitoring if diabetic
        
        #### Adjunctive measures
        
          **Antihistamines (non-sedating):** Cetirizine or loratadine may provide modest itch relief; sedating agents (promethazine) if sleep disrupted, safe in pregnancy
          **Avoid triggers:** Hot showers, harsh soaps, tight clothing; opt for warm water, gentle cleansers
          **Postpartum follow-up:** Reassure rash will resolve within weeks; support for sleep/quality of life

          Reassurance is part of treatment
          Highlight: PUPPP is benign, self-limited, will not recur in future pregnancies, and causes no harm to baby. Knowing the condition is not serious often reduces anxiety-driven itch cycle.

      💡Clinical pearls & pitfalls▾
      
        #### Pearls
        
          ✦**Periumbilical sparing is diagnostic.** Lesions never involve the umbilicus or immediate periumbilical skin. This single feature helps exclude herpes gestationis.
          ✦**Primigravidas are the rule, not exception.** ~80% are first pregnancies; 
          ✦**Onset in striae is pathognomonic.** Papules begin on abdominal stretch marks, then spread. This distribution is classic and helpful diagnostically.
          ✦**Direct immunofluorescence is your safeguard.** If diagnosis uncertain, order it to exclude herpes gestationis (which requires systemic treatment & has fetal risk).
          ✦**Resolves 4–6 weeks post-delivery.** Timeline is reliable. Ensure patient knows delivery = relief, even if rash persists briefly postpartum.
          ✦**Multiple gestation = much higher risk.** Twins/triplets carry 8–12× incidence. Screen carefully in multiples.
          ✦**Short course of systemic corticosteroids is safe.** If sleep/function severely disrupted, prednisolone 0.5–1 mg/kg × 1–2 weeks is safe in pregnancy; brief course does not carry teratogenic risk.
          ✦**Excoriation is rare.** Intense itching but little scratching/oozing. If excoriated with secondary infection, think drug reaction or atopic dermatitis instead.
        
        #### Pitfalls
        
          **Mistaking PUPPP for herpes gestationis.** Ask about periumbilical involvement; if present or diagnosis unclear, order direct IF to avoid under- or over-treating.
          **Missing the IgG/C3 pattern in herpes gestationis.** If IF positive, requires systemic corticosteroids and fetal monitoring; different risk profile from PUPPP.
          **Failing to reassure about lack of fetal risk.** Patients are often anxious; explicitly state condition is benign, no effect on baby, will not recur.
          **Prescribing systemic corticosteroids too early.** Try topical TCS + emollients first. Reserve systemic course for severe, sleep-disrupting pruritus.
          **Attributing rash onset to gestational diabetes or PE.** PUPPP is purely cutaneous; no hepatic involvement, hypertension, proteinuria, or metabolic derangement. Work-up not needed if clinical diagnosis clear.
          **Assuming rash will clear immediately post-delivery.** Resolves over 4–6 weeks postpartum (can linger), not overnight. Manage expectation.
          **Missing atypical presentations.** Postpartum-onset PUPPP is rare but documented. Consider diagnosis even after delivery if context fits.
