## Overview


The companion piece to this one is a clinical map of what people actually take to build muscle - what works, what's grey-market, what's dangerous. This one is about where that knowledge comes from. For thirty years, an enormous amount of the pharmacology, dosing lore, and harm-reduction practice around image- and performance-enhancing drugs has been generated, debated, and refined not in journals but on forums - communities of lifters teaching each other, badly and well, in public. Understanding that history changes how a GP hears a patient who says "I read about this on a forum."

        ## Before the internet: print culture and gym lore

        Bodybuilding had a rich culture of transmitted knowledge long before anyone dialled a modem. Joe Weider's magazines - Muscle & Fitness, Flex - were the dominant channel from the 1950s onward, mixing genuine training advice with "Weider Principles" that were as much marketing as physiology, and with steroid use present but almost never named directly. Knowledge about anabolic drugs moved by word of mouth in gyms, from training partners and from a small number of underground handbooks - Dan Duchaine's "Underground Steroid Handbook", first self-published in 1981, is the best known, a mimeographed guide that treated dosing and cycling as a technical problem to be solved rather than a moral one.1 That book's stance - harm reduction through information, delivered without judgment, outside any official channel - set the template that forums would later scale up enormously.

        ## Usenet and the first forums

        The internet's earliest bodybuilding community was misc.fitness.weights, a Usenet newsgroup that became active in the early 1990s. It was text-only, unmoderated in the modern sense, and dominated by a handful of prolific posters - but it established norms that persisted: detailed personal logs, blunt correction of bad technique or bad information, and an expectation that claims about drugs or supplements would be argued over rather than taken on faith. Duchaine himself posted there. It was, in effect, the first place lifters could get real-time feedback from strangers who actually knew the subject, rather than from a magazine advertorial.

        ## The golden era: cycle logs, coded language, and homegrown pharmacology

        The mid-2000s to mid-2010s were arguably the high point of forum culture. Sites like MESO-Rx (whose name nods to the "Mesomorphosis" e-zine that preceded it) built an entire literature of user-generated pharmacology: compound-by-compound profiles of anabolic steroids and ancillary drugs, half-life tables, aromatase inhibitor dosing debates, and - distinctively - a strong norm of getting bloodwork done and posting the results for community interpretation. Threads followed recognisable genres: the "cycle log", a day-by-day diary of compounds, doses and subjective effects; the "PCT" (post-cycle therapy) thread, working out how to restart endogenous testosterone production after a cycle; and the "bloods" thread, where a member's lipid panel or liver enzymes would be picked over by dozens of anonymous but often well-informed replies.

        A specific, semi-coded vocabulary developed, partly for search-evasion and partly as in-group shorthand: "gear" for anabolic steroids, "juice" or "the sauce" more colloquially, "blast and cruise" for a pattern of continuous use at high and maintenance doses rather than cycling on and off, "natty" for a lifter claiming not to use steroids, and specific compound abbreviations (test, tren, deca, var, mast) used so routinely that a thread could be almost unreadable to an outsider. Clinicians who work with this population benefit from at least a passive vocabulary here, because patients will often use these terms assuming they will be understood, or as a test of whether the conversation is safe to continue.

          This user-generated pharmacology was not simply cheap knock-off medicine. Structured population data on this is genuinely difficult to obtain, since use is illegal in most jurisdictions and stigmatised, but repeated cross-sectional surveys of needle-exchange and image-and-performance-enhancing-drug (IPED) clinics in the UK - where such services exist specifically because of this population's needle-borne risk - have found a majority of anabolic steroid users report their own protocols were self-designed or peer-designed, drawing on exactly this kind of forum literature rather than a prescriber.2 The forums were, in a real sense, the primary care these users were not otherwise accessing.

        ### Why the culture was unusually rigorous, for a while
        What set the peak forum era apart from what came after was structural: reputation was earned slowly, through years of posting history, cycle logs with verifiable bloodwork, and being corrected in public by more experienced members. A new poster asking for a first-cycle protocol would typically be redirected toward established compound choices, told to get baseline and follow-up bloods, and warned off the more dangerous grey-market drugs (DNP and unregulated growth hormone attracted particularly consistent pushback). None of this made the underlying activity safe. But the community had built in genuine peer correction, an approximation of clinical governance assembled from below because no formal system was available to this population.

        ## The forum-to-influencer shift
        That structure has substantially eroded. As attention moved to Instagram, YouTube and TikTok through the 2010s and 2020s, the unit of authority shifted from a community with visible argument and correction to an individual creator whose expertise is signalled by physique and follower count rather than by a verifiable posting history. Coaching businesses and sponsorship deals create commercial incentive to minimise discussion of risk. And short-form video is a poor format for the kind of granular, cited disagreement that a forum thread could sustain over 40 replies. The information volume has gone up; the peer-review layer that used to sit underneath it has, in large part, gone.

        ## What this means in the consultation room
        A patient who mentions "running a cycle" or asks about "PCT" is very often drawing on a specific, decades-deep body of lay knowledge, not inventing terminology on the spot - and is likely to have encountered both good harm-reduction information (get bloods done, know the injection-site infection risks, understand testicular atrophy is expected) and much worse information (that "natural" test boosters work, that certain compounds are "safe" at any dose). Approaching the conversation with curiosity rather than correction-by-lecture tends to work better: asking what protocol they're following and where it came from often reveals both the specific compounds involved and how sound the surrounding advice was.

          A practical opening question
          "A lot of the guys I see training seriously are following protocols they've picked up online or from a mate - is that something going on for you?" This acknowledges the culture exists without moralising, and it is far more likely to produce an honest answer than "are you using steroids?" asked cold.

        ## A nuanced position
        It is easy to read this history as either a cautionary tale about unregulated internet medicine or as an underappreciated harm-reduction success story, and the honest answer sits between the two. Forums did generate real, useful, peer-corrected knowledge in a domain the formal health system had almost entirely ceded, at a time when almost no lifter using anabolic drugs would ever disclose that use to a GP. They also spread genuine misinformation, normalised very risky compounds, and gave confident-sounding amateur pharmacology the appearance of settled expertise. The shift to influencer-led social media has, if anything, kept the confidence while thinning out the correction. For clinicians, the practical takeaway is not to dismiss what a patient has learned online, nor to accept it uncritically, but to treat it as a specific, traceable body of lay knowledge worth understanding on its own terms - because that is how you find out what someone is actually doing, and where the real risks in their particular protocol lie.
