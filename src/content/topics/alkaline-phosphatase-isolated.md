## Overview


**Alkaline phosphatase (ALP)** is an enzyme produced by multiple tissues. It's not liver-specific; this is the key to understanding isolated elevation.

          #### Main sources:
          
            **Bone (30–50% of serum ALP):** Produced by osteoblasts during bone turnover. Higher in periods of growth (childhood, adolescence) and bone remodelling (fractures, Paget's, osteoporosis).
            **Liver (30–50%):** Produced by hepatocytes. Rises with cholestasis (bile duct obstruction) or hepatic inflammation.
            **Intestine (10%):** Small contribution from GI mucosal cells. Can rise after fatty meals or in fasting states.
            **Placenta:** Elevated in pregnancy (benign).
            **Other sources (rare):** Kidney, leucocytes, tumours.

          #### Why is isolation important?
          If ALP is elevated but **AST/ALT are normal** and **bilirubin is normal**, the elevation is unlikely to come from liver disease (hepatocellular injury or cholestasis). Most commonly, it's from bone or an incidental benign source.

          📋
          Differential diagnosis by context
          ▼

          #### In children and adolescents (age < 20):
          **Physiological elevation is the norm.** ALP can be 2–3 times the adult upper limit during growth spurts. No investigation needed unless extremely high (> 400 U/L) or symptoms present.

            **Growth:** Rapid bone turnover during puberty (most common).
            **Seasonal variation:** ALP higher in winter in some populations.

          #### In adults (age 20–55):

              Bone sources (most likely)
              
                **Recent fracture:** ALP rises 48 h post-fracture, peaks at 3–4 weeks. History will be obvious.
                **Paget's disease:** Marked ALP elevation (often > 400 U/L). Bone pain, deformity, neurological complications. Diagnosis on X-ray + biochemistry.
                **Osteoporosis:** Mild ALP elevation during active bone loss.
                **Healing callus:** After any bone injury; time-limited.

              Benign sources
              
                **Pregnancy:** ALP rises in third trimester (placental source). Normalises post-delivery.
                **Fasting state:** Intestinal ALP can transiently rise when fasting. Repeat test after food intake.
                **Blood type isoforms:** Genetic variants in ALP expression; benign.

          #### In older adults (age > 55):

              Bone-related (common)
              
                **Osteoporosis:** Chronic mild elevation.
                **Paget's disease:** Marked elevation, bone pain.
                **Metastatic bone disease:** Rare in isolation; usually other LFTs abnormal. Look for malignancy signs.

              Other causes (rare)
              
                **Thyrotoxicosis:** Increased bone turnover raises ALP. Other signs present (tachycardia, weight loss, tremor).
                **Liver disease (early):** Cholestasis can raise ALP before bilirubin rises. Rare in isolation long-term.

          #### Red flags—investigate further:
          
            **Very high ALP (> 400 U/L):** Suggests Paget's or active bone disease. Image the skeleton if suspected.
            **Symptoms:** Bone pain, fractures, constitutional symptoms.
            **Signs of liver disease:** Jaundice, spider naevi, hepatomegaly, ascites (though transaminases and bilirubin should be normal).
            **Unexplained weight loss, fever:** Raises concern for malignancy.
            **Young age with marked elevation:** Paget's is rare < 50 years; consider other bone pathology or familial hypophosphatasia (very rare).

          🔍
          How to investigate isolated ALP elevation
          ▼

          #### Step 1: Clinical assessment (always first)

              1
              **Age?** If paediatric (< 20) or pregnant, likely physiological. Reassure and recheck in 3 months if asymptomatic.

              2
              **Symptoms?** Bone pain, fractures, constitutional symptoms (fever, weight loss, night sweats)? If yes, investigate further.

              3
              **Signs of liver disease?** Jaundice, hepatomegaly, spider naevi? (Unlikely if transaminases and bilirubin are normal, but check clinically.)

              4
              **Medications?** Phenytoin, carbamazepine, rifampicin can induce bone ALP. Review drug chart.

              5
              **History of fractures, malignancy, or thyroid disease?** Context guides further investigation.

          #### Step 2: Biochemistry (targeted, not reflexive)
          
            **Repeat fasting ALP:** Intestinal ALP can be transiently elevated postprandially. A fasting sample helps confirm true elevation.
            **ALP isoenzymes (bone vs. liver):** Only if ALP is markedly elevated (> 300 U/L) AND symptomatic or young. Specific for source, but expensive and rarely changes management in primary care.
            **Bone markers (P1NP, CTX):** Reflect bone turnover. Not routinely needed; useful if considering osteoporosis management.
            **Calcium, phosphate, magnesium:** Check if bone disease suspected (abnormalities uncommon unless advanced disease).
            **Vitamin D:** Low levels can drive secondary hyperparathyroidism and bone turnover. Reasonable to check.
            **TSH:** If older adult or symptoms of thyroid dysfunction.
            **Albumin:** Normal ALP is lower-bound corrected for albumin; hypalbuminaemia can falsely lower reported ALP.

          #### Step 3: Imaging (only if indicated)
          
            **Skeletal X-rays:** If Paget's suspected (markedly elevated ALP + bone pain + older age). Characteristic findings confirm diagnosis.
            **DEXA scan:** If osteoporosis assessment is the goal (separate indication).
            **Abdominal ultrasound:** Only if clinical liver disease is suspected (despite normal transaminases/bilirubin) OR if cholestasis marker GGT is also elevated.

          #### Step 4: When to refer
          
            **Paget's disease:** Refer to endocrinologist or rheumatologist for management (bisphosphonates).
            **Suspected metastatic bone disease:** Refer to oncology.
            **Marked elevation (ALP > 400 U/L) without diagnosis:** Consider specialist input, though often still benign.
            **Associated abnormalities:** If GGT is also elevated, imaging abnormal, or liver disease signs appear, refer to hepatology.

          💊
          Management in primary care
          ▼

          #### For asymptomatic, young, isolated ALP elevation:
          
            **Reassurance:** Most common scenario. Explain that isolated ALP elevation is usually benign, especially with normal transaminases and bilirubin.
            **Repeat testing:** Recheck ALP in 3 months. Most benign causes resolve or stabilise. Persistently elevated ALP is still usually benign but warrants further thought.
            **Vitamin D assessment:** Consider checking vitamin D; if low (< 30 nmol/L), supplementation may reduce bone turnover.
            **Lifestyle:** Ensure adequate calcium intake (1000–1200 mg/day), weight-bearing exercise, avoid smoking. These support bone health regardless.

          #### For symptomatic or older patients:
          
            **Targeted investigation:** As per algorithm above. History and examination guide further workup.
            **Paget's disease (if diagnosed):** Refer for specialist management. Bisphosphonates are indicated for symptomatic or progressive disease.
            **Osteoporosis (if suspected):** DEXA scan; consider treatment depending on T-score and fracture risk.

          #### Follow-up strategy:
          
            **Normal initial assessment:** Repeat LFTs in 3 months. If stable and asymptomatic, no further investigation; reassure and discharge from clinic.
            **Persistent or rising ALP:** Consider bone-specific isoenzyme testing; if still elevated and diagnosis unclear, refer for specialist assessment.
            **New symptoms or signs:** Revisit diagnosis; proceed with imaging or referral as appropriate.

          ⭐
          Clinical pearls and pitfalls
          ▼

          #### Pearls:
          
            **Isolation is reassuring:** Normal transaminases and bilirubin make hepatocellular or cholestatic liver disease very unlikely. ALP is a "marker" enzyme, not specific for disease.
            **Age context matters enormously:** A 16-year-old with ALP 250 U/L is almost certainly physiological. A 60-year-old with the same value needs investigation.
            **GGT is the key discriminator:** If GGT is **normal** with elevated ALP, the elevation is likely bone or intestinal (not hepatic cholestasis).
            **Paget's disease is rare:** But worth recognising: very high ALP (> 500 U/L), bone pain, older age, deformity. X-rays are diagnostic.
            **Over-investigation is common and unhelpful:** Many labs reflex to extensive testing when ALP is elevated. In primary care, thoughtful clinical assessment prevents unnecessary investigation.

          #### Pitfalls:
          
            **Assuming ALP elevation = liver disease:** It doesn't. Check transaminases and bilirubin first. If normal, the elevation is usually not from the liver.
            **Forgetting paediatric physiology:** Young people have naturally higher ALP; it's normal and doesn't require investigation unless extreme or symptomatic.
            **Missing pregnancy:** Always check pregnancy status in women of childbearing age. ALP elevation is expected and benign.
            **Ignoring symptoms:** Bone pain, recent fractures, weight loss, or fever should always prompt further investigation, even with normal transaminases.
            **Requesting isoenzymes reflexively:** These are expensive and rarely change management in primary care. Reserve for markedly elevated ALP with diagnostic uncertainty.

          #### What not to do:
          
            - Don't start investigating extensively for hepatocellular carcinoma or cirrhosis based on elevated ALP alone. Normal transaminases make liver disease very unlikely.
            - Don't repeat testing every month. Three months is a reasonable interval for reassessment in asymptomatic patients.
            - Don't prescribe vitamin D supplementation without checking baseline vitamin D first (though it's often reasonable).

        ## Quick scenarios

            #### 16-year-old, ALP 280 U/L, asymptomatic
            **Diagnosis:** Physiological (growth).
            **Action:** Reassure. Recheck in 6 months if concerned, otherwise discharge.

            #### 45-year-old, ALP 150 U/L, normal LFTs, no symptoms
            **Diagnosis:** Likely benign (bone, intestinal, or isoform variant).
            **Action:** Repeat ALP in 3 months. If stable and asymptomatic, reassure and discharge.

            #### 62-year-old, ALP 420 U/L, normal LFTs, bone pain
            **Diagnosis:** Suspect Paget's disease.
            **Action:** Check GGT. Image pelvis and spine. Refer if Paget's confirmed.

            #### 35-year-old woman, ALP 190 U/L, positive pregnancy test
            **Diagnosis:** Pregnancy-related (placental ALP).
            **Action:** Reassure. Will normalise post-delivery. Recheck postpartum if concerned.
