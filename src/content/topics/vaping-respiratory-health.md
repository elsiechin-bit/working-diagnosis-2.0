## The frame: harm reduction vs uncertainty
      Vaping emerged as a smoking cessation tool. For some people—entrenched smokers with failed quit attempts—switching to vaping reduces tobacco combustion toxins. That's harm reduction. But harm reduction doesn't equal safety. It means less bad, not not-bad. The industry sells it as consequence-free. The evidence says otherwise.

      ## What we know about the lungs
      
      ### Acute effects (weeks to months)
      
        **Cough, throat irritation, wheeze:** Propylene glycol (humectant in e-liquid) is irritating. Some users develop "vaper's cough"—takes weeks to settle if they quit. Inhaled over months/years, chronic airway irritation.
        **Bronchial hyperresponsiveness:** Vapers show increased airway reactivity on spirometry (similar pattern to early smoking damage). Reversible if stopping, but evidence for progression unclear.
        **Airway inflammation:** Cellular studies show vaping triggers inflammatory responses in airways. Whether this progresses to COPD-like disease long-term is still unknown (cohort studies ongoing).

      ### Longer-term concerns (1+ years)
      
        **Popcorn lung (bronchiolitis obliterans):** Diacetyl (flavouring chemical) caused this in factory workers inhaling it occupationally. Found in some e-liquids, especially butter-flavour vapes. Rare in vapers, but case reports exist. Irreversible airways disease if it develops.
        **Lipid pneumonia:** Case reports of lipoid pneumonia (exogenous lipid aspiration) in vapers using vitamin E acetate or certain oils. Presents as persistent cough, dyspnea, hypoxia. Rare but serious.
        **Nicotine dependence:** Vaping nicotine creates the same addiction as smoking. For ex-smokers using vaping to quit, this can become indefinite nicotine replacement without planned exit strategy.

      ### Secondhand exposure
      
        - Exhaled aerosol contains nicotine, formaldehyde (higher concentrations than fresh air), ultrafine particles, and volatile organic compounds. Not "just water vapour."
        - Pregnant partners exposed to secondhand vape show elevated maternal nicotine levels (placental exposure).
        - Children in homes with vaping parents have elevated urinary cotinine.

        Red flag: Vaping with asthma or COPD
        Even if switching from smoking, propylene glycol + nicotine can worsen airways in susceptible individuals. Monitor spirometry; consider structured cessation support rather than ongoing vaping.

      ## Cardiovascular effects (emerging concern)
      
        **Nicotine absorption:** Vapers absorb comparable nicotine to smokers (sometimes higher, especially with high-strength liquid). Nicotine increases heart rate and BP acutely; chronic effects unclear but concerning given smoking history.
        **Endothelial dysfunction:** Short-term studies show vaping impairs endothelial vasodilation (similar to smoking). Long-term atherosclerotic progression unclear.
        **Thrombotic risk:** Limited data, but nicotine is prothrombotic. Ex-smokers switching to long-term vaping may retain elevated clotting risk.

      ## The cessation conversation
      Vaping for smoking cessation has two possible paths: transition to vaping, then quit nicotine entirely; or indefinite vaping as nicotine replacement. The first requires a plan. Most people don't have one.

      ### If someone vaped to quit smoking (and succeeded):
      
        - "That's great—the difficult part was breaking the smoking habit. Now the question is: are you planning to wean off nicotine altogether, or continue vaping indefinitely?"
        - If continuing indefinitely: discuss long-term risks (respiratory, cardiovascular, nicotine dependence perpetuation). NRT (patch) may be lower-risk alternative.
        - If planning to quit: discuss timeline. Gradual nicotine reduction (lower-strength liquid) vs cold turkey. Behavioural support helps.

      ### If someone vapes but never smoked:
      
        - This is new—adolescents are experimenting with vaping without smoking history. Data on non-smokers vapers is sparse but concerning: they're inhaling irritants, nicotine, and unknowns without the "safer than smoking" justification.
        - Cessation should be straightforward (no competing nicotine dependence from smoking), but vaping's modern marketing (sleek devices, flavours, social ritual) makes quitting psychologically complex.

      ## Unregulated market risks
      NZ vape products aren't as tightly regulated as pharmaceuticals. E-liquids vary in quality. Illicit vapes may contain contaminants, heavy metals (found in some products), or undisclosed substances. Recent EVALI outbreak in US (vaping-associated lung injury) traced to THC vapes with vitamin E acetate—underscores that unregulated market = unknown risks.

      Ask: "Where do you get your vape juice? Is it from a regulated NZ supplier or online?" Homemade liquids are a particular risk (unknown proportions, contamination).

      ## Clinical approach
      
      ### For current vapers:
      
        **Ask about smoking history:** Are they ex-smokers (harm reduction context) or never-smokers (different risk calculus)?
        **Screen for respiratory symptoms:** Persistent cough, dyspnea, wheeze. If present, spirometry + consider cessation support.
        **Discuss duration & intensity:** How long? Daily or occasional? High-nicotine liquid? (Pods can deliver 40+ mg nicotine per pod.)
        **Cessation planning:** If open to quitting, structured approach (NRT, behavioral support, gradual reduction).

      ### For ex-smokers considering vaping as "safer":
      
        - It's harm reduction compared to smoking, not safety clearance.
        - If struggling with smoking cessation, evidence-based approaches (varenicline, NRT, behavioral therapy) are better-studied than vaping for long-term quit success.

      ### For adolescents/non-smokers:
      
        - Vaping nicotine poses unknown respiratory & cardiovascular risks without the "quit smoking" justification.
        - Nicotine addiction during brain development (prefrontal cortex still developing to age 25) may have long-term cognitive effects (evidence from animal studies; human data limited).

      ## The honest conversation
      Vaping is a harm reduction tool for committed smokers. It's not a health product. It's not consequence-free. Long-term respiratory and cardiovascular safety are unknown. If someone's using it to quit smoking, great—but plan the exit. If someone's using it long-term instead of smoking, that's a win, but monitor. If someone's never smoked and vaping, that's not reducible harm—that's creating risk where none existed.

      Marketing says "safer." Evidence says "less understood."
