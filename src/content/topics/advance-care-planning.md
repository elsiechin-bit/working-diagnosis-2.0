## Overview


The conversation that should have happened didn't, and now the patient is in ICU intubated, the family is divided, the partner is sure she wouldn't have wanted this, the daughter is sure she would. Nobody has the document. The patient cannot be asked.

        Some version of this scene plays out in NZ hospitals every week. The work to prevent it is done - when it is done at all - months or years earlier, in primary care, in conversations that take time we don't have, with patients who often don't want to be having them, in a system that doesn't reward them. Advance care planning is one of the things general practice is uniquely placed to do, and one of the things general practice consistently fails to do at scale. The reasons are structural as much as clinical, but the conversation itself, when it happens well, is durable and consequential.

        ## A brief history: how we got here

        Advance care planning is a twentieth and twenty-first century invention. It didn't exist a century ago because the context that makes it necessary didn't exist. In 1920, people died of things - infection, organ failure, exsanguination - and when they died, they were dead. The conversation about what you would want if you were dying was not a necessary conversation because there were no medical interventions that could sustain a dying person indefinitely in a state between life and something else.

        The technology of mechanical ventilation, introduced during the polio epidemics of the 1950s, changed this. For the first time, machines could breathe for people whose lungs had failed. Suddenly, there was a question: should we use the machine? And if we do use it, when do we stop? The Karen Ann Quinlan case in 1976 - a 21-year-old woman in persistent vegetative state after unknown cause of coma, whose parents wanted to withdraw life support, whose hospital refused, who ended up in the New Jersey courts - made this question unavoidable.

        The Quinlan decision established a legal principle: families could make decisions to withdraw life-sustaining treatment on behalf of patients who could no longer decide for themselves. But it also revealed a problem: the families had to guess what the patient would have wanted. They didn't know because nobody had asked. Subsequent legal and ethical developments - the US President's Commission for the Study of Ethical Problems in Medicine and Biomedical and Behavioral Research (1983), the passage of the Patient Self-Determination Act in the US (1991), the emergence of "living wills" and "advance directives" as legal documents - all emerged from this basic recognition: if we have the technology to sustain life indefinitely, we need to know what people actually want.

        New Zealand followed a similar trajectory. The Health and Disability Commissioner was established in 1994, and the Code of Health and Disability Services Consumers' Rights in 1996 explicitly recognised the right to refuse treatment, including life-sustaining treatment. But the institutional structures to make this easy - the forms, the training, the digital systems to make advance directives accessible - took much longer to develop. The Te Whatu Ora ACP programme (moved from HQSC in 2024) provides national resources and the standardised ACP document, but primary care adoption remains patchy.

        The Detering 2010 trial, showing that facilitated advance care planning reduced family anxiety and improved concordance between patient wishes and care received, was the turning point that shifted ACP from a legal afterthought to a clinical intervention with evidence of benefit. But even now, more than a decade later, most patients in NZ who die in hospitals do not have an advance care plan on file.

        ## Why it matters

        The case for advance care planning is empirical, relational, and ethical, and the strongest version threads all three.

        The empirical case rests substantially on the Detering 2010 BMJ randomised controlled trial - 309 medical inpatients aged 80 or over at Austin Health in Melbourne, randomised to facilitated advance care planning or usual care, followed for six months or until death 1. The intervention group's wishes were much more likely to be known and followed at end of life. Bereaved families in the intervention group experienced significantly less anxiety, depression, and post-traumatic stress in the months following the patient's death 1. The effect sizes were not subtle. Subsequent systematic reviews and meta-analyses across multiple healthcare contexts have largely confirmed the direction of these findings: ACP improves concordance between patient wishes and care received, reduces unwanted intensive treatment at end of life, and reduces psychological morbidity in surviving family 2.

        The relational case is the part that the empirical literature can't fully capture. People who have explicit conversations with their families about what they would and wouldn't want, recorded in some form, leave their families with something. People who don't, leave their families with the work of guessing - and the lifelong uncertainty about whether they got the guess right. The 82-year-old whose adult children are still arguing seven years after her death about whether they should have agreed to the second cycle of chemotherapy is a recognisable figure. So is the partner who, eighteen months after deciding to withdraw the ventilator, is still wondering whether they did the right thing.

        The ethical case is the simplest. Patients are entitled to have their preferences known and respected. The system cannot respect preferences it doesn't know about. The conversation is the mechanism.

        ## The legal architecture

        NZ advance care planning sits within a layered and sometimes ambiguous legal framework. Understanding the key pieces is essential for practice, because the law defines what can be done, what cannot be done, what is binding, and what remains discretionary.

        ### The Code of Health and Disability Services Consumers' Rights

        The Code, enforced by the Health and Disability Commissioner, applies to all health and disability services and gives patients explicit rights to information, informed consent, and refusal of treatment 3. Right 7(2) guarantees the right to refuse any medical treatment, including life-sustaining treatment, and to be fully informed of the implications. Right 7(5) specifically recognises advance directives - written or oral instructions about future treatment - as binding on health practitioners.

        The Code is the foundational rights framework for any health interaction in NZ. It establishes that patients have the right to decide what happens to their bodies, and that this right does not end when they lose capacity - it is exercised through advance directives, through substitute decision-makers, or through the person's known values and preferences.

        ### The Protection of Personal and Property Rights Act 1988

        The PPPR Act is the central statute for substitute decision-making when a person loses capacity. It establishes the framework for Enduring Powers of Attorney (EPA), which come in two forms: EPA for property (managing assets), and EPA for personal care and welfare (PCW) 4. The personal care and welfare EPA is the relevant one for medical decision-making.

        An EPA-PCW comes into effect only when the person has been assessed as lacking capacity for the relevant decision. There is no requirement that this assessment be done by a doctor, though in practice it often is. The EPA document must be signed by the person with capacity and witnessed by a lawyer or other prescribed witness. The document itself can specify when the EPA comes into effect - it can be immediate (useful for property and day-to-day decisions) or conditional on loss of capacity (more common for PCW).

        **Critical limitation:** An EPA-PCW attorney cannot refuse standard medical treatment intended to save life or prevent serious damage to health 45. This is a statutory limitation written into section 14 of the PPPR Act. It means that even if the patient, when competent, said they would not want CPR, and even if they appointed an attorney with explicit instruction not to consent to CPR, the attorney cannot actually prevent CPR if a clinician believes it is indicated to save life.

        This limitation has generated significant legal and ethical debate. It reflects a policy judgment that substituted decision-making should not be allowed to refuse "standard" life-saving treatment. But "standard" is itself contested. Is artificial nutrition and hydration "standard"? Is ventilation? Is ECMO? The Act doesn't define it, and case law is sparse.

        In practice, the way around this limitation is the advance directive. An advance directive is not made through an EPA framework; it is made directly to clinicians and is given different legal weight.

        ### Advance directives: the binding instruction

        An advance directive is a written or oral instruction made by a competent person about future treatment they want or don't want. It is different from an EPA and more powerful in a specific way: it can refuse treatment that an EPA attorney cannot refuse.

        Advance directives are recognised under right 7(5) of the Code and are binding on health practitioners, provided four conditions are met 3:

          - The directive must apply to the situation that has actually arisen. A directive that says "I do not want CPR if I have advanced dementia" does not apply if the person is in acute respiratory failure from pneumonia without dementia. The situation must match.
          - The person must have had capacity when they made the directive. This is usually assumed if the directive is recent and clearly articulated, but can be challenged if there is evidence of mental illness, delusion, or diminished capacity at the time.
          - The directive must have been made of the person's own free will. If there is evidence of coercion or undue influence, it can be challenged.
          - The person must have been adequately informed at the time. If the person did not understand the implications of refusing treatment, the directive may not be binding.

        The threshold for treating an advance directive as binding is therefore not trivial - it has to fit the situation closely enough that the clinician can be confident the patient meant this situation. A clinician who is uncertain whether a directive applies can, in principle, seek a court order. In practice, this rarely happens; instead, clinicians struggle with the uncertainty and often resolve it in favour of treatment when in doubt.

        The distinction between an advance directive and an EPA is important. A directive is a specific instruction about what not to do in specified circumstances. An EPA is a delegation of authority to a person to make decisions on your behalf when you lose capacity. A directive can refuse treatment that an EPA attorney cannot refuse. But a directive is situation-specific; it doesn't give the attorney authority to make novel decisions in situations you didn't specifically anticipate.

        The ideal arrangement, from a legal standpoint, is to have both: an advance directive that specifies what you don't want in clearly defined scenarios, and an EPA-PCW that appoints someone to make decisions about other matters according to your values and best interests. The directive covers the specific refusals; the EPA covers the rest.

        ### Capacity and the point of no return

        Capacity is the legal and clinical threshold that separates the person who can consent or refuse from the person who cannot. Under the PPPR Act, capacity is decision-specific: a person can have capacity for some decisions and not others. Someone with early dementia might have capacity to decide about a blood test but not about a complex surgical decision requiring understanding of risk.

        The assessment of capacity is usually done by the clinician involved in the decision. It is not a formal process requiring specialist assessment (except in disputed cases). A clinician can simply document: "The patient understands what I am proposing. She understands the alternatives. She understands the risks. She can express a preference. She appears to understand what the implications are. I am satisfied she has capacity for this decision."

        Once capacity is lost, it is very difficult to recover it in the legal sense, even if the clinical condition improves. An advance directive made before capacity is lost is binding. A wish expressed after capacity is lost is not binding, even if it is expressed clearly.

        ### The End of Life Choice Act 2019

        This Act, which came into force on 7 November 2021, established assisted dying as a legal option in NZ for eligible patients, administered by the SCENZ Group (Specialist Clinicians Ending and Caring via Noncriminal Exit) 6. The eligibility criteria are stringent: NZ citizenship or permanent residence, age 18 or over, terminal illness with prognosis of less than six months, advanced irreversible physical decline, unbearable suffering not relievable in a tolerable manner, and capacity to make an informed decision 6.

        For primary care, the critical restriction is section 8: health practitioners are prohibited from initiating discussion of assisted dying with a patient 6. The patient must raise it first. Only then can a clinician provide information or refer to SCENZ. Conscientious objection is protected, and a practitioner who objects must inform the patient of SCENZ as the route to access the service.

        This legal restriction sits awkwardly alongside the clinical reality that ACP conversations naturally cover the full range of end-of-life options. A patient in an ACP discussion might ask, "Is assisted dying something I could choose?" Under section 8, you can answer fully. But you cannot raise the possibility yourself, even if you think it might be relevant to their situation.

        The 2024 review of the End of Life Choice Act considered whether section 8 should be amended to allow health practitioners to inform patients about assisted dying as part of routine ACP discussions, particularly for patients with terminal illness. The review recommended no change, reasoning that the prohibition protects vulnerable patients from subtle pressure and preserves the patient-initiated approach 7. But the restriction remains controversial among clinicians who think it artificially constrains honest ACP conversations.

        ### Health information and clinical records

        An advance directive or ACP document recorded in a GP practice system is a health record and is subject to the Health Information Privacy Code 8. This means: the patient has the right to access it, to have it amended if inaccurate, and to have their privacy protected. It also means that if a patient asks for an ACP to be removed from their record or for certain information in it to be deleted, the practice must comply with privacy law.

        The problem is that advance directives are most useful when other clinicians can access them. A hospital ED, an after-hours clinic, an ambulance service - these need to know what the patient decided. But they often cannot access information held in a primary care system. This gap is one of the most persistent practical problems with NZ's advance care planning system. The solution is to ensure the patient has a copy, gives copies to family members, and that a copy is sent to the relevant specialist or facility. Digital integration remains incomplete.

        ### Disputes and the courts

        When there is disagreement about what the patient would have wanted, or whether an advance directive applies, or whether a substitute decision-maker is acting in the patient's best interests, the matter can be taken to the courts. The High Court has jurisdiction under the PPPR Act to make orders about personal care and welfare decisions.

        In practice, court orders are rare and slow. The case that does reach court - where the family is divided, where there is genuine uncertainty about what the patient would have wanted, where the stakes are high - often gets resolved through the court process itself, by forcing explicit articulation of values and evidence. But the time taken (weeks or months) makes court orders impractical for acute end-of-life decisions.

        ## Cultural frameworks and advance care planning

        ACP frameworks designed for individual-autonomy-based decision-making translate imperfectly to cultural contexts where end-of-life decisions are properly held by whānau, by collective, or by spiritual frameworks the standard framework doesn't acknowledge. NZ is a multicultural society where different cultural traditions carry fundamentally different assumptions about who decides, what matters, and how death should be approached.

        ### Māori health and decision-making frameworks

        The Te Whare Tapa Whā model, developed by Sir Mason Durie, frames health as a balance of four dimensions - taha tinana (physical), taha hinengaro (mental and emotional), taha whānau (family and social), and taha wairua (spiritual).9 For ACP, the implication is that a conversation focused narrowly on physical-treatment preferences misses three of the four dimensions that the patient and whānau may understand to be central to their end-of-life experience.

        **Key characteristics of Māori end-of-life decision-making:**

          - Whānau is the decision-making unit - medical decisions are collective, not individual
          - Tikanga (cultural protocols) around death, dying, karakia (prayers), and tangihanga (funeral rites) carry spiritual and cultural meaning inseparable from medical decisions
          - Wairua (spiritual dimension) is part of medicine - not "non-medical" but essential clinical information
          - Connection to whenua (land) and tīpuna (ancestors) shapes what a good death looks like
          - Ability to be home, buried in family urupā (burial ground), and maintain connection to place matters as much as physical comfort

        ### Pacific cultural perspectives

        Pacific communities in Aotearoa - Tongan, Samoan, Niuean, Cook Islands Māori, Tokelauan, Fijian, and others - bring distinct but often overlapping cultural frameworks to end-of-life care.

        **Key characteristics of Pacific end-of-life decision-making:**

          - Family-centred decision-making - family consensus and elders' wishes carry weight alongside individual preference
          - Faith is often central to decision-making - religious leaders and spiritual advisors may be as important as medical teams
          - Strong obligation to pursue life-extending treatment, particularly for elders - withdrawing treatment may be experienced as abandonment
          - End-of-life care understood through legacy and obligation - what the person has contributed, who depends on them, shapes the decision
          - Relational and social dimensions are as much part of medical conversation as physical symptoms

        ### Chinese and East Asian perspectives

        Chinese, Vietnamese, Korean, and other East Asian communities in NZ often bring Confucian, Buddhist, or Taoist frameworks to end-of-life decision-making.

        **Key characteristics of East Asian end-of-life decision-making:**

          - Filial piety and obligation - duty of adult children to care for aging parents is absolute; eldest son or daughter may take primary decision-making role
          - Avoiding explicit discussion of death is culturally appropriate - talking about death while person is alive may be considered harmful or unlucky
          - Harmony and balance (yin-yang) - aggressive medical intervention late in life may work against natural process; peaceful, natural death aligned with one's time is preferred
          - Continuation and remembrance matter - how person is remembered, whether wishes are honored, maintaining connection after death carries importance
          - Indirect communication about concerns is normal - silence may mask discomfort; separate family conversations may reveal hidden concerns

        ### Middle Eastern, North African, and Islamic perspectives

        Islamic teachings and cultural traditions from these communities shape end-of-life care in specific ways.

        **Key characteristics of Islamic and cultural perspectives on end-of-life:**

          - Sanctity of life - life is sacred (haram) and only Allah determines when death comes; strong obligation to preserve life and pursue medical treatment
          - Withdrawing or refusing treatment can be religiously problematic for families - consulting the imam may be essential to decision-making
          - Family-centred with defined gender roles - elder male family members, spouse, and adult children; women's voices present but structured differently
          - Ritual and spiritual practices are not optional - ablution (wudu), prayer (salat), Qur'an recitation, and facing Mecca are religious obligations
          - Rapid preparation for burial - Islamic practice prioritizes burial within 24 hours; delays for autopsy or post-mortem create serious family distress and religious conflict
          - Modesty and gender restrictions - care of body (particularly for women) restricted to same-sex carers; ritual washing (ghusl) must be done according to Islamic practice

        ### Christian perspectives (diverse traditions)

        Christian faith in NZ encompasses Catholic, Anglican, Pentecostal, Methodist, Presbyterian, and other traditions with somewhat different end-of-life perspectives.

        **Key characteristics of Christian perspectives:**

          **Catholic and conservative Protestant:** Sanctity of life - extraordinary means should be used to preserve it, but doctrine distinguishes "ordinary" (obligatory) from "extraordinary" (optional) means
          **General Christian:** Hope in resurrection and afterlife - focus is on spiritual preparation for death; pastoral presence, anointing of sick, confession, and communion carry importance equal to medical intervention
          **Pentecostal and some traditions:** Suffering may be spiritually meaningful - aggressive pain relief or sedation may interfere with spiritual process
          **Pentecostal and charismatic:** Prayer for healing is prioritized - families may continue medical intervention alongside spiritual prayer, hoping for divine intervention
          **Protestant traditions:** Individual conscience and personal faith commitment emphasized - end-of-life decisions more individually oriented but still informed by faith understanding

        ### Secular and humanist perspectives

        A significant minority of New Zealanders identify as non-religious or secular.

        **Key characteristics of secular perspectives:**

          - Autonomy-centred - individual's choices and self-determination take precedence over family consensus or religious obligation
          - Quality of life over quantity - shorter, conscious, pain-free period often preferred to extended unconscious or dependent survival
          - Rational evaluation of treatment burden - interventions evaluated on practical, evidence-based grounds; side effects and functional impact matter
          - Open to end-of-life choice - more receptive to discussing assisted dying and medical aid in dying without religious objections

        ### Practical implications for ACP conversations

        **Ask about cultural and spiritual practices that matter.** "What tikanga, cultural practices, spiritual beliefs, or family traditions are important to you at the end of life?" This opens space for the patient to articulate what matters without the GP needing to be expert in every tradition.

        **Understand the difference between individual choice and family obligation.** For some patients and families, the question is not "what do you want?" but "what does our family need? What is right for us?" Honoring this difference is not a failure to support patient autonomy; it is a failure of respect if you treat it as deviation from an individualist model.

        **Don't assume silence means consent.** In some cultural contexts, disagreement is expressed indirectly or not at all. Follow-up conversations with family members separately or asking "Is there anything about this plan that worries you?" can reveal hidden concerns.

        **Timing and setting matter.** A conversation in a busy clinic with an interpreter on the phone, no family present, produces very different results from one at home, with key family members present, in the patient's first language. The second conversation will be more accurate and durable.

        **Work with cultural brokers and community leaders.** For some communities, the GP's relationship with the patient may be less influential than the patient's relationship with their religious leader, community elder, or cultural advisor. Involving these people (with patient permission) in the ACP conversation strengthens rather than undermines the clinical work.

        ## Clinical case study 1: The straightforward conversation

        A 78-year-old woman with stable COPD comes in for a routine review. She has had COPD for fifteen years, is on a combination of inhaled corticosteroid and long-acting beta agonist, has had two hospitalisations in the last five years, and is functionally limited to short walks around the house. You have been her GP for eight years and have a good relationship.

        You say: "I'd like to have a conversation about what would matter to you if your breathing got worse. Both so I know how to look after you, and so your family knows what you would want." She is quiet for a moment, then says, "I've been wondering when you'd ask me that."

        You go through the ACP document together. She tells you: Her understanding of COPD is reasonably accurate - she knows it's progressive, knows there's no cure, knows that acute infections can be life-threatening. What matters most to her is being at home, not in hospital. She wants to see her grandchildren and be present for important family events. She doesn't want to be on a ventilator - "I don't want to be kept alive by a machine." She would want antibiotics for a chest infection if it happens, and would want pain relief, but wouldn't want ICU care.

        You ask about CPR. She says, "No. I'm too old and too tired for that." You explain that if her heart stops, CPR involves rib fractures and a breathing tube, and for someone in her situation, the chance of it resulting in her being able to go home and do the things she currently does is very small. She nods and says that confirms what she was thinking.

        You ask who she'd want to speak for her if she couldn't make decisions. She says her daughter, who is present at the appointment. You ask the daughter if she's willing, and she says yes. You document all of this in the ACP form, and give her a copy. You also send it to her respiratory specialist and to the local after-hours clinic. You make a note to review it in two years, or sooner if her condition changes significantly.

        Six months later, she is admitted to hospital with a chest infection. She is breathless and anxious, but she is clear. Her daughter gives the hospital her copy of the ACP document. The hospital doctors have her wishes on file. She is treated with antibiotics and oxygen, feels better after three days, and goes home. The infection is managed without discussion of ventilation or ICU because everyone knows that's not what she would want.

        This is the conversation working as intended.

        ## Clinical case study 2: The complicated conversation

        An 81-year-old man with metastatic prostate cancer comes in for a review. He has known about the cancer for three years and has been on active treatment - initially androgen deprivation therapy, then chemotherapy. His PSA has recently started rising again despite chemotherapy, and his oncologist has suggested that further chemotherapy is unlikely to be effective. The conversation with the oncologist was brief and left him uncertain about prognosis.

        He comes to you angry. He says: "I want to try everything. I don't want to give up." You take time to explore what he understands. It turns out he thinks "further chemotherapy" means there are other chemotherapy options, and he wants those options explored. He doesn't actually understand that his cancer is now resistant, that his prognosis is in the order of months, or that his oncologist is suggesting a shift from curative to comfort-focused care.

        You spend forty minutes with him and his wife. You go back to basics. You explain what stage 4 cancer means. You talk about what the rising PSA means. You explain that the goal of treatment has shifted from trying to cure the cancer to managing his symptoms and maintaining quality of life. You ask what he understands about his prognosis. He says, "So I might have a year?" You gently say, "The honest conversation is that your doctors are thinking more in terms of months than years. But there's uncertainty, and some people live longer."

        He gets quieter. His wife starts to cry. You give them space. After a while, he says, "I thought I'd have longer." You say, "I know. This is harder than you thought it would be." You ask if he would like to think about what would matter to him in this next phase - not because he needs to decide everything today, but because it's worth thinking about now, while he's well enough to think clearly.

        He says he wants to think about it, and he comes back in two weeks with his wife. This time, the conversation is different. He talks about wanting to see his grandson graduate in two months - that matters to him more than extending his life by a few weeks if it means being too unwell to attend. He talks about not wanting to be in pain. He doesn't want CPR. He would like to be at home, not in hospital, at the end. He's less certain about whether he would want chemotherapy in the next month if there was a chance it might help him live to see the graduation. You talk through the likely side effects, the small chance of benefit, and what "benefit" would mean for him. He decides to have a conversation with his oncologist about whether it's worth trying, with the clear understanding that if it's not working or making him feel worse, he will stop.

        This ACP captures not a fixed set of decisions, but a framework: what matters (seeing the grandson graduate), what he doesn't want (CPR, prolonged hospitalisation), and what remains negotiable (some interventions might be worth trying if they might help him get to what matters). The document is less useful here than the conversation itself - the conversation that reframes what he's trying to achieve.

        ## The CPR conversation

        This is the conversation most people associate with ACP, and it is also the one that is most often handled badly.

        Two errors are common. The first is collapsing all of advance care planning into the CPR question. Whether to attempt CPR is one decision in a much wider field of decisions about treatment goals, place of care, and what matters most. Asking "do you want CPR?" out of the blue, without the broader conversation, produces answers that don't mean what they appear to mean. Patients agreeing to CPR are often agreeing to "anything that might help"; patients declining CPR are sometimes declining "being kept alive on machines" - neither is the actual question.

        The second error is framing CPR as an option of equivalent likelihood of benefit to other interventions. Survival to hospital discharge after in-hospital CPR is in the order of 15-25% in unselected adult populations and substantially lower in older patients with multiple comorbidities, frailty, or terminal illness. For specific patient groups - advanced metastatic cancer, end-stage heart failure, severe frailty - meaningful survival with restoration of function is rare. The conversation that frames CPR as a routine option, rather than a specific intervention with specific likely outcomes for this patient, gives the patient an inaccurate picture of what they are choosing.

        A more honest version is: "If your heart stops, we can try to restart it with chest compressions and shocks. For a healthy person, that works about a quarter of the time. For someone in your situation, the chance of it working in a way that means you wake up and go home doing the things you currently do is much smaller - maybe 5% or less. And the process itself involves rib fractures, a breathing tube, and ICU. Knowing that, what would you want?"

        This is harder to say than "do you want CPR if your heart stops?", but it is more accurate to what is actually being decided. The "Allow Natural Death" framing - increasingly used in some NZ services as an alternative to "Do Not Attempt CPR" - is part of the same shift. The framing matters because the language matters. "Do not attempt CPR" sounds like withholding; "Allow natural death" sounds like permitting. The clinical decision is the same; the conversation about it lands differently.

        ## Making it work in practice

        **Start earlier than feels comfortable.** The standard prompt - when you would not be surprised if the patient died within the next twelve months - captures the patients who most need an ACP, but in practice, advance care planning works better when initiated earlier in a chronic illness trajectory, before the conversation is freighted with the proximity of death. Patients with new diagnoses of advanced heart failure, COPD, cirrhosis, dementia, or metastatic cancer should be offered the opportunity to think about an ACP, ideally in a planned follow-up rather than at the diagnostic appointment itself.

        **The substrate is the conversation, not the document.** The ACP document recorded on paper or in a practice management system is necessary but not sufficient. The conversation itself, witnessed by family and clinical team, is what produces durable understanding. The document records the outcome; the conversation produces the alignment. Both matter.

        **Document specifically, but don't fetishise the document.** The ACP form is a record of a conversation, not a substitute for it. Ensure that the document goes home with the patient (and their whānau), is sent to the relevant specialty teams, is uploaded into hospital systems where possible, and is reviewed and reconfirmed periodically - not just filed and forgotten.

        **Bring whānau in if the patient wants it.** The clinical work that gets done is more accurate, the family work that follows the death is easier, and the cultural work is less likely to be done badly.

        **Review periodically.** ACPs should be reviewed when circumstances change: new diagnosis, change in functional status, transition to residential care, after each significant hospital admission. A 75-year-old's preferences in 2020, before her dementia and her partner's death, may not be the preferences her 80-year-old self would now express. Make a habit of asking every twelve to eighteen months whether the document still reflects what the person wants.

        **Don't initiate discussion of assisted dying.** Section 8 of the End of Life Choice Act is clear, and the prohibition is binding on conscientious-objector and non-objector practitioners alike. If the patient raises it, you can engage fully. If they don't, you cannot be the one to bring it up, even if you suspect it might be relevant to their situation.

        ## Why the work matters

        The conversation that should have happened is the conversation that prevents the scene at the start - the patient in ICU, the family divided, the document not present, the wishes unknown. Each conversation that does happen is, in some small way, a future scene that doesn't.

        For working GPs, advance care planning is one of the few interventions where the evidence is clear, where the clinical work is within scope, where the patient wants it (once you've managed the awkwardness of initiating it), and where the downstream effects - less family conflict, less unwanted intensive treatment, fewer late-life regrets - are genuinely significant. It is also one of the interventions that practices most consistently fail to do at scale, because it takes time, it requires skill, and it doesn't generate income.

        But the work itself, when it is done well, is among the most important things general practice does.
