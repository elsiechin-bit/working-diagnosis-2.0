#### Diagnosis
          
            - Acute onset, signs of middle-ear effusion, and a bulging, erythaematous or perforated tympanic membrane with discharge
            - Erythaema alone (e.g. from crying or fever) is not enough - look for bulging or effusion
          
          #### Management
          
            **Analgesia first** - regular paracetamol or ibuprofen; most resolve within a few days
            **Antibiotics (amoxicillin)** are indicated for: children under 6 months; under 2 years with bilateral AOM; perforation with discharge; or systemic illness / not improving after 48 hours
            Otherwise a **delayed or no-antibiotic** approach with safety-netting is appropriate
            - High-risk children (including many Māori and Pacific children, and those with recurrent disease) warrant a lower threshold and follow-up
          
          #### Otitis media with effusion ("glue ear")
          
            - Common after AOM; usually resolves. Watchful waiting with hearing assessment; refer if persistent with hearing or developmental impact

        🗣️Sore throat / pharyngitis▾
        
          #### Viral vs group A streptococcus (GAS)
          
            - Most sore throats are viral. Scoring tools (Centor / FeverPAIN) estimate the chance of GAS but do not, on their own, decide antibiotics in low-risk groups
            - Cough, coryza and hoarseness point towards a viral cause
          
          #### Two pathways in New Zealand
          
            **Low rheumatic-fever risk:** manage symptomatically with analgesia and safety-netting; routine throat swabs and antibiotics are not needed
            **High rheumatic-fever risk** (Māori or Pacific peoples aged roughly 3-35 in higher-risk settings, or anyone with a personal/family history of rheumatic fever): take a throat swab and treat GAS sore throat with **10 days of oral amoxicillin or penicillin** to prevent acute rheumatic fever

      Rheumatic fever prevention - the NZ priorityAcute rheumatic fever remains a serious problem for Māori and Pacific children and young people in Aotearoa. In these high-risk groups, a GAS sore throat should be swabbed and treated with a full antibiotic course - this is the key exception to "most sore throats don't need antibiotics". Follow local HealthPathways and the Heart Foundation guidance.

      Don't miss complicationsMastoiditis (postauricular swelling, protruding ear), peritonsillar abscess / quinsy (trismus, "hot potato" voice, deviated uvula), stridor or airway compromise, and deep neck-space infection are emergencies needing urgent referral.
