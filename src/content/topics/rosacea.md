## Overview


**Rosacea is not acne.** It is a chronic vascular and inflammatory dermatosis characterised by centrofacial erythema, flushing, telangiectasia, and inflammatory papules/pustules. **2024–2025 paradigm shift:** Rosacea is now classified by phenotype (observable clinical features) rather than discrete subtypes, reflecting the spectrum of presentations.

        #### Clinical phenotypes (not mutually exclusive)
        
          **Persistent centrofacial erythema** – fixed redness, cheeks/nose/forehead, may be seasonal or year-round
          **Flushing** – transient intensification of erythema; triggered by heat, spicy food, alcohol, stress, temperature extremes
          **Papules & pustules** – sterile inflammatory lesions, resembling acne but without comedones; can be sparse to numerous
          **Telangiectasia** – visible dilated blood vessels; often perinasal, cheeks; may be cosmetically significant
          **Phymatous changes** – thickening, enlargement of nose, cheeks, forehead (especially in men); rhinophyma in severe cases
          **Ocular involvement** – conjunctival injection, lid margin telangiectasia, dry eye, blepharoconjunctivitis (up to 50% of patients)

          Diagnostic criteria (AAD/ROSCO 2025)
          **Rosacea diagnosed if:** (1) Fixed centrofacial erythema periodically intensifying, OR phymatous changes; OR (2) Two or more of: papules/pustules, flushing, telangiectasia, ocular manifestations.

        #### Pathophysiology (multifactorial, incompletely understood)
        
          **Vascular dysfunction:** Persistent vasomotor instability, reactive vasodilation; neoangiogenesis; endothelial dysfunction
          **Innate immunity dysregulation:** Elevated cathelicidins (antimicrobial peptides); abnormal TLR (toll-like receptor) signalling; abnormal complement cascade
          **Inflammation:** Dysregulated pro-inflammatory mediators (IL-6, IL-8, TNF-α); elevated VEGF (vascular endothelial growth factor)
          **Microbiota:** Increased Demodex mites, dysbiotic flora; role in triggering inflammation unclear
          **Environmental triggers:** UV exposure, temperature extremes, spicy foods, alcohol, emotional stress, wind

      💊Topical treatment hierarchy▾
      
        #### First-line: anti-inflammatory & antimicrobial topicals
        
          AgentMechanismEfficacy & notes
          
            **Metronidazole 0.75%**Antibiotic (antimicrobial peptide inhibition)Mild-moderate rosacea; reduces papules & erythema; well-tolerated; minimal systemic absorption
            **Azelaic acid 15–20%**Anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, depigmentingPapulopustular & erythematotelangiectatic; reduces Demodex; also effective in rosacea-related post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation
            **Ivermectin 1%**Antiparasitic, anti-inflammatory (TLR-mediated)Papulopustular & erythematotelangiectatic; emerging as first-line; superior to metronidazole in some trials
            **Topical minocycline foam 1.5%**Antibiotic (anti-inflammatory dose-dependent)Papulopustular; applied once daily; effective; avoids systemic absorption
            **Sulfacetamide 10%/sulfur 5%**Antimicrobial, keratolyticOlder agent; effective but cosmetic acceptability lower; odour, staining

        #### Vasomotor-targeted topicals
        
          **Brimonidine 0.33% gel:** α₂-adrenergic agonist; reduces flushing & erythema within 30 min; lasts ~12 h. Use for persistent erythema. Risk of rebound erythema if stopped abruptly; warn patients.
          **Oxymetazoline 1% cream:** α₁-adrenergic agonist; vasoconstrictor; reduces flushing & erythema; similar duration to brimonidine. Alternative to brimonidine; comparable efficacy.
        
        #### Adjunctive topicals
        
          **Benzoyl peroxide (microencapsulated):** Emerging evidence for anti-inflammatory & potential dysbiotic effects; used off-label, typically 2–5%
          **Retinoids (tretinoin, adapalene):** Off-label; may improve erythema & post-inflammatory changes; use with caution (irritation risk in rosacea)
          **Sunscreen (SPF 30+):** Essential; broad-spectrum UVA/B; daily use prevents flares
        
        #### Practical approach
        
          **Mild rosacea:** Topical agent alone (metronidazole, azelaic acid, or ivermectin)
          **Moderate:** Topical anti-inflammatory + brimonidine or oxymetazoline for flushing
          **Combination:** Two topical agents (e.g. ivermectin + brimonidine) or topical + oral if inadequate response

      💊Oral treatment & systemic options▾
      
        #### First-line: low-dose doxycycline
        
          **Doxycycline 50 mg once daily (sub-antimicrobial dose)** – most widely used; anti-inflammatory effects (inhibits matrix metalloproteinases, reduces pro-inflammatory cytokines); used for 12–16 weeks initially, then reassess for tapering or maintenance
          **Alternative oral tetracyclines:** Minocycline (50–100 mg daily; additional anti-inflammatory effects); tetracycline (500 mg daily, less preferred due to GI upset & food interactions)
          **Efficacy:** Reduces papules, pustules, and erythema; onset 4–12 weeks
          **Tolerability:** Generally excellent; photosensitivity (manage with sunscreen); esophageal irritation risk if taken upright; occasional vaginal candidiasis or C. difficile
        
        #### Second-line oral agents
        
          **Azithromycin:** Macrolide antibiotic; used off-label for anti-inflammatory effects; less evidence than tetracyclines; reserve for doxycycline contraindication or intolerance
          **Beta-blockers (propranolol, labetalol, carvedilol):** For flushing-predominant rosacea; off-label; modest evidence; consider if other options fail
          **JAK inhibitors (upadacitinib, tofacitinib):** Emerging 2024–2025; small trials show promise for rosacea phenotypes; not yet standard-of-care in most regions; check approvals & cost
        
        #### Isotretinoin (severe/refractory)
        
          **Reserved for severe, treatment-resistant phymatous rosacea** with poor prognosis despite topical/oral therapy
          **Dose & duration:** Low-dose protocols (0.25–0.5 mg/kg/day × 16–20 weeks); fewer adverse effects than acne protocols
          **Monitoring:** LFTs, lipids, teratogenicity (effective contraception required); depression risk

      🔥Laser & light-based therapies▾
      
        **Lasers and intense pulsed light (IPL) are the gold standard for vascular rosacea (telangiectasia, persistent erythema, flushing).** Mechanism: selective photothermolysis targeting oxyhemoglobin in dilated vessels.

        #### Laser & light modalities
        
          Laser typeWavelengthTarget / Clinical use
          
            **Pulsed dye laser (PDL)**595 nmGOLD STANDARD. Telangiectasia, erythema, flushing; excellent efficacy; most published data. Ultra-long-pulse duration reduces purpura.
            **Intense pulsed light (IPL)**500–1200 nmTelangiectasia, erythema, flushing; comparable efficacy to PDL; fewer adverse effects; useful for larger areas.
            **Potassium titanyl phosphate (KTP) 532 nm**532 nmTelangiectasia, erythema; comparable to PDL; shorter wavelength better for superficial vessels.
            **Long-pulse Nd:YAG 1064 nm**1064 nmDeeper vascular lesions, telangiectasia refractory to PDL; greater depth penetration; risk of purpura/scarring; reserve for appropriate cases.
            **Long-pulse Alexandrite 755 nm**755 nmEmerging for papulopustular rosacea (when combined with PDL); reduces follicular inflammation; limited evidence.
            **CO₂ & Er:YAG lasers**10,600 nm / 2,940 nmPhymatous rhinophyma; ablative resurfacing; dramatic improvement but longer downtime & scarring risk; reserve for severe cases.

        #### Treatment protocols & outcomes
        
          **Number of sessions:** Typically 2–4 treatments spaced 4–6 weeks apart; cumulative improvement
          **Efficacy:** 70–90% improvement in telangiectasia & erythema with PDL/IPL; flushing improvement variable
          **Downtime:** Transient purpura (PDL), erythema lasting hours to days; minimal with IPL/KTP
          **Side effects:** Purpura, transient erythema, hyperpigmentation (darker skin types), rarely textural changes or scarring
          **Maintenance:** Repeat treatments every 6–12 months; improvement often sustained with trigger avoidance & topical therapy
        
        #### Combination therapy emerging evidence
        Combining PDL + long-pulse Alexandrite laser (for papulopustular rosacea) shows synergistic benefit in recent studies; addresses both vascular and inflammatory components.

          NZ access & cost
          Laser therapy for rosacea is typically private; not funded by DHB/public dermatology in most regions. Cost $400–1500 NZD per session depending on area & laser type. Consider referring to dermatology with laser capability or specialized aesthetic centres.

      🎯Trigger identification & lifestyle management▾
      
        **Trigger avoidance is foundational.** Even with optimal pharmacotherapy, uncontrolled triggers limit efficacy.

        #### Common triggers (variable by patient)
        
          **Temperature:** Hot drinks (tea, coffee), hot water, saunas, heated spaces; cold wind; extreme temperature swings
          **Food & beverages:** Spicy foods (capsaicin), alcohol (especially red wine, beer), hot/warm foods
          **Environmental:** Wind, UV exposure, humidity fluctuations, air pollution
          **Emotional:** Stress, anxiety, embarrassment (feedback loop with flushing)
          **Physical exertion:** Exercise, strenuous activity (heat production)
          **Topical irritants:** Harsh cleansers, astringents, fragrance, menthol; acids (overuse); irritating cosmetics
        
        #### Management approach
        
          **Diary keeping:** Patient identifies personal triggers; highly individual (e.g. alcohol triggers some, not others)
          **Avoidance:** Practical strategies (room temperature drinks, stress management, appropriate sunscreen, gentle skincare)
          **Gentle cleansing:** Lukewarm water, fragrance-free gentle cleanser (avoid foaming/astringent products); pat dry gently
          **Sunscreen SPF 30+:** Broad-spectrum UVA/B; daily even indoors. Photosensitivity risk with doxycycline.
          **Moisturiser:** Fragrance-free; after damp skin to minimize irritation
          **Cosmetics & skincare products:** Avoid alcohol-based, heavily fragranced, or astringent formulations; use make-up only if necessary (mineral formulas often tolerated better)

      🔍Differential diagnosis & ocular involvement▾
      
        #### Common mimics
        
          **Acne:** Comedones absent in rosacea; age (rosacea 30+; acne teens–20s); distribution (rosacea centrofacial, cheeks/nose; acne often forehead/chin)
          **Seborrheic dermatitis:** Papules & erythema, but often scaly; responds to antifungals; distribution includes eyebrows, nasolabial folds
          **Contact dermatitis:** History of allergen exposure; demarcated lesions; eczematous appearance; pruritus prominent
          **Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE):** Malar rash, but photosensitive; arthralgia, systemic symptoms; ANA positive; distinct serology
          **Carcinoid syndrome:** Flushing; usually accompanying diarrhoea, wheezing; elevated 5-HIAA; rare
          **Perioral dermatitis:** Perioral papules; often from topical steroid overuse; typically young women; responds to tetracyclines
        
        #### Ocular rosacea
        Present in ~50% of rosacea patients; may be subclinical. **Symptoms:** Dry eye, grittiness, redness, lid margin inflammation, foreign-body sensation. **Signs:** Conjunctival telangiectasia, lid margin erythema, meibomian gland dysfunction. Rarely: blepharoconjunctivitis, iritis (severe). Always ask about eye involvement; refer to ophthalmology if symptoms or signs of keratitis.

      💡Clinical pearls & pitfalls▾
      
        #### Pearls
        
          ✦**Rosacea is phenotype-based, not subtypes.** Clinical features (erythema, flushing, papules, telangiectasia, phymatous, ocular) mix and match. Tailor treatment to patient's phenotype, not old "subtype" categories.
          ✦**Trigger identification is as important as pharmacotherapy.** Patient education on personal triggers often improves outcome more than adding another topical.
          ✦**Rosacea ≠ acne.** No comedones; erythema is persistent (not inflammatory acne-driven); demographic & distribution different. Avoid confusing with acne; misuse of acne treatments (benzaclin, isotretinoin at acne doses) can worsen rosacea.
          ✦**Pulsed dye laser is gold standard for telangiectasia & flushing.** If refractory to topical/oral, consider laser referral. Most effective vascular treatment available.
          ✦**Low-dose doxycycline is first-line oral agent.** Sub-antimicrobial dose (50 mg daily); anti-inflammatory mechanism; ~4–12 weeks to maximum effect. Not abrupt; taper gradually to assess relapse.
          ✦**Ivermectin is emerging as first-line topical.** Superior efficacy vs metronidazole in recent trials; anti-inflammatory + antiparasitic; well-tolerated. Consider as first choice.
          ✦**Flushing-predominant rosacea may need vasomotor agents.** Brimonidine or oxymetazoline address flushing when anti-inflammatory topicals insufficient. Risk of rebound erythema on discontinuation; warn patients.
          ✦**Ocular involvement is common and often subclinical.** Ask about dry eye, grittiness, lid redness. Refer to ophthalmology if concerning symptoms; may require systemic doxycycline + topical antibiotics.
          ✦**This is a lifelong, relapsing-remitting condition.** Set realistic expectations; no cure; maintenance therapy often needed indefinitely. Adjust based on flares & remissions.
        
        #### Pitfalls
        
          **Treating rosacea like acne.** Benzoyl peroxide, salicylic acid, isotretinoin at acne doses can worsen rosacea; choose appropriate anti-rosacea agents.
          **Overuse of topical corticosteroids.** Short-term use acceptable; chronic use leads to steroid rosacea & paradoxical worsening. Educate patients to avoid self-treating with OTC hydrocortisone long-term.
          **Ignoring trigger identification.** Prescribing medication alone without trigger discussion limits success.
          **Premature therapy cessation.** Doxycycline takes 4–12 weeks for maximum effect; don't abandon too early. Topical agents similarly need 8–12 weeks.
          **Missing ocular involvement.** Don't assume skin disease only; ask systematically about eye symptoms; refer when indicated.
          **Inadequate sun protection.** UV exposure is a major trigger; doxycycline increases photosensitivity. Counsel strict daily SPF 30+ use.
          **Brimonidine/oxymetazoline rebound risk not explained.** Warn patients about potential rebound erythema if stopped abruptly; taper gradually or transition to other agents.
