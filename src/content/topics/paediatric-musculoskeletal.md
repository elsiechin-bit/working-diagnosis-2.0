#### Red flags requiring urgent investigation
        
          **Fever with joint pain or bone pain:** Septic arthritis, osteomyelitis, septic bursitis until proven otherwise
          **Limp with refusal to weight-bear:** Fracture, infection, slipped epiphysis
          **Severe swelling, erythema, warmth:** Infection or inflammatory arthritis
          **Night pain or pain waking child from sleep:** Malignancy, osteomyelitis
          **Systemic symptoms:** Fever, weight loss, fatigue, rash — suggests infection, inflammatory, or malignancy
          **Unilateral limb swelling, asymmetrical growth:** Vascular issue, soft tissue mass, malignancy
          **Progressive limp or gait disturbance:** Neurological cause, structural abnormality

        #### Benign features of common paediatric conditions
        
          - Bilateral symmetrical limb pain (growing pains)
          - Pain reproducible by palpation of specific site
          - No swelling or only mild, localised swelling
          - No fever or constitutional symptoms
          - Normal gait and ROM except for activities causing pain
          - Good general health and normal growth

        #### Key history questions
        
          - Onset: acute vs insidious?
          - Location: one or multiple joints/bones?
          - Associated trauma?
          - Systemic symptoms: fever, rash, weight loss?
          - Impact on function: refusing to bear weight, unable to play?
          - Pattern: constant vs intermittent? Worse with activity or rest?

      📈Growing pains▾
      
        #### Overview
        Most common cause of limb pain in children 3-12 years; benign; self-limited. Thought related to rapid bone growth or muscle spasm.

        #### Classic presentation
        
          - Bilateral lower limb pain (thighs, shins, behind knees)
          - Pain in evening or night; may wake child from sleep
          - No limp or minimal limp during day
          - Child well during day; pain resolves by morning
          - No swelling, erythema, or warmth
          - Normal physical exam between episodes

        #### Diagnostic criteria (clinical diagnosis)
        
          - Age 3-12 years
          - Bilateral, symmetrical pain (intermuscular, not intra-articular)
          - Pain typically evening/night
          - No limp or functional impairment during day
          - Normal growth parameters
          - Normal physical exam
          - No systemic symptoms

        #### Investigations (not needed if typical presentation)
        
          - Clinical diagnosis; X-rays or labs not indicated
          - If atypical features (unilateral, constant pain, swelling, fever), investigate for other causes

        #### Management
        
          **Reassurance:** Benign; self-limiting; does not cause permanent damage
          **Analgesia:** Paracetamol or ibuprofen for symptom relief
          **Heat and stretching:** Warm pack, massage, stretching exercises before bed
          **Review:** If symptoms persist beyond 12 years or features atypical, refer

      🏃Osgood-Schlatter disease (OSD)▾
      
        #### Overview
        Traction apophysitis at the tibial tuberosity; common in adolescents during growth spurt; self-limiting.

        #### Epidemiology
        
          - Age 11-18 years; peak 13-14 years in girls, 14-15 years in boys
          - More common in active adolescents (sports, rapid growth)
          - Bilateral in 25% of cases

        #### Clinical presentation
        
          **Pain:** Anterior knee pain, worse with running, jumping, climbing stairs
          **Swelling:** Visible or palpable swelling at tibial tuberosity
          **Tenderness:** Point tenderness over tibial tuberosity
          **Functional impact:** Limp possible; often affects sports participation
          **No warmth or erythema** (unlike infection)

        #### Diagnosis
        
          - Clinical diagnosis; X-ray confirms if needed (fragmentation, irregularity of tibial tuberosity)
          - Ultrasound shows swelling and abnormal tuberosity

        #### Management
        
          **Activity modification:** Reduce aggravating activities (running, jumping); rest from sports often needed
          **Analgesia:** Paracetamol, ibuprofen
          **Ice after activity**
          **Physiotherapy:** Quadriceps and hamstring stretching; strengthening; patellar taping
          **Infrapatellar strap or knee brace:** May reduce symptoms by offloading tuberosity
          **Reassurance:** Self-limiting; resolves as growth plate closes (typically by age 17-18)

        #### When to refer
        
          - Severe functional impairment refractory to conservative care
          - Uncertainty about diagnosis
          - Persistent symptoms into adulthood (rare)

      🦴Slipped capital femoral epiphysis (SCFE)▾
      
        #### Overview
        Displacement of femoral head on growth plate; orthopaedic emergency; must not miss.

        #### Risk factors
        
          - Age 10-16 years; more common in boys; Black children at higher risk
          - Obesity (strong association)
          - Hypothyroidism, hypogonadism, hypopituitarism
          - Radiation exposure

        #### Clinical presentation
        
          **Acute (sudden onset):** Hip or groin pain; may have been minor trauma; unable to bear weight
          **Chronic (insidious):** Groin pain, thigh pain, or referred knee pain; limp; external rotation of hip
          **Physical exam:** Hip held in flexion and external rotation; limited internal rotation (frog-leg position)
          **Symptoms can mimic knee pain:** Child may report knee pain but exam shows hip pathology

        #### Diagnosis
        
          - X-ray (AP and frog-leg lateral) confirms; may show slippage of femoral head
          - If high suspicion but X-ray normal, MRI may be needed

        #### Management
        
          **URGENT orthopaedic referral:** Do NOT delay; do NOT attempt reduction in primary care
          - Nil by mouth pending surgery (may need GA for reduction)
          - Analgesics as needed
          - Surgical fixation with screws to prevent further slippage
          **Screen contralateral hip:** 20-30% develop SCFE on other side

        #### Red flags: When to suspect SCFE
        
          - Obese adolescent with hip or groin pain
          - Referred knee pain in adolescent without knee pathology on exam
          - External rotation deformity of hip
          - Limp in adolescent with BMI >85th percentile

      🦴Juvenile idiopathic arthritis (JIA)▾
      
        #### Overview
        Chronic inflammatory arthritis in children **Oligoarticular (50%):** 
          **Polyarticular (30%):** >5 joints; symmetrical; worse prognosis
          **Systemic-onset (10%):** High fever, rash, hepatosplenomegaly, polyarthritis; may progress to severe disease
          **Enthesitis-related arthritis:** Hip, knee pain; tendon insertion inflammation; male predominance
          **Psoriatic arthritis:** Associated with psoriasis; nail changes, skin lesions

        #### Clinical presentation
        
          **Morning stiffness:** Key feature; often >30 minutes; improves with activity
          **Joint swelling, warmth, pain**
          **Limp or reluctance to weight-bear**
          **Eye involvement (uveitis):** Asymptomatic; screen all JIA patients regularly with slit-lamp exam
          **Systemic features (if systemic-onset):** High spiking fever, rash, hepatosplenomegaly, lymphadenopathy

        #### Diagnosis
        
          - Clinical diagnosis (no single diagnostic test)
          - ESR, CRP often elevated; mild anaemia common
          - ANA positive in 40-60%; RF positive in 5-10%
          - X-rays may show juxta-articular osteoporosis, early joint damage in chronic disease
          - Slit-lamp exam essential (uveitis may be asymptomatic)

        #### Management (in primary care)
        
          **URGENT referral to paediatric rheumatology**
          - Do not delay; early DMARD therapy (methotrexate) and biologic agents improve outcomes
          - NSAIDs for symptom relief pending specialist review
          - Regular eye screening (ophthalmology)

        #### Prognosis
        
          - Highly variable; 50% go into remission
          - Early, aggressive treatment improves outcomes
          - Untreated or late-treated disease can lead to permanent joint damage

      🔴Septic arthritis and osteomyelitis▾
      
        #### Septic arthritis
        
          **Presentation:** Acute joint swelling, pain, warmth; fever common; child refuses to move joint or weight-bear
          **Common organisms:** Staph aureus (including MRSA), Group A Strep, Gram-negatives (neonates)
          **Diagnosis:** Joint aspiration (culture, Gram stain, cell count); X-ray rule out fracture
          **Management:** URGENT; IV antibiotics; may need arthrotomy drainage

        #### Osteomyelitis
        
          **Presentation:** Bone pain, fever, local swelling; often metaphyseal (near knee, hip, ankle)
          **Night pain:** Classic feature
          **Diagnosis:** X-ray (may be normal early); MRI most sensitive; blood and bone cultures
          **Management:** URGENT; IV antibiotics for 4-6 weeks; imaging-guided drainage if abscess

        #### When to suspect infection
        
          - Fever + joint/bone pain until proven otherwise
          - Acute swelling with fever
          - Child unable or refusing to bear weight
          - High inflammatory markers (elevated CRP, ESR, WBC)
