#### 2019 EULAR/ACR classification criteria
      Positive ANA ≥1:80 required as entry criterion, then additive weighted criteria across 7 domains (constitutional, haematological, neuropsychiatric, mucocutaneous, serosal, musculoskeletal, renal). Score ≥10 = classified as SLE.

      #### Clinical features to look for
      
        #### Mucocutaneous
          Malar (butterfly) rash — photosensitive, spares nasolabial folds
          Discoid rash — scarring
          Photosensitivity, oral/nasal ulcers, alopecia
        
        #### Musculoskeletal
          Arthritis/arthralgia — non-erosive, symmetric, small joints
          Avascular necrosis (especially with corticosteroids)
          Myositis (less common)
        
        #### Renal (lupus nephritis)
          Proteinuria (>0.5 g/day), haematuria, casts
          Rising creatinine in active disease
          Class III/IV = proliferative — high risk of ESRD
        
        #### Haematological
          Haemolytic anaemia (Coombs positive), thrombocytopenia, leukopenia, lymphopenia
          Anaemia of chronic disease also common
        
        #### Neuropsychiatric
          Seizures, psychosis, cognitive dysfunction
          Stroke, transverse myelitis, peripheral neuropathy
          Often difficult to attribute to SLE vs comorbidity/drugs
        
        #### Cardiopulmonary
          Pleuritis, pericarditis, pleural/pericardial effusions
          Libman-Sacks endocarditis
          Pulmonary hypertension, interstitial lung disease (rare)

    💊Treatment▾
    
      Hydroxychloroquine — for every patientHCQ 200–400 mg/day (≤5 mg/kg/day) should be prescribed for **all patients with SLE**. It reduces flares by ~50%, prevents damage accrual, reduces mortality, prevents thrombosis and reduces lupus nephritis risk. Ophthalmology review annually after 5 years (retinopathy risk).

      #### Non-renal disease — stepwise
      
        **Mild:** HCQ ± NSAIDs (short-term) ± low-dose prednisolone (≤7.5 mg/day)
        **Moderate:** HCQ + methotrexate (15–25 mg/week, skin/joints) or azathioprine (2 mg/kg/day)
        **Refractory:** mycophenolate mofetil, belimumab (anti-BLyS), anifrolumab (anti-IFNAR1)
      
      #### Lupus nephritis
      
        - Kidney biopsy for classification — guides treatment intensity
        **Class III/IV (proliferative):** induction — mycophenolate mofetil 3 g/day + high-dose steroids (methylprednisolone IV pulses then oral prednisolone), or cyclophosphamide (Euro-Lupus low-dose) + steroids
        - Maintenance: mycophenolate 1.5–2 g/day or azathioprine + HCQ
        - Voclosporin or belimumab add-on to standard therapy (improved renal response rates)
        - ACEi/ARB for proteinuria and BP control in all lupus nephritis
      
      #### Corticosteroid minimisation
      Long-term corticosteroids cause significant damage accrual in SLE (osteoporosis, cataracts, cardiovascular, infection). Target ≤7.5 mg prednisolone/day for maintenance. Use steroid-sparing immunosuppressants early. Bone protection: calcium, vitamin D, bisphosphonate if prolonged use.

    📈Monitoring in primary care▾
    
      #### Regular bloods (every 3–6 months in stable disease)
      
        - FBC — cytopaenias; leukopenia from disease or immunosuppressants
        - UEC, eGFR — renal function; early nephritis detection
        - Urine PCR or ACR + microscopy — proteinuria and haematuria
        - Anti-dsDNA titre + complement (C3, C4) — rising anti-dsDNA + falling complement = active nephritis/systemic flare
        - LFTs — azathioprine/methotrexate hepatotoxicity
      
      #### Cardiovascular risk
      
        - SLE patients have 5–10× increased CV risk — treat aggressively
        - BP target <130/80 mmHg; statin for standard cardiovascular indications (lower threshold)
        - Antiphospholipid antibody syndrome (APS): low-dose aspirin for primary prophylaxis; warfarin for thrombosis (INR 2–3, or 3–4 if arterial)
      
      #### Vaccinations
      
        - Influenza annually; pneumococcus; COVID-19; Hep A+B; HPV; Tdap
        **Avoid live vaccines** on immunosuppressants
        - Vaccinate before starting immunosuppressants where possible

    🤰Pregnancy in SLE▾
    
      #### Counselling before pregnancy
      
        - Ideally plan pregnancy during remission (>6 months of disease quiescence)
        - Active lupus nephritis, APS, pulmonary hypertension — very high-risk; defer pregnancy
        - Anti-Ro/La antibodies: risk of neonatal lupus, complete congenital heart block (~2%) — fetal cardiac monitoring from 16–26 weeks
      
      #### Safe medications in pregnancy
      
        **Continue HCQ** throughout pregnancy — reduces flares and neonatal lupus risk
        - Prednisolone: generally safe at low doses; increases risk of gestational DM, pre-eclampsia
        - Azathioprine: safe; use as steroid-sparing agent
        **Stop:** methotrexate (3 months before conception), mycophenolate (6 weeks before conception, highly teratogenic), cyclophosphamide
        - Aspirin 100 mg/day from early pregnancy (reduces pre-eclampsia risk in SLE)
      
      #### Monitoring in pregnancy
      
        - Regular urine PCR — lupus nephritis can first present or flare in pregnancy
        - Monitor BP closely — SLE + pre-eclampsia can be difficult to distinguish from renal flare
        - Monthly anti-dsDNA + complement in 2nd and 3rd trimester
        - Co-manage with obstetric physician and rheumatologist

          #### Key guidelines & evidence
          
            - EULAR SLE management recommendations (2019, updated).
            - ACR/EULAR Classification Criteria for SLE (2019).
            - Hydroxychloroquine reduces flares and improves long-term outcomes — consider for all patients.
            - Belimumab (anti-BLyS) reduces flares in seropositive patients.
            - Cardiovascular risk in SLE — aggressive lipid and BP management essential.
            - Pregnancy in SLE — requires specialist input; increased miscarriage risk with anti-Ro/La.
