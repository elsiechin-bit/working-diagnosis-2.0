## Overview


**New Zealand has the world's highest age-standardised melanoma incidence & mortality per capita.** Environmental (intense Southern Hemisphere UV, ozone thinning), genetic (fair skin dominance), and behavioural (historical sun-seeking, beach culture) factors converge. Skin type matters but cumulative UV damage is the dominant driver.

        #### UV biology & NZ context
        
          **Southern Hemisphere UV index:** Peaks November–January (summer); highest globally in NZ/Australia (UV index 11–13 possible); ozone depletion over Antarctic region increases ground-level UVB
          **Cumulative UV damage:** UV exposure (both UVA & UVB) causes DNA mutations in melanocytes (BRAF, NRAS, KIT mutations); initiates malignant transformation
          **Intermittent intense exposure:** Sunburns (intermittent high-dose UV) carry higher melanoma risk than chronic sun exposure; childhood/adolescent sunburns particularly damaging (lifetime risk setting)
        
        #### Epidemiology & incidence (NZ 2024–2025)
        
          **Incidence:** ~3,500 melanomas/year in NZ; ~150 deaths/year; age-standardised incidence 28–35 per 100,000 (vs global average 2–3 per 100,000)
          **Age of onset:** Median age 60–65 years; younger cases (age 
          **Anatomical site:** Back, legs (women), face/scalp/ears (men); sun-exposed areas dominant
          **Skin type stratification:**
            
              **European/Caucasian:** Highest melanoma risk (~1 in 75–100 lifetime); fair skin, blue/green eyes, red/blonde hair, poor tanning ability
              **Māori:** Intermediate risk (~1 in 300–500 lifetime); darker skin offers baseline protection but cumulative UV damage still occurs
              **Pasifika:** Lower absolute risk but increasing; similar or higher mortality (later detection, barriers to access)

        #### Risk factors
        
          **Personal history:** Prior melanoma (~5–10% risk of second primary); prior non-melanoma skin cancer
          **Family history:** Familial melanoma (CDKN2A mutations) confers very high lifetime risk (~50–70%); requires surveillance & genetic counselling
          **Atypical naevi:** >50 naevi or dysplastic naevi increase risk; requires total body skin examination annually
          **Sun exposure & sunburns:** Lifetime cumulative; particularly childhood/adolescent sunburns
          **Immune suppression:** HIV, transplant recipients, chronic corticosteroids; increased melanoma & non-melanoma risk

      🔍Clinical recognition: ABCDE & dermoscopy▾
      
        #### ABCDE criteria (melanoma red flags)
        
          **Asymmetry:** One half does not match the other
          **Border irregularity:** Jagged, scalloped, or ill-defined borders
          **Colour variation:** Multiple colours (brown, black, red, white, blue) within one lesion
          **Diameter:** ≥6 mm (though smaller melanomas exist; diameter alone not sufficient)
          **Evolving:** Changes in size, shape, colour, itching, bleeding over weeks–months
        
        #### Additional red flags
        
          **Bleeding or ulceration:** Spontaneous or with minor trauma
          **Itching or pain:** New symptom in existing naevus
          **Amelanotic melanoma:** No pigment; pink, red, or flesh-coloured; easily missed (5–10% of melanomas); high mortality if delayed diagnosis
          **Nodular variant:** Rapidly enlarging papule/nodule; aggressive growth pattern; frequently stage III at diagnosis
        
        #### Dermoscopy (hand-held or digital)
        
          **Useful features suggestive of melanoma:** Atypical network, streaks, dots/globules, blue-white veil, regression, branched capillaries
          **Sensitivity/specificity:** ~95% sensitivity, ~90% specificity in trained hands; improves clinical accuracy when available
          **NZ access:** Increasingly available in primary care & dermatology; digital dermoscopy enables remote assessment
        
        #### Indications for urgent dermatology referral
        
          Refer same-day or next available urgent slot
          **Any lesion with:** ABCDE features, changing naevus, bleeding/ulceration, itching/pain, amelanotic lesion, >6 mm with any atypical feature. Clinical suspicion for melanoma → dermatology urgently.

      📊Melanoma staging (AJCC 8th edition) & prognosis▾
      
        #### Breslow thickness & Clark level (primary determinants of prognosis)
        
          **Breslow thickness:** Measured in mm from granular layer to deepest malignant cell; strongest prognostic factor
            
              **≤1.0 mm:** Stage I; 95% 5-year survival
              **1.01–2.0 mm:** Stage I–II; 90% 5-year survival
              **2.01–4.0 mm:** Stage II; 75–80% 5-year survival
              **>4.0 mm:** Stage III (node-positive) or II (node-negative); 60–70% 5-year survival

          **Clark level:** Depth of invasion (I = epidermis only, V = subcutaneous); prognostic but less important than Breslow
          **Ulceration:** Present in ~10% of melanomas; worsens prognosis (moves stage up)
          **Mitotic rate:** High mitotic rate (≥1 mitosis/mm²) worsens prognosis
        
        #### Stage grouping (TNM 8th edition)
        
          StageDefinition5-yr survival
          
            **0**Melanoma in situ (MIS); no invasion99%
            **IA**≤1.0 mm, no ulceration95%
            **IB**≤1.0 mm with ulceration OR 1.01–2.0 mm no ulceration90%
            **IIA**1.01–2.0 mm with ulceration OR 2.01–4.0 mm no ulceration80%
            **IIB**2.01–4.0 mm with ulceration OR >4.0 mm no ulceration70%
            **IIC**>4.0 mm with ulceration60%
            **IIIA**1–3 lymph nodes involved, microscopic70%
            **IIIB**1–3 nodes macroscopic OR 4+ nodes microscopic OR in-transit metastases50%
            **IIIC**4+ nodes macroscopic40%
            **IV**Distant metastases (brain, lung, liver, other)

      💊Melanoma management & treatment▾
      
        #### Surgical excision (primary treatment)
        
          **Excision margins (NCCN/AJCC 2024):**
            
              MIS or 
              - 1.01–2.0 mm: 10 mm margin
              - 2.01–4.0 mm: 10–20 mm margin
              - >4.0 mm: 20+ mm margin (or wider if feasible)

          **Technique:** Elliptical excision to fascia with full-thickness removal; mark margins with ink for pathology
          **Staged excision:** If wide excision impossible, re-excision after pathology review acceptable (margins clarified by histology)
        
        #### Sentinel lymph node biopsy (SLNB)
        
          **Indications:** Breslow thickness >1.0 mm (stage IB and higher); selected T1b lesions with other adverse features (ulceration, high mitotic rate, Clark level IV–V)
          **Technique:** Radioisotope + blue dye injection near primary; first-draining lymph node identification & biopsy
          **Staging benefit:** Identifies occult node involvement in ~20% of Breslow >1.0 mm; upstages to stage III
          **Therapeutic benefit:** Complete nodal dissection if SLNB positive improves overall survival (MSLT-II trial controversy; still standard at NZ centres)
          **NZ access:** Available at major centres (DHB oncology, plastic surgery); may require travel
        
        #### Adjuvant therapy (stage III–IV)
        
          **Checkpoint inhibitors (PD-1/PD-L1):** Pembrolizumab (Keytruda) or nivolumab for stage IIIB/C resected melanoma; improves recurrence-free & overall survival
          **BRAF inhibitor + MEK inhibitor:** Dabrafenib + trametinib for BRAF V600-mutant metastatic melanoma; ~50% response rate
          **Radiation:** Palliative radiotherapy for brain/bone/soft tissue metastases
          **Systemic therapy (stage IV):** Immunotherapy (checkpoint inhibitor) first-line for most; BRAF inhibitor for BRAF-mutant if immunotherapy contraindicated
          **NZ funding:** Pembrolizumab funded for stage IIIB+ resected; nivolumab for metastatic disease; BRAF inhibitors increasingly accessed

      🏥Non-melanoma skin cancers (BCC, SCC, Merkel)▾
      
        #### Basal cell carcinoma (BCC; ~6,000 cases/year NZ)
        
          **Epidemiology:** Most common skin cancer (~80% of non-melanomas); older age (>60), cumulative sun exposure; rarely metastasises
          **Presentation:** Pearly nodule with central ulceration ("rodent ulcer"), telangiectatic vessels, rolled borders; often on face/scalp/ears
          **Subtypes:** Nodular (most common), superficial, infiltrating; superficial lower recurrence risk
          **Management:** Surgical excision (standard); Mohs micrographic surgery for high-risk (face, recurrent); cryotherapy or imiquimod for small superficial lesions
          **Prognosis:** Excellent (>95% cure with appropriate excision); recurrence ~5–10% (higher if inadequate margins or infiltrating subtype)
        
        #### Squamous cell carcinoma (SCC; ~2,000–3,000 cases/year NZ)
        
          **Epidemiology:** 2nd most common skin cancer; sun-exposed areas; cumulative UV & age >65; higher risk if immunosuppressed
          **Presentation:** Scaly, erythematous papule/plaque, often with ulceration; well-demarcated borders; may bleed if traumatised
          **Risk stratification:** Low-risk (well-differentiated, 2 cm, depth >4 mm, immunocompromised host, location on ear/lip/genitals)
          **Metastatic potential:** Low-risk SCC ~1% metastasis risk; high-risk ~5–30% (especially if >4 mm depth or poor differentiation)
          **Management:** Surgical excision with 4–6 mm margins (low-risk) to 10 mm+ (high-risk); Mohs for high-risk or facial lesions; radiotherapy for unresectable
          **Regional node assessment:** High-risk SCC warrants lymph node examination; consider imaging/SLNB if concerning features

          **Epidemiology:** Aggressive neuroendocrine tumour; age >65, sun-exposed skin, immunocompromised (especially HIV); Merkel cell polyomavirus association
          **Presentation:** Painless firm nodule, often on head/neck/extremities; rapid growth; may have regional lymphadenopathy
          **Prognosis:** Poor (30–40% 5-year survival stage I); ~50% have lymph node involvement at diagnosis
          **Management:** Surgical excision + SLNB/staging; adjuvant radiation & chemotherapy for high-risk

      🌍NZ-specific: equity & Māori/Pasifika disparities▾
      
        #### Epidemiological paradox
        
          **Incidence vs mortality:** Fair-skinned Europeans have 5–8× melanoma incidence vs Māori/Pasifika; yet Māori/Pasifika mortality rates are similar or higher (2–3× higher when adjusted for incidence)
          **Explanation:** Later-stage diagnosis at presentation (advanced Breslow thickness, node involvement), reduced access to specialist dermatology & oncology, potential for worse outcomes with delay
          **Barriers to care:** Cost (specialist visits, travel to centres), health literacy, language, cultural factors, prior experience with healthcare system, rurality
        
        #### NZ Health Equity Priority (Ministry of Health, DHBs)
        
          **Screening access:** Funded skin cancer screening clinics in primary care, especially in high-Māori/Pasifika regions; GP-led dermoscopy training
          **Referral pathways:** Faster access (2-week waits) for suspicious skin lesions in high-risk groups
          **Cultural competence:** Healthcare provider training on Māori health outcomes & cultural safety; engagement with whānau
          **Translation & promotion:** Health promotion in te reo Māori, Pacific languages; community-based education

      ☀️Prevention & sun safety▾
      
        #### Primary prevention: reducing UV exposure
        
          **Avoid intense sun exposure (11 am–4 pm):** Peak UV hours; stay in shade when possible
          **Protective clothing:** Long sleeves, wide-brimmed hats, UPF-rated sunglasses, rash guards in water
          **Sunscreen (SPF 30+):** Reapply every 2 hours, after swimming, after sweating; UVA & UVB protection (broad-spectrum); apply generously (at least 1 teaspoon face, 1 ounce body)
          **Avoid indoor tanning beds:** Increase melanoma risk 40–75% with regular use
        
        #### Secondary prevention: surveillance & early detection
        
          **Self-examination:** Monthly full-body skin check with mirror; note new lesions, changes in existing naevi; photograph concerning lesions for serial comparison
          **Clinician surveillance:**
            
              **Fair-skinned >40 years or significant sun exposure:** Total body skin examination every 2–3 years in primary care
              **Prior melanoma or atypical naevi:** Annual dermatology visit; consider digital dermoscopy or baseline photography
              **Familial melanoma (CDKN2A mutation):** 4–6 monthly dermatology visits

          **NZ resources:** SunSmart (Cancer Society NZ), Health Promotion Agency UV messages, school sun safety programmes

      💡Clinical pearls & pitfalls▾
      
        #### Pearls
        
          ✦**NZ has the world's highest melanoma incidence.** High index of suspicion in any changing naevus; refer for dermoscopy/dermatology urgently if ABCDE features.
          ✦**Breslow thickness is the strongest prognostic factor.** Early diagnosis (≤1.0 mm) = 95% 5-year survival vs 
          ✦**Amelanotic melanoma is easily missed.** Pink/red nodules on exposed skin → refer for dermoscopy. Don't assume non-pigmented lesions are benign.
          ✦**Māori & Pasifika have lower incidence but higher mortality.** Equity gap driven by later diagnosis. Proactive screening & rapid referral in these populations critical.
          ✦**Sunburn as a child = lifetime risk set.** Prevent intermittent intense UV exposure, especially in youth; cumulative dose matters less than peak exposure pattern.
          ✦**BCC is most common but rarely fatal.** Excision >95% curative. SCC carries higher metastatic risk, especially high-risk subtypes; close follow-up needed.
          ✦**SLNB changes staging in ~20% of Breslow >1.0 mm.** Occult node involvement identified; consider referral to centre offering SLNB for intermediate-risk melanoma.
        
        #### Pitfalls
        
          **Delaying referral of changing naevus.** Any ABCDE feature or patient concern → dermatology urgently. Early detection is life-saving.
          **Assuming amelanotic lesion is benign.** Pink or flesh-coloured nodules can be melanoma (5–10% of cases); dermoscopy helpful but dermatology referral wise if uncertain.
          **Inadequate excision margins.** 5 mm margins for thin melanoma is minimum; inadequate margins increase recurrence risk & may require re-excision.
          **Missing health equity in skin cancer care.** Proactive outreach to Māori/Pasifika populations, address barriers, ensure rapid access to specialists.
          **Not counselling on sun protection post-diagnosis.** Patients with prior skin cancer at high risk for subsequent lesions; reinforce sun safety & surveillance.
          **Underestimating SCC risk.** High-risk SCC (>4 mm, poor differentiation, immunocompromised, ear/lip) carries 5–30% metastatic risk; close follow-up & consider SLNB.
