## Overview


Affected individuals require two mutated copies (one from each parent). Parents are carriers (heterozygotes) with no symptoms but 25% risk of affected child if both are carriers.

          Cystic Fibrosis (CF)
          **Incidence:** ~1 in 2500 Caucasians (NZ ~1:2200); rare in other ethnicities
          **Gene:** CFTR on chromosome 7; >1700 mutations identified
          **Diagnosis:** Newborn screening (immunoreactive trypsinogen), sweat chloride >60 mmol/L, genetic testing
          **Management:** Multidisciplinary (respiratory, nutrition, endocrine); CFTR modulators (ivacaftor, lumacaftor) for specific mutations; gene therapy emerging
          **Prognosis:** Median life expectancy >50 years with modern therapy; lung disease and malabsorption main complications

          Spinal Muscular Atrophy (SMA)
          **Incidence:** ~1 in 10,000; ~1 in 40 are carriers
          **Gene:** SMN1 deletion or point mutation on chromosome 5
          **Types:** Type I (severe, onset 
          **Diagnosis:** SMN1 copy number analysis (90% of cases); sequencing for point mutations
          **Management:** Nusinersen (antisense oligonucleotide, funded in NZ) or onasemnogene (gene therapy); supportive care (respiratory, nutritional, physical therapy)
          **Prognosis:** Type I historically fatal 

          Thalassaemia
          **Incidence:** 1 in 100,000 in NZ (higher in Mediterranean, Southeast Asian communities)
          **Gene:** Beta-globin gene deletions or mutations
          **Types:** Beta-thalassaemia major (transfusion-dependent), intermedia (transfusion-independent), trait (asymptomatic)
          **Diagnosis:** Low MCV, high HbA2 (beta-thalassaemia trait); genetic testing for mutations
          **Management:** Regular transfusions, iron chelation (deferasirox, deferoxamine), spleen monitoring; folic acid; hematopoietic stem cell transplant or gene therapy curative
          **Prognosis:** Transplant-free survival with modern chelation and supportive care; fetal hemoglobin inducers (hydroxyurea, luspatercept) emerging

          Phenylketonuria (PKU)
          **Incidence:** ~1 in 10,000 (Caucasians); newborn screening now standard
          **Gene:** PAH (phenylalanine hydroxylase) deficiency
          **Diagnosis:** Elevated phenylalanine on newborn screen (>120 micromol/L); confirmed by plasma amino acids and enzyme testing
          **Management:** Lifelong low-phenylalanine diet; phenylalanine-free formulas; regular monitoring of plasma Phe levels (target 120-360 micromol/L); tetrahydropteridine (BH4) if responsive
          **Prognosis:** With early treatment, normal IQ and life expectancy; untreated leads to intellectual disability and seizures

          Maple Syrup Urine Disease (MSUD)
          **Incidence:** ~1 in 180,000 (higher in consanguineous populations)
          **Gene:** Branched-chain alpha-ketoacid dehydrogenase (BCKDH) complex deficiency
          **Diagnosis:** Elevated branched-chain amino acids (leucine, isoleucine, valine) on newborn screen and urine amino acids
          **Management:** Urgent dietary restriction of branched-chain amino acids; medical foods; tight metabolic control to prevent neurological crisis
          **Prognosis:** Classic presentation without treatment: metabolic crisis in first weeks of life, seizures, coma, death; with early intervention, normal development possible

          Sickle Cell Disease (HbSS)
          **Incidence:** ~1 in 500 Black Africans; rare in NZ overall but increasing with migration
          **Gene:** Beta-globin point mutation (glutamic acid to valine at codon 6)
          **Diagnosis:** Neonatal screening (high HbS on HPLC); confirmed by hemoglobin electrophoresis
          **Management:** Penicillin prophylaxis (infants); vaccinations (pneumococcal, meningococcal); folic acid; pain management in crises; hydroxycarbamide reduces vaso-occlusive events; transfusion for acute chest syndrome, stroke prevention
          **Prognosis:** Median life expectancy ~55 years in developed countries; acute chest syndrome, stroke, organ failure main causes of morbidity/mortality

      🧬Autosomal dominant conditions▾
      
        Affected individuals have one mutated copy; 50% of offspring will inherit the mutation. Often appear as new mutations in unaffected parents.

          Familial Hypercholesterolaemia (FH)
          **Incidence:** ~1 in 250-500 (heterozygous); ~1 in 160,000 (homozygous); NZ prevalence ~1 in 200
          **Gene:** LDLR, APOB, PCSK9 mutations; LDLR most common
          **Diagnosis:** Total cholesterol >7.5 mmol/L (aged 6.5 (aged >30); LDL-C >4.9 mmol/L; corneal arcus, tendon xanthomas; genetic testing confirmatory
          **Management:** High-intensity statin (atorvastatin 40-80 mg) from childhood; ezetimibe; PCSK9 inhibitors if inadequate response; LDL apheresis for homozygotes
          **Prognosis:** Heterozygotes: 20-30% risk of premature CAD by age 50; homozygotes: CAD in childhood/adolescence without treatment

          Marfan Syndrome
          **Incidence:** ~1 in 5,000
          **Gene:** FBN1 (fibrillin-1) on chromosome 15
          **Diagnosis:** Ghent nosology (major criteria: ectopia lentis, aortic root dilatation, systemic features score); genetic testing
          **Major features:** Tall stature, arachnodactyly, chest deformity, lens dislocation, myopia, aortic root dilation (leading cause of death)
          **Management:** Ophthalmology and cardiology surveillance (echo annually); beta-blockers or losartan to slow aortic root progression; prophylactic surgery (composite graft) if aorta >50 mm
          **Prognosis:** Life expectancy ~72 years; sudden cardiac death from aortic dissection main risk; with aggressive management, near-normal lifespan possible

          Hereditary Spherocytosis
          **Incidence:** ~1 in 2,000-3,000 (higher in Northern European, Asian descent)
          **Gene:** Spectrin, ankyrin, band 3, or other red cell membrane protein genes
          **Diagnosis:** Spherocytes on blood film; elevated osmotic fragility; family history
          **Management:** Folic acid supplementation; avoid oxidative stress (infections, certain drugs); splenectomy for symptomatic haemolysis (80-90% effective)
          **Prognosis:** Highly variable; some asymptomatic, others with chronic haemolysis; gallstones common; excellent prognosis post-splenectomy

          Achondroplasia
          **Incidence:** ~1 in 25,000
          **Gene:** FGFR3 (fibroblast growth factor receptor 3); ~80% new mutations
          **Features:** Disproportionate dwarfism, rhizomelic limb shortening, genu varum, kyphosis, foramen magnum stenosis
          **Diagnosis:** Clinical + imaging (short long bones, lumbar lordosis); genetic testing confirmatory
          **Management:** Neurosurgery for spinal stenosis/cord compression; orthopedic management (limb alignment); developmental support; growth hormone not routinely indicated
          **Prognosis:** Normal life expectancy with appropriate surveillance and management; heterozygotes mostly unaffected; homozygotes usually lethal

      🧬X-linked conditions▾
      
        Males (XY) with one mutated X are affected; females (XX) with one mutated X are carriers. Affected males cannot pass to sons (sons get Y from father); all daughters of affected males are carriers.

          Haemophilia A
          **Incidence:** ~1 in 5,000 males
          **Gene:** F8 (Factor VIII) on X chromosome; >3000 mutations known
          **Severity:** Severe (
          **Diagnosis:** Prolonged aPTT, low Factor VIII activity, family history
          **Management:** Factor VIII replacement (recombinant or plasma-derived); DDAVP for mild disease; antifibrinolytics; inhibitor management in patients who develop alloantibodies
          **Prognosis:** Life expectancy near-normal with modern replacement therapy; chronic joint disease from repeated bleeds main morbidity; gene therapy emerging

          Haemophilia B (Christmas Disease)
          **Incidence:** ~1 in 30,000 males
          **Gene:** F9 (Factor IX) on X chromosome
          **Diagnosis:** Low Factor IX activity, prolonged aPTT
          **Management:** Factor IX replacement (recombinant or plasma-derived, prothrombin complex concentrate); activated prothrombin complex for inhibitors
          **Prognosis:** Similar to Haemophilia A; gene therapy shows promise

          Duchenne Muscular Dystrophy (DMD)
          **Incidence:** ~1 in 3,500 male births
          **Gene:** DMD (dystrophin) on X chromosome; largest gene in human genome; deletions 60%, duplications 6%, point mutations 34%
          **Features:** Proximal weakness (legs >arms), pseudohypertrophy of calves, Gowers sign, cardiomyopathy, respiratory failure, cognitive decline possible
          **Diagnosis:** Elevated creatine kinase (CK >10x normal), genetic testing; muscle biopsy if genetic testing inconclusive
          **Management:** Corticosteroids (prednisolone 0.75 mg/kg/day) slow progression; cardiac monitoring (ACEi, beta-blocker); respiratory support; physical therapy; exon-skipping therapies (eteplirsen) for specific mutations
          **Prognosis:** Wheelchair dependence by early teens; life expectancy mid-to-late 20s with modern supportive care; cardiac and respiratory failure main causes of death

          Fragile X Syndrome
          **Incidence:** ~1 in 4,000 males; 1 in 6,000-8,000 females
          **Gene:** FMR1 (fragile X mental retardation) on X chromosome; >200 CGG repeats (full mutation)
          **Features:** Intellectual disability (moderate-severe in males), autism spectrum features, attention deficit, macro-orchidism, facial features (long face, prominent ears)
          **Diagnosis:** CGG repeat analysis; FMR1 protein (FMRP) absent or reduced
          **Management:** Early developmental intervention; autism/ADHD management (stimulants, SSRIs); behaviour support; seizure management if needed
          **Prognosis:** Females often milder due to X-inactivation; seizures in 10-15%; life expectancy normal to near-normal

          Colour Blindness (Red-Green)
          **Incidence:** ~8% of males (Caucasian); 0.4-0.5% of females
          **Gene:** OPN1LW and OPN1MW (opsin genes) on X chromosome
          **Types:** Protanopia (red-blind), deuteranopia (green-blind), protanomaly, deuteranomaly
          **Diagnosis:** Ishihara colour plates, anomaloscope testing
          **Management:** Colour-blindness glasses (limited efficacy); workplace adjustments; career counselling (certain professions require colour vision)

      🧬Mitochondrial and complex inheritance▾
      
        Mitochondrial conditions inherited maternally (all children of affected mothers); variable penetrance and heteroplasmy complicate inheritance patterns.

          Mitochondrial Myopathy, Encephalomyopathy, Lactic Acidosis, and Stroke (MELAS)
          **Incidence:** ~1 in 30,000
          **Gene:** mtDNA mutations; m.3243A>G most common
          **Features:** Seizures, stroke-like episodes (not vascular occlusion), migraine, myopathy, diabetes, hearing loss, short stature
          **Diagnosis:** Stroke-like lesion not respecting vascular territory on MRI; elevated lactate on MR spectroscopy; mtDNA analysis
          **Management:** Seizure control; L-arginine infusion during acute stroke-like episodes (vasodilation); migraine prevention; metabolic support (L-citrulline, CoQ10)
          **Prognosis:** Variable; seizures often refractory; stroke-like events cause cumulative disability; median life expectancy ~50 years

          Leber Hereditary Optic Neuropathy (LHON)
          **Incidence:** ~1 in 30,000
          **Gene:** mtDNA complex I subunit genes (ND1, ND4, ND6); three primary mutations account for ~90%
          **Features:** Acute vision loss (optic atrophy), painless, young adults; peripheral neuropathy, cardiac arrhythmias possible
          **Diagnosis:** Optic disc swelling, central scotoma on visual field; mtDNA sequencing
          **Management:** Avoid tobacco and alcohol (triggers); idebenone (ubiquinone analogue) may slow vision loss; gene therapy (intramitochondrial) in trials
          **Prognosis:** 50% recover some vision over months; variable prognosis; men more severely affected than heterozygous women

          MODY (Maturity Onset Diabetes of the Young)
          **Incidence:** 1-5% of "Type 2 diabetes" in young people; 6 main types
          **Gene:** HNF1A (MODY 3), GCK (MODY 2), HNF4A (MODY 1), others
          **Features:** Autosomal dominant inheritance; diabetes before age 25; strong family history; often misclassified as Type 1 or Type 2
          **Diagnosis:** Clinical presentation + genetic testing; fasting glucose, oral glucose tolerance test
          **Management:** MODY 2 (GCK): usually no treatment; MODY 3 (HNF1A): sulfonylureas very effective; HNF4A similar
          **Prognosis:** Variable; generally good with appropriate therapy; genetic counselling for family planning important

      🧬Cancer predisposition syndromes▾
      
        Germline mutations conferring high lifetime cancer risk; genetic testing indicated for individuals with early-onset cancer, multiple affected relatives, or specific histologies.

          BRCA1/BRCA2 Breast and Ovarian Cancer
          **Incidence:** ~1 in 400 (Ashkenazi Jewish); 1 in 1,000-1,500 (general population)
          **Gene:** BRCA1 or BRCA2; >1800 mutations identified
          **Lifetime cancer risk:** BRCA1: breast 70%, ovarian 40%; BRCA2: breast 60%, ovarian 20%
          **Diagnosis:** Genetic testing recommended for: early-onset breast cancer (
          **Management:** Breast surveillance (MRI, mammography from age 25-30); risk-reducing salpingo-oophorectomy post-childbearing; tamoxifen or aromatase inhibitors for chemoprevention; enhanced ovarian cancer screening (limited evidence)
          **Prognosis:** Genetic counselling essential for at-risk relatives; penetrance variable (genetic background, reproductive factors)

          Lynch Syndrome (Hereditary Non-Polyposis Colorectal Cancer)
          **Incidence:** ~1 in 300-500
          **Gene:** Mismatch repair genes (MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, PMS2); ~90% of Lynch cases
          **Cancer risk:** Colon 70-80% by age 70; endometrial 20-60% (women); ovarian, gastric, urinary tract cancers increased
          **Diagnosis:** Family history (early-onset CRC, multiple generations); microsatellite instability (MSI) or mismatch repair protein loss on tumour testing; genetic testing
          **Management:** Colonoscopy every 2 years starting age 20-25; aspirin for chemoprevention (controversial); hysterectomy and oophorectomy for women (controversial)
          **Prognosis:** Colorectal cancers in Lynch syndrome often right-sided, earlier stage; with surveillance, improved outcomes

          Familial Adenomatous Polyposis (FAP)
          **Incidence:** ~1 in 10,000
          **Gene:** APC (adenomatous polyposis coli) on chromosome 5
          **Features:** Hundreds to thousands of colorectal polyps by age 40; near 100% CRC risk by age 50 if untreated
          **Diagnosis:** Clinical (>100 colorectal polyps); genetic testing of APC
          **Management:** Prophylactic proctocolectomy (ileal pouch) or colectomy recommended by age 20-30; surveillance of FAP-associated cancers (gastric, duodenal, pancreatic, thyroid); NSAIDs (sulindac, celecoxib) may reduce polyp burden
          **Prognosis:** Excellent with prophylactic surgery; without surgery, near-100% CRC risk

      🧬NZ-specific and population-enriched conditions▾
      
        Some genetic conditions are enriched in NZ populations due to founder effects or ethnicity-specific prevalence.

          Māori and Pacific Islander Genetic Conditions
          **Higher prevalence:** Polycystic kidney disease, familial hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (founder mutations), type 2 diabetes (genetic and environmental)
          **Notes:** Ancestry-based carrier screening panels less established; whole genome sequencing increasingly available for at-risk individuals

          Alpha-Thalassaemia Trait (HbH disease)
          **Incidence:** Elevated in Pacific Islander and Southeast Asian populations in NZ
          **Gene:** Deletions or mutations in alpha-globin genes
          **Features:** Microcytic anaemia, normal or slightly elevated HbA2, HbH (beta-4 tetramers) on electrophoresis
          **Management:** Monitoring for chronic haemolysis; avoid oxidative stress; counsel for carrier testing in future partners

          Amyloidosis (AL and AA)
          **Incidence:** Rare but increasing recognition in NZ; hereditary transthyretin (TTR) amyloidosis present
          **Gene:** TTR mutations (hereditary), serum amyloid A (AA), immunoglobulin light chains (AL)
          **Features:** Cardiomyopathy, neuropathy, renal disease, autonomic dysfunction
          **Diagnosis:** Tissue biopsy (Congo red), electron microscopy, genetic testing
          **Management:** TTR stabilizers (tafamidis), immunosuppression (for AL), cardiac/renal support; liver transplant in hereditary TTR

      🔬Diagnostic approach and NZ resources▾
      
        #### When to suspect a genetic condition
        
          - Family history of the same or related conditions (multiple generations, multiple siblings)
          Early age of onset relative to population (e.g. cardiac event 
          - Unusual phenotype or severity for a common condition
          - Consanguinity (increased risk of autosomal recessive disease)
          - Ethnic ancestry (e.g. Ashkenazi Jewish, Mediterranean, Southeast Asian with carrier risk enrichment)

        #### Diagnostic tests available in NZ
        
          **Newborn screening:** 28 conditions screened from heel prick blood (funded by MOH)
          **Carrier screening:** Ethnicity-based panels (Ashkenazi Jewish, European); expanded panels available privately
          **Prenatal testing:** Non-invasive prenatal testing (NIPT) for trisomies 21, 18, 13; chorionic villus sampling (CVS) or amniocentesis for confirmatory testing
          **Genetic testing:** Single gene testing (e.g. BRCA1/2, CFTR), gene panels (cancer predisposition, cardiomyopathy), whole exome/genome sequencing (increasingly available)

        #### Key NZ resources
        
          **Genetic Health Service NZ (now part of Te Whatu Ora):** Regional genetics services; referral via GP
          **NZ Newborn Screening Programme:** Expanded screening from age 2-3 days
          **Canterbury Health Laboratories, Medlab, LabPlus:** Genetic testing services
          **Rare Genetic Disorders NZ:** Patient support and resources
          **NZ Genetic Society:** Clinician resources and guidelines
          **ClinVar, OMIM:** International genetic variant databases

        #### Genetic counselling
        Genetic counsellors help interpret results, discuss inheritance patterns, and support decision-making. Referral via Genetic Health Service NZ or private genetic counsellors. Important for: cancer predisposition syndromes, recurrent miscarriage, carrier testing before pregnancy, prenatal counselling.
