## Overview


The companion to this piece deals with the practical work of managing early dementia in the community - the history, the driving conversation, the enduring power of attorney, the slow scaffolding of a life lived well with the condition. This one steps back. Dementia is not only a clinical problem; it is a demographic phenomenon, a contested medical idea with a surprisingly short history as a "disease", and a deeply cultural experience whose meaning shifts across the communities a New Zealand practice serves. Understanding that wider frame changes how the clinical work is done.

        ## The numbers

        Globally, dementia is among the largest and fastest-growing contributors to disability and dependence in older people. Around 55 million people were estimated to be living with dementia worldwide at the start of this decade, a figure projected to reach roughly 139 million by 2050 as populations age - the growth concentrated in low- and middle-income countries.1,2 It is now consistently among the leading causes of death in high-income countries.

        In Aotearoa the trajectory mirrors this. Estimates put the number of New Zealanders living with dementia at roughly 70,000 around 2020, rising to approximately 170,000 by 2050 - well more than a doubling in three decades - with annual economic costs measured in the billions and projected to climb steeply.3

        Age is the dominant risk factor. Prevalence is low below 65 and roughly doubles with every five years of age thereafter, so the apparent epidemic is in large part a story about how many people now reach their eighties and nineties.

        There is a genuinely hopeful and under-reported finding hidden inside the alarming totals: in several high-income countries the *age-specific* incidence of dementia has been falling over recent decades, probably reflecting better vascular health, more education, and less smoking across successive cohorts.4 Individual risk at a given age is dropping even as the absolute number of cases rises with the size of the older population. Both things are true at once, and conflating them is a common source of confusion.

        ## The equity dimension

        The numbers are not evenly distributed. In Aotearoa, Māori and Pacific peoples are affected at younger ages and, on modelling, at higher rates than the European/Other population, against a backdrop of higher vascular risk, socioeconomic deprivation, and later presentation.3,5 The same pattern - dementia falling earliest and hardest on the communities with the least - recurs internationally. It is one of the clearest examples of how a condition framed as a problem of individual brains is in fact patterned by the social determinants of health across a lifetime.

        ## A short history of an idea

        Dementia as a *disease* is a surprisingly recent construction. For most of recorded history, the loss of memory and reason in old age was regarded as senescence - the expected, if unfortunate, condition of "dotage" or "senility", a normal part of growing very old rather than a pathology to be diagnosed and treated.

          How the idea changed - selected milestones
          
            1906-07Alois Alzheimer describes Auguste Deter's case - profound dementia in a woman in her fifties, with plaques and neurofibrillary tangles at autopsy. Kraepelin later attaches Alzheimer's name to it, initially as a rare *presenile* disease.
            ~1910-60"Senile dementia" in older people is held to be distinct from Alzheimer's rare presenile disease - and largely accepted as ordinary ageing of the brain.
            1976Robert Katzman argues that senile and presenile dementia share the same pathology - reclassifying common "senility" as Alzheimer's disease, "a major killer". A statistical and political turning point: a normal-ageing phenomenon becomes a named, fundable disease.
            1991-92The amyloid cascade hypothesis is formalised, placing beta-amyloid at the causal centre and shaping three decades of research and drug development.
            1993-96First cholinesterase inhibitors arrive (tacrine, then donepezil) - symptomatic, modest, not disease-modifying.
            2021-23After decades of failed anti-amyloid trials, monoclonal antibodies (aducanumab, then lecanemab and donanemab) show measurable amyloid clearance and modest slowing of decline - alongside real harms, high costs, and fierce debate about whether the benefit is clinically meaningful.

        The 1976 reframing deserves emphasis because it is so easy to miss its significance. By collapsing "senility" into Alzheimer's disease, the medical world converted an accepted feature of ageing into a discrete pathology - and in doing so created the modern dementia field: its research funding, its advocacy organisations, its diagnostic categories, and its expectation that there should be a cure. That expectation has driven extraordinary science and extraordinary disappointment in roughly equal measure.

        ## The amyloid argument, and why nuance matters

        For thirty years the dominant theory held that the accumulation of beta-amyloid was the central cause of Alzheimer's disease, with the corollary that removing it should treat the disease. The track record was punishing: trial after trial cleared amyloid without clearly helping patients, prompting persistent argument that the hypothesis was, at minimum, incomplete. Confidence was further shaken by scrutiny of influential amyloid research and by the contested 2021 approval of aducanumab on a surrogate endpoint.8

        The newer antibodies, lecanemab and donanemab, did finally show statistically significant slowing of decline in early Alzheimer's disease - a genuine milestone.9,10 But the effect sizes are modest and of uncertain clinical significance to patients and families; the drugs require amyloid confirmation, repeated infusions, and MRI monitoring for amyloid-related imaging abnormalities (ARIA, including brain swelling and microhaemorrhage); and the cost and infrastructure implications are formidable. For a small, geographically dispersed health system like New Zealand's, the question of who would be eligible, who would pay, and whether the benefit justifies it is genuinely unresolved. The honest clinical position is that this is a real but incremental advance, not the cure the headlines imply.

          The most actionable science of the last decade may not be the drugs at all. The Lancet Commission estimates that something like 40% of dementia cases are associated with modifiable risk factors across the life course - among them less education, hearing loss, hypertension, smoking, obesity, depression, physical inactivity, diabetes, excess alcohol, social isolation, air pollution, and untreated vision loss.11 Prevention, much of it ordinary primary-care work, remains the largest lever available.

        ## The cultural frame

        What dementia *means* - and therefore how it is recognised, named, disclosed, and cared for - varies profoundly across cultures, and a New Zealand practice sits at the meeting point of several.

        ### Mate wareware: a te ao Māori understanding
        Māori understandings of cognitive change in older age are not simply a translation of the biomedical concept. The term *mate wareware* has been developed and explored through kaupapa Māori research as a way of naming the experience on its own terms, situating it within whānau, whakapapa, and the status of kaumātua rather than within an individual brain in deficit.5 Elders hold mana and a valued social role; a framing that reduces them to a diagnosis can be both inaccurate and harmful. Care is understood as a collective whānau responsibility, and culturally grounded assessment tools and services matter precisely because the Western, individualised, test-score model maps poorly onto this world.

        ### Stigma, "normal ageing", and disclosure
        In many communities - and this cuts across ethnicity - cognitive decline is read as inevitable ageing rather than illness, which delays presentation until crisis. In others, dementia carries significant stigma, sometimes attached to the whole family, which suppresses help-seeking. Norms around *disclosure* also differ: the Western default of telling the patient their diagnosis directly is not universal, and some families, from a protective rather than controlling motive, prefer that a diagnosis not be named baldly to the person. Navigating this without either steamrolling the patient's right to know or imposing a culturally alien script is delicate work.

        ### Filial care and the place of institutions
        Across many Asian and Pacific cultures, care of older parents is a profound filial duty, and placing a relative in residential care can be experienced as a failure or a shame rather than a service. This shapes everything: the late presentation, the resistance to respite, the carer who is exhausting themselves silently because asking for help feels like a betrayal. Recognising the framework, rather than assuming a Western individual-autonomy model, is what allows support to be offered in a form the family can actually accept.

        ## A nuanced position

        Holding all of this together produces a more honest clinical stance than either the catastrophic or the dismissive one. Dementia is common and becoming more so in absolute terms, while individual age-specific risk is, encouragingly, drifting downward. It is a relatively young disease-concept whose boundaries with normal ageing remain genuinely contested - which should make clinicians humble about overdiagnosis at the mild end and about medicalising the ordinary forgetfulness of age. Its dominant causal theory has been productive and disappointing, and its first plausibly disease-modifying drugs are real but marginal and, for now, largely out of reach here. And its meaning is irreducibly cultural: the same MRI and the same test score sit inside utterly different lived worlds.

        For the GP, the practical implication is consistent with the management piece this essay accompanies. Treat the vascular and lifestyle risk factors, because prevention is the strongest evidence we have. Diagnose carefully and humbly, mindful of the boundary with normal ageing and of the tools' cultural limits. Disclose in a way that honours both the right to know and the family's frame. And remember that the person in front of you - kaumātua, parent, elder - is not a failing organ but someone embedded in a history, a culture, and a web of relationships that will do far more to shape their years with dementia than anything written on a prescription.
