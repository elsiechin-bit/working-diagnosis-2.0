## The problem

Your liver makes cholesterol. A lot of it. Cells need cholesterol for their membranes and for making hormones, but excess cholesterol in the bloodstream clogs your arteries—the classic process of atherosclerosis.

## The key enzyme

The rate-limiting step in cholesterol manufacture is catalysed by an enzyme called HMG-CoA reductase. This enzyme sits on the endoplasmic reticulum inside your hepatocytes and controls how much cholesterol gets made.

**Statins are competitive inhibitors of this enzyme.** They look so similar to the enzyme's normal substrate (HMG-CoA) that they slot into the active site instead. Once they're bound, the enzyme can't work, and cholesterol synthesis grinds down.

## Why this matters

1. **Less cholesterol made** → lower circulating LDL cholesterol
2. **Cells sense the shortage** → they upregulate LDL receptors on their surface
3. **More LDL removed from blood** → faster clearance from the circulation

This dual effect—slowed synthesis plus increased clearance—is why statins are so effective.

## The potency spectrum

- **Low potency**: pravastatin, lovastatin (older drugs, less lipophilic)
- **High potency**: atorvastatin, rosuvastatin (more lipophilic, cross the blood-brain barrier)

The newer, high-potency statins are more potent because they dissolve in fat; they cross into cells more easily and spend longer in the liver.

## The catches

- **Muscle**: rarely, statins cause myopathy (statin-induced muscle injury). Rhabdomyolysis is vanishingly rare, but myalgia is not uncommon.
- **Liver**: liver enzymes can rise, but hepatotoxicity is genuinely rare.
- **Drug interactions**: high-potency statins are metabolised by CYP3A4; coadministration with CYP3A4 inhibitors (macrolides, azole antifungals, protease inhibitors) can cause toxic statin levels.

## Clinical pearl

💡 Statins are most effective when taken in the evening, because cholesterol synthesis peaks overnight. But modern ER formulations work just as well once daily whenever you take them.
